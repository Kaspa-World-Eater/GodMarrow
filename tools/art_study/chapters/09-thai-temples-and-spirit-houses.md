## 1. What a temple is made of

A wat is a group of buildings, not one:
- **the ubosot (bot):** the ordination hall, the most sacred;
- **the wihan:** the assembly and image hall;
- **the chedi:** a bell-shaped relic tower;
- **the prang:** a corn-cob tower, from the Khmer;
- **the sala:** an open pavilion;
- **the ho trai:** a library;
- **the kuti:** the monks' small stilted huts.

A village shrine in deep forest would be small: one hall, perhaps a small brick chedi, a sala, a few kuti.

**The hall.** A rectangle on a raised platform of brick and stucco, with moulded tiers at its foot. Its walls are whitewashed brick, thick, pierced by tall narrow windows with carved shutters. A porch of columns stands at the front (lotus capitals), and a naga stair leads up to it.

**The roof is the temple.** It comes in tiers stacked from front to back:
- the lowest and largest tier is over the porch, then smaller tiers over the hall;
- each tier is often broken into two or three overlapping layers, so the roofline steps and the mass lightens;
- the eaves sweep far out over the walls, resting on carved brackets;
- the pitch is steep, the tiles glazed (green, orange, rust) and laid in overlapping rows.

This deep overhang is the shape a height field cannot make.

The gable trim (the parts that make a roof Thai):
- **chofa:** the tall, slender finial at the top of each gable. It curves up like a bird's beak or horn (garuda or naga), and often hangs a small bell that chimes in the wind.
- **lamyong / nak sadung:** the bargeboard running down each gable edge, carved as a serpent's undulating body.
- **bai raka:** a row of blade-like serrations along it, the naga's fins or the garuda's feathers, like saw teeth or flames.
- **hang hong:** at the bottom end of each bargeboard, an upturned naga head.
- **na ban:** the gable's triangular face, carved and gilded, often set with glass mosaic.

Other parts:
- **naga balustrades:** serpent bodies run down both sides of the stair, their hooded heads (often multi-headed) rearing at the foot.
- **bai sema:** eight leaf-shaped boundary stones round the ordination hall mark the sacred ground, each on its own small plinth.
- **guardians:** stone or stucco guardian giants stand at gates and doors.

**Materials and colour:**
- brick (deep red-orange when bared), lime stucco over it (white, greying), laterite (pitted rust-brown);
- teak for the roof frame, doors and shutters;
- black lacquer with gold leaf (the doors and shutters, lai rot nam: gold patterns on black);
- glass mosaic in mirror, green and gold;
- glazed roof tiles.

The palette: white walls, a red and green tiled roof, gold on black, gilt trim.

## 2. How it falls apart (the Ayutthaya lessons, and a wooden roof in a rainforest)

- **Stucco fails first.** It cracks, bellies and drops in sheets, baring the red brick beneath in irregular continents. The edges of what stays are rounded and grey-black with lichen. Carved stucco loses its fine relief to soft lumps.
- **Brick then weathers.** Joints erode deeper than the bricks; corners round off; courses slump where the core settled. Green moss and small ferns root in every joint and on every ledge. Bell-shaped chedis lose their spires and slump into rounded mounds.
- **The roof goes first of all in a wet forest.** Teak rafters rot where water sits:
  - tiles slide in sheets and pile at the wall foot;
  - the frame shows as bare ribs (rafters, purlins) against the sky;
  - one tier falls in while another hangs;
  - a chofa snaps and hangs by its bargeboard.
  
  This is also the overhang the 3D trial must show: ribs over empty air.
- **Trees take it back.** Fig and banyan roots climb walls, split them and wrap whole figures. The famous Ayutthaya head held in banyan roots is the image: a figure taken by the tree.
- **Lacquer and gold:**
  - lacquer crazes, then flakes off in islands;
  - gold leaf survives in protected recesses and is lost on exposed faces;
  - glass mosaic pops out piece by piece, leaving a grey bed with a few glints.
- **Wet:** everything is darker at its foot and under its drips. Black streaks run down from every ledge and gargoyle. Green algae lies on the north-facing and shaded sides.

## 3. Spirit houses (san phra phum)

- A small house for the guardian spirit of the land, set on **one pillar** at about chest height. A four-pillared version (san chao thii) is for ancestral spirits.
- It is a miniature temple: a raised platform, a tiered roof with tiny chofa, and often a small porch.
- **Offerings:**
  - flower garlands (marigold, jasmine);
  - incense sticks in a pot;
  - candles;
  - water or drinks, fruit;
  - small figurines (dancers, elephants, servants).
- Every household has one, and when a building goes up the spirit of that ground is given a house so it does not trouble the people. Old spirit houses are often set out, not destroyed, round a sacred tree.

**For the game:**
- A village that fell would leave its spirit houses still standing in the forest round it, tilted on their pillars, roofs broken, garlands rotted to brown string, figurines fallen and filmed with algae.
- One or two would still have fresh-looking offerings that no one living could have left.

## 4. Recipes (for both the height method and the 3D trial)

1. **Massing first:** platform, then walls, then the roof tiers. At true size a village hall is about 13 × 8 yd. The platform is 1 yd high, the walls 4 yd, and the ridge 9 to 10 yd. The porch is lower and the eaves overhang 1 to 1.5 yd. The hero is 2 yd beside it.
2. **The roof is built, not painted:**
   - rafters, purlins, battens, then tile rows (each tile a curved shell, overlapping);
   - the serpent bargeboard with its bai raka teeth and hang hong head;
   - the chofa at each gable peak.
3. **Ruin by cause:**
   - the wet side (where water runs and sits) rots first: its tiles slide and its rafters sag;
   - the dry side holds;
   - stucco drops where the wall bellies;
   - roots enter at the base where the wet stays.
4. **Material by surface angle:**
   - moss on the tops of things (up-facing), algae on the shaded sides;
   - streaks down from every lip;
   - lacquer and gold kept in the recesses.
5. **The one living light:** a temple's lamps are out. The light at the shrine is the overcast sky. If anything glows, it is an offering candle no one could have lit.
6. **The god's hint** (Derek: only a hint in the first area): a pale root, warm to the touch and slowly beating (the Hollow Wood's lore: the god's veins stood up as trees), has grown round the guardian's head, as the banyan holds the Ayutthaya head.
