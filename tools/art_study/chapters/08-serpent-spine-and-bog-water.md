# Chapter 8: the serpent's spine, and black bog water

For the spine path through the Sunken Bog (Derek, 2026-10-08): "an ancient spine path from a giant snake ... wind[ing]
through a swampy bog ... blackish mirror-like water that reflects ... lots of plant life ... the vertebrae sometimes
poking through". The lore ruling: a long-dead demon serpent god, its names half remembered and all wrong.

## 1. The serpent's vertebra (the real thing)

Sources: descriptions of fossil snake vertebrae (Palaeo-Electronica's systematic palaeontology of fossil snakes; Plazi's
*Pterosphenus* treatment) and Titanoboa (Head et al. 2009; Wikipedia's summary).
- **Many, alike, overlapping.** A snake has 200 to 400 vertebrae, each nearly the same as the next and changing only
  slowly along the body. Each carries a pair of ribs. Seen from above, the column reads as a chain of overlapping
  winged plates, not as separate bones.
- **The body (centrum)** is procoelous: a cup (cotyle) in front, a round ball (condyle) behind, and each ball sits in the
  next one's cup. The joint is a dark ring between rounded bodies.
- **The neural arch** roofs the body. On it stands the **neural spine**, a thin blade along the midline. The **zygosphene**
  (a wedge in front that fits the zygantrum of the vertebra ahead, a joint only serpents and a few lizards have) is
  thick and arched, and forms the foot of the spine's front edge.
- **Two pairs of zygapophyses** stand out sideways above the body like wings: the front pair faces up and in, the back
  pair down and out, so each vertebra's back wings lie over the next one's front wings. **This overlap is the shape of a
  serpent's back:** a run of chevrons or arrowheads, each lapping the next.
- **Below,** a blade-like **hypapophysis** ends in a point behind (sunk in the bog: unseen).
- **The ribs** articulate low on each side and curve out and down.
- **Giants:** in the great constrictors, size comes from bigger vertebrae, not more of them. Titanoboa's are "very robust
  and wide, with a pentagonal shape in anterior view": broad and flat-roofed, a near-pentagon seen end on. A god's
  vertebra on that plan, 1.6 to 2.2 yd long and 3 to 4 yd across its wings, is a road.

**What this means for the art:**
- The walked surface is the roof of the arches and the wings, near flat, lapping forward like roof slates.
- The neural spines, once a fin down the middle, are broken to worn stumps, and between them the zygosphene humps.
- The joints between bodies are dark crescents, filled with mud and moss.
- On a bend, the joints open on the outside of the curve and close on the inside, and the wings lap more on the inside.
- The ribs leave the sides at intervals and arch out into the water. Most have broken or sunk; some stand as arches.

## 2. Bone in a bog

- Bog water is acid and anoxic. It stains bone tea-brown to black and keeps it from rotting. Bog bone is dark and
  glossy, not white.
- Where it stands proud of the water and feet wear it, it is paler and dry on its crowns: grey-ivory under the dark.
- Below the old water line it is green-black with algae and slime, with a sharp tide line where the water stood
  longest.
- Mud packs every joint and hollow first (the burial rule: lows fill first). Moss, sedge and reed root in that mud, so
  the bone is covered wherever it dips and bare only on its high crowns. Hence "the vertebrae sometimes poking
  through": the overgrowth is the rule, the bone the exception.

## 3. Black bog water (the Famine's lesson, `painted_swamp_god.py`)

- Peat tannins make bog water tea-black, so it reflects like a dark mirror and you see almost nothing below the first
  hand's depth. What stands over it shows in it, darker and a little cooler than itself.
- The surface lies level at the bog's water table. It is broken only by slow rings, wind ruffles in the open, scum lines
  along the shore and floating mats.
- **On it:** duckweed in drifts that break the reflection (they collect at the lee shore and against things); lily pads
  with a notch, lying flat, their rims catching light; a pale flower now and then. In the shallows, sedge and reed
  stand in clumps, each mirrored.
- **Method (from the Famine study):** for each water pixel, march the mirrored ray up through the height field and read
  the painted image where it lands; ripple it slightly; darken and cool it. The duckweed and pads are drawn on top and
  break it.

## 4. Wisp-fire over water (St Elmo's fire, ignis fatuus)

- Marsh lights are seen as small pale flames low over wet ground, drifting, going out and coming back, never close. They
  are cold: blue-white to pale green.
- In the world they light only a little round themselves (the water under them, a reed, the bone) and show again in the
  mirror. A few at a time, never a swarm.

## 5. What stands in a bog, a fen and a swamp (Derek 2026-10-08: "study swamps and bogs and fens for structures that appear in them")

Sources: wetland microtopography studies (hummock and hollow; the tropical peat swamp surveys; Copernicus HESS 2019,
Biogeosciences 2020), the Irish Peatland Conservation Council's bog plant notes, Saul Lake bog notes, and the
woodland and carr chapters already in this library.
- **Hummock and hollow** is the bog's whole grammar. The hollows are flat, wet and often flooded; the hummocks rise a
  hand to a yard out of them. Plants take the slightly higher ground first, build peat there and raise it further,
  so the hummocks are where life is thickest. The hollows are open water or bare black peat.
- **Tussock sedge** (*Carex paniculata*) builds columns: a stool of dead leaf bases and roots up to a yard high and
  half a yard across, its living blades fountaining off the top. In flooded carr they stand in the water like posts.
- **Alder stools and root islands:** carr trees grow on raised pedestals of their own roots. When the tree dies and
  rots, the stump stands on a mound of root, peat and dirt above the water, moss and ferns on it, its heart rotted
  into a hollow.
- **Windthrow in wet ground:** shallow-rooted trees on saturated peat go over easily and lie in the water. The root
  plate is torn up on its edge, with dirt held in it; its pit fills with water. The trunk lies along the water,
  half sunk and moss-backed, the snags of its limbs up. Vines and creepers hang off it into the water.
- **Floating mats** (schwingmoor): sphagnum and sedge knit a raft over open water that quakes underfoot. Its edge
  is ragged and overhangs the black water.
- **Peat hags:** where water cuts the peat, banks stand as dark cut faces with an overhanging lip of moss, the cut
  peat black and layered.

**The plants, each to its own band of the water table:**
- open water: lily pads, pondweed, duckweed, bogbean (three-part leaves held just above the water);
- the shallows: reed, bulrush (cattail: a dark brown spike on a stem among flat blades), horsetail (jointed green
  stems in stands);
- the hummocks: tussock sedge, cotton grass (white tufts that catch any light at night, the bog's brightest small
  thing), heather where it is driest, sphagnum carpets in reds and greens dulled by night.

## 6. The god in it (the Hide)

The tendrils are the god's veins come up out of the bog (the Vigil's blood tendrils, `vessel.py`). Here they are
rare: a few wrapped round a drowned post or stump, and in their grip, a pale pustule of the god's flesh, barely
glowing. Restrained: it never casts red light, only a faint pale bloom on the water under it.
