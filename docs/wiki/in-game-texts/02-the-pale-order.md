# The Pale Order

*The Pale Order keeps the bone-houses cut into the white ridges of the Ossa, where it lays the dead and counts them.
Its brothers write little and count much. These texts were read to novices, cut over the Order's stairs, or copied
by the few outsiders the Order let in.*

## The Falling

*Read aloud to every novice on his first night in the Order, by the eldest brother of the house. This copy was found
folded into a novice's yoke-strap in a ruined bone-house on the white ridges.*

Before the Count there was no count. That is all a brother may say of that time, so I say it, and I say no more. In
our oldest books it is written as a gap, a space left on the page, and you will not put your finger in it.

Then the Mother fell.

She is the frame the whole world hangs on. She bears it, and she has never once set it down. She came down out of
that time we do not speak of, and where her bones came to rest, there is the world. The ridges you climbed to reach
this door are her bones. The white ground of these mountains is bone, ground fine. The lowlands are her far edges,
where the flesh still lies over her and hides what she is.

The first brothers were there. They stood beside her when she fell, and they did what had to be done, which was to lay
her bones. Every bone went to its place, and every bone was given its name. None was dropped and none was broken.
That was the first Laying, and the Order has been laying bones ever since.

The world was set down, as a load is set down at the end of a long road. It is waiting to be set down once more, at
the very end, and we are here to keep it until then.

Over the Ninth Stair of this house, cut deep, it says: *WE WERE HERE WHEN SHE FELL. WE WILL BE HERE WHEN SHE RESTS.*
A traveller who once copied it wrote that it says *we stood beside her*. The brothers say he copied it wrong. Under
those words there are older lines, cut smaller. Nobody who has copied them has copied them the same way twice.

The lowlands will tell you the god was murdered, or eased, or unsaid. The Order says only that the Mother fell. Why
she fell, the books do not say. A novice who asks is given a broom.

## How the first brother learned to lay a bone

*The sweepers' teaching, given by the oldest brothers to the youngest. A novice wrote it down for his own
remembering. It was found in his niche after him, with his first teeth.*

When the Mother fell, the first brother went to lift the long bone of her leg, to carry it to where it belonged. He
took it in one hand, as you would take up a stick. It would not come. He pulled until his arm shook, and it would not
come. So he knelt beside it and put both hands under it, and it rose, and he carried it.

That is the first thing a novice learns. One hand is a grasp. Two hands are a bearing. A brother never holds a bone
in one hand. A dropped bone is a sin, and a broken bone is a mourning.

As the first brother laid each bone, he said its name. When a bone came to him whose name he did not know, he did not
lay it nameless. He named it for what it was and where it came from: the long bone of the left arm, from the
lowlands. Nothing goes into the wall nameless. Not then, not now.

And as he laid them, he counted them, because a name can be taken from a thing and a number cannot. The Unmaking that
took the lowlands' god apart took it one name at a time, but it has never yet unsaid a number. That was the first
count. Brothers are written by their numbers for the same reason, and a novice lays his birth-name in a niche with his
first teeth, so that the Unmaking has nothing of his to unsay.

The first brother found that a grown man has two hundred and six bones. Add the figures, two and six, and you have
eight, one short of nine. Nine is the Mother's number, because nine is the only number that keeps itself: take nine
of anything, or ninety, or nine hundred and ninety, add up the figures and nine comes back. So a man lives his whole
life one short of the Mother, an open count. He is closed only in the niche. That is why every novice ties a cord of
two hundred and six knots and wears it until he is laid. That is why a novice carries his dead teacher up the stair
on a yoke across his shoulders, the whole of his first year, and learns the weight of a bearing.

The sweepers add one more thing, and only the sweepers say it. The fewer the bones, the holier. A child has more
bones than a man, because some of a child's bones have not yet joined. A man has more than an old brother whose
joints have fused. The Mother is looking for the One Bone, the frame with nothing left to join. No brother has ever
seen it, and every brother hopes to be laid near it.

## The Unfallen

*From a star-watcher's book, kept at one of the bone-houses on the high ridges. The brother signed every page with
his number, 5,013.*

The stars are pieces of the Mother's bone that have not yet come down. We call them the Unfallen. The roof of the
world is a frame of bone strung with cords, nine by nine, and the Unfallen hang in its squares. When one comes loose,
it falls, and a brother of the Seekers walks the line it fell along until he finds it. He brings it back in both his
hands, and it is laid in the Wall of the Come Down.

I have held one. A fallen star is colder than any bone in any niche, and there is no marrow in it at all.

I have counted the sky every clear night for forty years, and I write the count in this book. On the night I write of
now, I was on the high ledge with my lamp shuttered, and there was a man on the ridge across from me, alone, in the
plate of an Ossuarch. He stood a long while looking up. Then he raised both his hands, palms up, as a brother raises
them to receive a bone, and he asked.

I did not hear what he asked. I saw the Unfallen come down. They came down early, before their time, seven of them,
in white lines, all toward the place where he stood.

The sky was short by seven that night, and it is short by seven still. In the morning the Seekers went out. I have
not asked what they found, and I have not counted that ridge since.

## The well

*A brother of the Well wrote this for his house's book, in the year he was made keeper of the windlass. The page
after it is cut out.*

Under the fifth house of the Order there is a well. We draw marrow from it: amber, slow and warm. We render it into
the chrism the brothers use to anoint the bones they lay, and the chrism closes wounds, which stay hard and warm
afterward and swell a little with the years. The Mother gives it freely. She has never once refused.

The rope is spliced longer every year, and every year the marrow still comes heavy at the nine hundredth turn. I do
not know why that is, and I have stopped asking.

At the bottom of the well kneels the Marrow Pontiff. That is an office and not a man, as the Ossuarch is an office
and not a man. It went down before the stair was cut, and it has never come up. Some brothers say it is the eldest
thing in the Order. Some say it is only one bone of the Mother's, never laid, kneeling in the dark where it fell.

It speaks very seldom. In my first year at the windlass, one night, it spoke up the shaft to the brother turning
beside me. It said his number, which was four thousand and fifty. Then it said four hundred and five. Then forty-five.
Then nine. Then it said *Kneel*.

He knelt. He did not get up again. We laid him on the fifth stair with both hands, and he was heavier than he should
have been, and nobody said so.

## Beyond the Pale

*Told in the lowlands, where the saying comes from. A copyist set it down.*

Each of the Order's bone-houses has a warden, and the warden is called the Ossuarch. It is the name of an office,
the way reeve is. Ask a brother the name of the man who holds it and he will give you a number.

Sometimes the Order sends an Ossuarch out of the mountains. When it does, he does not come home. He walks what the
brothers call the Pale. Wherever he gathers bone that needs laying, he sets a white stone, and the stones make a line
that goes on across the lowlands, and the line moves outward year by year. Inside the line, the brothers say, the
dead are kept. Outside it, they are not. That is where the lowland saying comes from: a thing gone *beyond the Pale*
is lost and past keeping.

In the lowlands under the mountains they call him Grandfather Dust, and the children have a counting rhyme about
him, in which he sweeps the child behind the door. Mothers sing it to make the children laugh, and then stop singing
it when the children ask what happens to the child.

There is a story told only in whispers, of an Ossuarch the Order shut out. His count came out above two hundred and
six, they say. He came down the stair with dead of no house anyone knew, standing at his back, and he read the old
lines under the Ninth Stair aloud. The Order closed the gate on him and has never opened it since, and he walks the
Pale alone, outside his own line.

## The Setting Down

*Cut over the door of the Ninth Stair in every bone-house, and read aloud on the Day of the Count, when every wall is
counted.*

When the Count closes at the Last Number, the Mother will set the world down.

Everything finished will stay with her: the laid bone, the closed counts, the dust. Everything unfinished will be
given to the wind: the flesh still growing, the breath still leaving, every name.

And the sky, which is our dust, will come down at last and lie over her like a cloth over a face. Then she will rest.

*A brother's note, written in the margin of a copy of these lines:* This is why we give the dead three funerals. The
flesh goes to the high ledges, for the cold and the wind. The bones go to the niche, for the vigil. The dust goes to
the wind at first light from the highest peak, and the sky over these mountains is pale because it is full of us. No
one has ever seen the dust come down. Before it goes, it is weighed on the Ninth Stair, where only the Ossuarch goes
down, and the weights are never spoken of.

On the Day of the Count, every wall in every house is counted, and every year the count is higher than the year
before, by more than the year's dead. The brothers call the difference the Mother's gift. It is written on a second
leaf, in a hand no tallier has ever seen at work. A tallier may add across that leaf. He may never add down it.

At the end of every Day of the Count, the brothers stand and name every other end of the world that any faith has
ever told, one by one, and condemn each of them aloud. It takes until dark.
