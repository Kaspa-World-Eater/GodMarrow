# The Old Powers

*Before the god died, other powers walked the world, and some of them still do. Most of what is known about them is
wrong. These texts are what frightened people wrote down: readers at the Citadel of the Shattered Femur, copyists,
grandmothers, and a few who saw.*

## The Terrors

*The opening lecture given to new readers at the Citadel of the Shattered Femur, as one of them copied it down in
his first week. He did not finish his reading.*

Forget what the faiths have told you about the beginning. Every faith begins its story with its own god, and the
world is older than any of them.

A god is a belief that grew a body. When enough people fear a thing, or pray to it, for long enough, it stands up.
Fear grows one fastest. A city that is afraid of its river for a hundred years will one spring see the river stand
up. We call the gods of that age the Terrors, because that is what they were made of, and because terror was what
they ate. A city that trembles is a city that feeds its god, and a god nobody fears any more grows thin, and then
hungry, and then comes looking. There was an age when they walked the world, and it is the age most of our ruins come
from, and most of what we know about it is wrong.

Belief does not pass through a god and away. It settles. In the bones of anything that was long feared or long
prayed to, it hardens, and we call what it hardens into marrow. That is why the faiths dig. You will notice, if you
walk the old diggings, that a field dug for marrow gives nothing afterward, and a hill dug hollow sinks. The faiths
dig anyway.

And when you sleep, you dream, and the Terrors walk there too. A dream is a door left open. A nightmare is something
feeding.

Not everything was a god, then or now. There were great things that never learned a name. There were the dead, who in
that age did not lie down unless they were told. And there were things from outside the world that owe nothing to any
of it. Some of those ate the shapes of things, and the gods could do nothing about them.

A god that wastes to the very end does not simply die. It breaks apart into lesser things, the way a dead thing
breaks apart in the heat into grubs, and each of them carries one piece of the god's hunger and nothing else. The
lowlands call them Wants. A Want is hungry for what its god was fed, and it cannot get it, so it bargains, because a
bargain is the nearest thing to a prayer it can get. Whatever you have bargained with in the dark, it was one of
those.

## The Starved Crown

*A warning the grandmothers of the white country give to children who go out after dark. A reader of the Citadel
set it down, and wrote under it what the Citadel thinks.*

There was only ever one dragon. Anyone who tells you there were many has been listening to songs.

It was dead before it was ever alive. When the things with no shape came into the world and began to eat it, the
mountains and the rivers and the faces off people's heads, the god under us made itself a hand out of its own dead to
push them out again: bones the length of a valley, and fire in the cage of the ribs. It was not afraid of them,
because it had nothing left to lose. It drove them out the way they had come in, every one, and then the god went
still again and left it here alone.

So it mended what they had eaten. It raised the mountains again where mountains had been, and cut the rivers back
into their beds with its claws, and that is why the rivers of the north run straight as a cut. The people who lived
in what it made called it the Shaper, and it listened to them so long that it believed them. It forgot whose hand it
had been.

When its worshippers grew few, it ate them, and when there were none left it began on itself, a little at a time, to
stay awake. It sits on a broken throne in a citadel it has eaten from the inside, wearing an iron crown it forged when
it still had subjects to forge for. It is very thin now. It wants nothing in the world so much as to be believed in
again.

So when you go out at night, do not be afraid of the dark. That is how it finds you.

*Under it the reader wrote:* The grandmothers say the ribs that stand in a valley of the white country, twenty-three
of them or twenty-four, depending on who counted, are its own, shed when it began on itself. The Citadel holds that
the god never raised it at all, that nothing did, and that it tells the story of the god's hand only because a thing
made by a god may ask to be worshipped like one. Nobody at the Citadel has gone to count the ribs again.

## The Famines

*From a pilgrim's account of the road through the far south, written at the next inn.*

On the third day we saw one of the Famines walking, far off across the plain.

The readers say the Famines were gods once, or the corpses of gods. Each was as tall as a hill. Whole kingdoms
worshipped them by walking up the long ramp of their own bodies into them, and were not seen again. Most of them
have fallen and lie where they fell, and grass grows on them. One still walks.

It walked very slowly. It took the whole afternoon to cross the edge of the sky. Where it had trodden, the ground had
a pulse in it, a beat you could feel in your feet, and we walked round those places afterward.

At dusk, a long way up its side, there was a light. Our guide said it was Dhum the Swallowed, the last city that
walked up into it, and that the lamps of Dhum are still lit inside it after all these years. He said nobody knows who
lights them.

## The tower that drinks

*A letter from a town in the sight of the tower. It was carried out by a pedlar, who said it was the only letter
anybody there had written in a year.*

We can see it from every window. It is a tower, and it is full to the top of something that moves. It is all one
thing, and it is full of eyes, and every eye is someone's. On a clear day you can see the eyes turn to look at the
town, all together.

It is awake. It has always been awake.

Nobody here has dreamed for as long as I have been alive. We lie down and close our eyes and open them again in the
morning, and nothing has happened in between. The old people say it was not always so. They say the tower drinks the
dreams of any town in its sight, and that a town in its sight has no dreams of its own.

I do not know what a dream is. I am writing to ask whether anyone where you are still has them, and what they are
like.

## The reaper below

*A note found in the margin of the Citadel's oldest book of drawings, beside a drawing of a cavern. The hand is a
scholar's of the Pale Order.*

The book calls it the Reaper Below. It is a skeleton too great to measure, the drawing says, lying in a cavern
floored with the dead of a whole age, and its right arm ends in a scythe grown out of its own bone. The book says it
may be asleep.

I have walked the lowest halls under the fifth house of my Order, where the stair's risers come only to the knee and
the niches are too long for any body. I have looked at this drawing for a long time.

I will say only that I have seen this shape twice.

## Vesk

*Told by iron-gleaners, who cross the empty country a long way behind Vesk and pick up what it leaves.*

The readers will tell you Vesk is one of the Famines, a god that outlived its people and kept on walking. We say it
was a city, and it still is.

The people of Vesk worshipped their walls. A wall keeps you alive, and a people that lives behind a wall long enough
begins to pray to it, and a thing prayed to long enough stands up. One night the walls of Vesk stood up and walked,
and the city went with them, because the city was inside them. Its people were turned to iron where they stood, in
the streets and doorways and at their windows, and they went too.

It is all iron now, walls and towers and streets and people grown together into one body a league long, and it walks
still, a grey line moving on the edge of the sky. It eats as it goes. It eats the iron out of the ground, and the
soil behind it goes grey and dead and will not hold a post.

Where it has stopped to rest, it leaves a square of flattened ground with the marks of streets in it, and sometimes,
in the middle, an iron child nobody could carry, left behind. We do not take those. Some of us have lain near one at
night and heard it tapping, very slowly, as if it were trying to walk after the rest.

## The Lanternless

*From a letter of the tender of the lantern round, written to a new tender. It is the part of the letter
the new tender underlined.*

There are things on the roads at night that eat souls. They are older than the faiths, the old tenders said, and in
the old age they were rivals of the gods themselves, and fed where the gods fed. The readers call them the
Lanternless. They come near lanterns, because a lantern has a soul in it, and they wait at the edge of the light.
They do not step into it. They wait for it to gutter.

You will know when one is near. The flame leans away from it. It draws down small and blue and leans against the far
side of the glass, as far from the dark as it can get.

When that happens, do not open the glass to trim the wick. Do not look into the dark to see what it is. Walk on,
steady, the way you would walk past a stranger in a doorway, and talk to the lantern. Tell it where you are going. It
helps them to know someone means to bring them there.

## The Hallowed

*Told in whispers by people who live under one. A copyist who did not say where he lived wrote it down.*

There are men and women who drink the belief that blood carries. They take a little, from many, over many years,
and they do not grow old. One lives among a people. Never two.

You cannot kill one with iron, or fire, or water. The only thing that kills one is being forgotten, and a people that
has one never forgets it. They keep its name in every prayer without ever saying whose name it is. They keep its house
swept. They send their daughters to the door on the right nights, and the daughters come back pale and say nothing.
The readers call them the Hallowed, after the prayers: hallowed be the name.

If you ever come among people whose prayers all end with a name you do not know, keep walking.

## The Thrones Below

*Written on the wall of the Citadel's prison by a reader kept there. Under it, another hand has written: "He was
right."*

Belief makes more than gods. Kings were believed in, and abbots, and matrons of great houses, and most of all the
sovereigns of the cities that once hung in the sky, believed in so hard and so long by every frightened people under
them that they stopped being people. When their cities came down, they did not die with them, because they were too
believed-in to lie down. They sit where they fell, crowned, in the broken hearts of the cities, still holding court,
still sending for marrow that does not come. The faiths call them liches. The Citadel calls them the Thrones Below.
There are more of them than the Citadel admits.

## The Uninvited

*From the Citadel's book of things it cannot place. The reader who kept the book wrote at the top of every page: these
owe nothing to the corpse, nor to any god, nor to the Silence.*

The first is the Unborn. It lies curled in the dark between the stars, a shape like a child the size of the night,
not yet born. The grandmothers of the white country say the earthquakes are its kicks. When it is born, they say, it
will be very hungry, and the world will be the first thing it sees.

The second is the Drowned Twin. It stands on the other side of every still surface: a puddle, a polished blade, the
blood of the far sea in a calm. When you look in, it looks out, wearing your face, a little late. The circles of the
Veiled Crone say that over there it lives your life a breath behind you, and that it is tired of being behind, and
that one day it will step through first. The circles cover every mirror at a death, and some say this is why.

The third is a gaunt pilgrim. He has been walking upward since before the god's last breath, on roads that go up even
where the ground is flat. Wherever he passes, the dead lie back down, and so do some of the living.

The last is a lectern. It is a reading-stand with a book open on it, found in different places and never twice in the
same one. The book is the life of whoever reads it, and it is always one page ahead of where they are.
