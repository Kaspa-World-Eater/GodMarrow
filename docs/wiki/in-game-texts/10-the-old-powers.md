# The Old Powers

*Before the god died, other powers walked the world, and some of them still do. Most of what is known about them is
wrong. These texts are what frightened people wrote down: readers at the Citadel of the Shattered Femur, copyists,
grandmothers, and a few who saw.*

## The loud gods

*The opening lecture given to new readers at the Citadel of the Shattered Femur, as one of them copied it down in
his first week. He did not finish his reading.*

Forget what the faiths have told you about the beginning. Every faith begins its story with its own god, and the
world is older than any of them.

A god is a belief that grew a body. When enough people fear a thing, or pray to it, for long enough, it stands up.
Fear feeds it and worship feeds it, and a god nobody remembers starves. So the old powers ruled by terror, because
terror is the surest worship there is. A city that trembles is a city that feeds its god. There was an age when gods
walked the world, loud with it, and it is the age most of our ruins come from, and most of what we know about it is
wrong.

Belief does not pass through a god and away. It settles. In the bones of anything that was long feared or long
prayed to, it hardens, and we call what it hardens into marrow. That is why the faiths dig.

And when you sleep, you dream, and the powers walk there too. A nightmare is something feeding.

Not everything was a god, then or now. There were great things that never learned a name. There were lords, the
dead, and things from outside the world that owe nothing to any of it. Some of those things ate gods.

A god that starves to the very end does not simply die. It breaks apart into lesser things, the way a dead thing
breaks apart in the heat into flies. Those lesser things are what the lowlands call demons. They are hungry for what
the god was fed, and they cannot get it, and so they bargain. A bargain is the nearest thing to a prayer they can get.

## The last of the dragons

*A warning the grandmothers of the white country give to children who go out after dark. A reader of the Citadel
set it down.*

In the loud age there were dragons. They were gods, and they lived on the terror of a world without walls. They were
one power among many in that age. Perhaps they were the greatest; perhaps they were only the ones that lasted
longest. Some say the ribs that stand in a valley of the white country, twenty-three of them or twenty-four, depending
on who counted, were one of them.

They starved like all the rest, as the walls went up and people stopped looking at the sky. All but one.

The last of them is called the Starved Crown. When its worshippers grew few, it ate them, and when there were none
left, it began on itself, a little at a time, to stay awake. It sits on a broken throne in a citadel it has consumed
from the inside, wearing an iron crown it forged when it still had subjects to forge for. It is very thin now. It
wants nothing in the world as much as it wants to be feared again.

So when you go out at night, the grandmothers say, do not be afraid of the dark. That is how it finds you.

## The Famines

*From a pilgrim's account of the road through the far south, written at the next inn.*

On the third day we saw one of the Famines walking, far off across the plain.

The readers say the Famines were gods once, or the corpses of gods. Each was as tall as a hill. Whole kingdoms
worshipped them by walking up the long ramp of their own bodies into them, and were not seen again. Most of them
have fallen and lie where they fell, and grass grows on them. One still walks.

It walked very slowly. It took the whole afternoon to cross the edge of the sky. Where it had trodden, the ground had
a pulse in it, a beat you could feel in your feet, and we walked round those places afterward.

At dusk, a long way up its side, there was a light. Our guide said it was Dhum the Swallowed, the last city that
walked up into it, and that the lamps of Dhum are still lit inside it after all these years. He said nobody knows who
lights them.

## The tower that drinks

*A letter from a town in the sight of the tower. It was carried out by a pedlar, who said it was the only letter
anybody there had written in a year.*

We can see it from every window. It is a tower, and it is full to the top of something that moves. It is all one
thing, and it is full of eyes, and every eye is someone's. On a clear day you can see the eyes turn to look at the
town, all together.

It is awake. It has always been awake.

Nobody here has dreamed for as long as I have been alive. We lie down and close our eyes and open them again in the
morning, and nothing has happened in between. The old people say it was not always so. They say the tower drinks the
dreams of any town in its sight, and that a town in its sight has no dreams of its own.

I do not know what a dream is. I am writing to ask whether anyone where you are still has them, and what they are
like.

## The reaper below

*A note found in the margin of the Citadel's oldest book of drawings, beside a drawing of a cavern. The hand is a
scholar's of the Pale Order.*

The book calls it the Reaper Below. It is a skeleton too great to measure, the drawing says, lying in a cavern
floored with the dead of a whole age, and its right arm ends in a scythe grown out of its own bone. The book says it
may be asleep.

I have walked the lowest halls under the fifth house of my Order, where the stair's risers come only to the knee and
the niches are too long for any body. I have looked at this drawing for a long time.

I will say only that I have seen this shape twice.

## The walking walls

*Told by salt-carriers, who cross the empty country where Vesk used to stand.*

There were cities that worshipped their walls. A wall keeps you alive, and a people that lives behind a wall long
enough begins to pray to it, and a thing prayed to long enough stands up.

Vesk was one. One night its walls stood up and walked away, and the city went with them, because the city was inside
them. Its people were turned to iron where they stood, in the streets and doorways and at their windows, and they
went too.

Vesk walks still. Salt-carriers have seen it on the horizon, a grey line moving. Where it has stopped to rest, it
leaves a square of flattened ground with the marks of streets in it, and sometimes, in the middle, an iron child
nobody could carry, left behind.

## The mouths at the wick

*From a letter of the tender of the lantern round, written to a new tender. It is the part of the letter
the new tender underlined.*

There are things on the roads at night that eat souls. They are older than the god, the old tenders said, and they
were its rivals once. The readers call them the Mouths at the Wick. They come near lanterns, because a lantern has a
soul in it, and they wait at the edge of the light.

You will know when one is near. The flame leans away from it. It draws down small and blue and leans against the far
side of the glass, as far from the dark as it can get.

When that happens, do not open the glass to trim the wick. Do not look into the dark to see what it is. Walk on,
steady, the way you would walk past a stranger in a doorway, and talk to the lantern. Tell it where you are going. It
helps them to know someone means to bring them there.

## The unforgotten

*Told in whispers in towns that have one. A copyist who did not say which town he lived in wrote it down.*

There are men and women who drink the belief that blood carries. They take a little, from many, over many years,
and they do not grow old. One lives in a town. Never two.

You cannot kill one with iron, or fire, or water. The only thing that kills one is being forgotten, and a town that
has one never forgets it. The town keeps its name in the prayers without ever saying whose name it is. The town keeps
its house swept. The town sends its daughters to the door on the right nights, and they come back pale and say
nothing.

A town owned by one of the Unforgotten worships it and never says so. If you ever come to a town whose prayers all end
with a name you do not know, keep walking.

## The lords

*Written on the wall of the Citadel's prison by a reader kept there. Under it, another hand has written: "He was
right."*

Belief makes more than gods. Kings were believed in, and abbots, and matrons of great houses, and generals,
believed in so hard and so long by so many frightened people that they became small gods themselves. Some of them
became something worse when they died, because they were too believed-in to lie down. The faiths call those the
liches. There are more of them than the Citadel admits.

## The Visitors

*From the Citadel's book of things it cannot place. The reader who kept the book wrote at the top of every page: these
owe nothing to the corpse, nor to any god, nor to the Silence.*

The first is the Tallyman. Wherever a count is wrong, he comes to take the difference. Nobody has ever seen him
arrive. A tally of the dead made twice and found short, a barrow with one grave more than its families: he has been
there.

The second is the Ferryman. The river at his ford left long ago, the lowlands say, when its god rode off on the
breath. The Ferryman still takes fares across it. Pay him, and he poles you across the dry bed, slowly, and you come
out on the far side wet to the knees.

The third is the Gaunt Pilgrim. He has been walking upward since before the god's last breath, on roads that go up
even where the ground is flat. Wherever he passes, the dead lie back down, and so do some of the living.

The last is the Lectern. It is a reading-stand with a book open on it, found in different places and never twice in
the same one. The book is the life of whoever reads it, and it is always one page ahead of where they are.
