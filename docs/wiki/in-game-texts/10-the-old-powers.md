# The Old Powers

*Before the god died, other powers walked the world, and some of them still do. Most of what is known about them is
wrong. These texts are what frightened people wrote down: the last readers at the Citadel of the Shattered Femur,
grandmothers, and a few who saw.*

## The Ythraal

*The opening lecture given to the rare new reader at the Citadel of the Shattered Femur, as one of them copied it down
in his first week. He did not finish his reading.*

Forget what the faiths have told you about the beginning. Every faith begins its story with its own god, and the
world is older than any of them.

A god is a belief that grew a body. When enough people fear a thing, or pray to it, for long enough, it stands up.
Fear grows one fastest. In that age, when there were cities, a city that was afraid of its river for a lifetime would
one spring see the river stand up. The oldest books call the gods of that age the Ythraal. Nobody knows what the word
meant; everyone says it quietly. Fear is what they were made of, and fear was what they ate. A city that trembled was
a city that fed its god, and a god nobody fears any more grows thin, and then hungry, and then comes looking. There was an age when they walked the world, and it is the age most of our ruins come
from, and most of what we know about it is wrong.

Belief does not pass through a god and away. It settles. In the bones of anything that was long feared or long
prayed to, it hardens, and we call what it hardens into marrow. That is why the faiths dig. You will notice, if you
walk the old diggings, that a field dug for marrow gives nothing afterward, and a hill dug hollow sinks. The faiths
dig anyway.

And when you sleep, you dream, and the Ythraal walk there too. A dream is a door left open. A nightmare is something
feeding.

Not everything was a god, then or now. There were great things that never learned a name. There were the dead, who in
that age did not lie down unless they were told. And there were things from outside the world that owe nothing to any
of it. Some of those ate the shapes of things, and the gods could do nothing about them.

A god that wastes to the very end does not simply die. It breaks apart into lesser things, the way a dead thing
breaks apart in the heat into grubs, and each of them carries one piece of the god's hunger and nothing else. The
lowlands call them Wants. A Want is hungry for what its god was fed, and it cannot get it, so it bargains, because a
bargain is the nearest thing to a prayer it can get. Whatever you have bargained with in the dark, it was one of
those.

## The Starved Crown

*A warning the grandmothers of the Pale give to children who go out after dark. A reader of the Citadel
set it down, and wrote under it what the Citadel thinks.*

There was only ever one of it. Anyone who tells you there were many has been listening to songs.

It was dead before it was ever alive. When the three things with no shape put themselves into the world, feeling for
something, and the mountains and the rivers and the faces off people's heads came away wherever they felt, the god
under us made itself a hand out of its own dead: bones the length of a valley, wings of bone that held no air and flew
anyway, and fire in the cage of the ribs. It was not afraid of them, because it had nothing left to lose. It fought
them longer than anyone has counted, and they never once turned round. They did not know it was there. They did not
know we were.

One of them lay down at last, by the red sea, and did not get up. The other two went away. And it fell, because it had
broken itself on them too many times, and it struck the ground so hard it made a cup a day's walk across, and lay in the
bottom of it, burning low.

The people who found it there knelt. They built a city round it, ring on ring up the sides of the cup, and worshipped it
for saving the world, and it lay and listened to them, and it woke. When its worshippers grew few, it ate them, and
when there were none left it began on itself, a little at a time, to stay awake. It sits on a broken throne at the
bottom of the cup, in the city it has eaten from the inside, wearing an iron crown they forged for it when it still had
subjects. It is very thin now. It wants nothing in the world so much as to be believed in again.

So when you go out at night, do not be afraid of the dark. That is how it finds you.

*Under it the reader wrote:* The grandmothers say the ribs that stand in a valley of the Pale, twenty-three
of them or twenty-four, depending on who counted, are its own, shed when it began on itself. The Citadel holds that
the god never raised it at all, that nothing did, and that it tells the story of the god's hand only because a thing
made by a god may ask to be worshipped like one. Nobody at the Citadel has gone to count the ribs again.

## The Famines

*From a pilgrim's account of the road through the far south. The leaves were found at a waystone, weighted down with
a stone.*

On the third day we saw one of the Famines walking, far off across the plain.

The readers say the Famines were gods once, or the corpses of gods. Each was as tall as a hill. Whole kingdoms
worshipped them by walking up the long ramp of their own bodies into them, and were not seen again. Most of them
have fallen and lie where they fell, and grass grows on them. One still walks.

It walked very slowly. It took the whole afternoon to cross the edge of the sky. Where it had trodden, the ground had
a pulse in it, a beat you could feel in your feet, and we walked round those places afterward.

At dusk, a long way up its side, there was a light. Our guide said it was Dhum the Swallowed, the last city that
walked up into it, and that the lamps of Dhum are still lit inside it after all these years. He said nobody knows who
lights them.

## The Needle

*A letter with nobody's name on the outside, found on the body of a woman on the road away from the Needle. She came
from one of the few houses still standing in its sight.*

We can see it from the window, over the trees. It is iron, and it goes up until the grey takes it, and it has no
door. Old Haddo climbed it once when he was young, as far as the crack where the rust has eaten through, and looked in.
He said it is full to the top of something like the water in a blister, and that there are bones in it all the way
down, going soft, and eyes. He said the eyes were bigger than he was, and that they turned to look at him. He came
down and told us, and the next winter he got up in his sleep and walked into the trees, and we have not seen him
since. We can all remember his dreams now. They are mostly about the sea.

Nobody here has dreamed for as long as I have been alive. We lie down and close our eyes and open them again in the
morning, and nothing has happened in between. The old ones say it was not always so. They say the Needle drinks the
dreams of anyone who lives in its sight, and that a house in its sight has no dreams of its own. We tie our sleepers
by the ankle.

I do not know what a dream is. I am writing to ask whether anyone where you are still has them, and what they are
like.

## The reaper below

*A note found in the margin of the Citadel's oldest book of drawings, beside a drawing of a cavern. The hand is a
scholar's of the Pale Order.*

The book calls it Oss-Karrhath. It is a skeleton too great to measure, the drawing says, lying in a cavern
floored with the dead of a whole age, and its right arm ends in a scythe grown out of its own bone. The book says it
may be asleep.

I have walked the lowest halls under the fifth house of my Order, where the stair's risers come only to the knee and
the niches are too long for any body. I have looked at this drawing for a long time.

I will say only that I have seen this shape twice.

## Vesk

*Scratched into the wall of a pithead hut in the iron hills, by a miner who worked the hills alone, went down one more
time, and did not come up. A reader of the Citadel found it and copied it out.*

The readers will tell you Vesk is one of the Famines, walking on the edge of the sky. It is not. Vesk is under us.

It was a city once, up here, where the pithead is. When the ground went soft in the old time it went soft under Vesk
first, because Vesk had dug it hollow, and one night the whole city started going down. And the people would not let
it. They went down under their houses, every one of them, the children too, and put their shoulders to it and held it
up. The iron came up into them out of the dark. Their blood stopped in them and went hard, and they went to iron where
they stood, and grew together, shoulder to shoulder and hip to hip, and they did not let go. Vesk stood up on them. On
their legs. Then it walked, down, into the Deep, where the iron is, and it walks there still.

There are prints in the ore at the bottom of the new shaft. Round. Hundreds. Going on under the rock.

I have had my ear to the ore. You can hear it walking. Under the walking you can hear them. They are not screaming.
They are only holding, the way you hold your breath.

I am going down again tomorrow. I want to hear whether they say anything.

## The Hallowed

*Told in whispers by the few who live under one. Written on a leaf left at a waystone, with no name on it.*

There are men and women who drink the belief that blood carries. They take a little, from many, over many years,
and they do not grow old. One lives among whoever is left in a place. Never two.

You cannot kill one with iron, or fire, or water. The only thing that kills one is being forgotten, and the few who
have one never forget it. They keep its name in every prayer without ever saying whose name it is. They keep its house
swept. They send their daughters to the door on the right nights, and the daughters come back pale and say nothing.
The readers call them the Hallowed, after the prayers: hallowed be the name.

If you ever come among people whose prayers all end with a name you do not know, keep walking.

## The Man in the Rust

*Written on the wall of the Citadel's prison by a reader kept there. Under it, another hand has written: "He was
right."*

Belief makes more than gods. Kings were believed in, and abbots, and matrons of great houses, and more than any of them,
whoever ruled the great city in the sky, believed in so hard and so long by every frightened people under it that it
stopped being a person, if it ever was one. When the great city cut its chains and went up, its ruler went up with it.
The Citadel teaches that the city got out of the world. It did not. Look at the dark moon on a clear night. Look at its
edges. That is the city, and it is not small; it is only far away, and it is brown because it is rust.

It is dead up there, all of it, and the dead walk its streets as if they still had somewhere to be. And on the highest
throne there is one thing that does not walk, because it is sitting, and it gives the order to rise. Every day. To a
city that cannot rise any further. It has been giving it for longer than anyone has counted, and it has not
once been obeyed, and it has not once stopped. The shore folk called it the Man in the Rust. The Citadel calls it the
Regent and will not discuss it, and that is why I am here.

## Outside of everything

*From the Citadel's book of things it cannot place. The reader who kept the book wrote at the top of every page: these
owe nothing to the corpse, nor to any god, nor to the Silence.*

The first is the Unborn. It lies curled in the dark between the stars, a shape like a child the size of the night,
not yet born. The grandmothers of the Pale say the earthquakes are its kicks. When it is born, they say, it
will be very hungry, and the world will be the first thing it sees.

The second is the Drowned Twin. It stands on the other side of every still surface: a puddle, a polished blade, the
blood of the far sea in a calm. When you look in, it looks out, wearing your face, a little late. The circles of the
Veiled Crone say that over there it lives your life a breath behind you, and that it is tired of being behind, and
that one day it will step through first. The circles cover every mirror at a death, and some say this is why.

The third is a gaunt pilgrim. He has been walking upward since before the god's last breath, on roads that go up even
where the ground is flat. Wherever he passes, the dead lie back down, and so do some of the living.

The last is a lectern. It is a reading-stand with a book open on it, found in different places and never twice in the
same one. The book is the life of whoever reads it, and it is always one page ahead of where they are.
