# The Long Back

*The only firm road through the Sunken Bog is a line of great round bones running through the black water, mostly
overgrown, coiling back on itself across the whole of the bog. Everyone who has walked it has a name for it and a story
about it, and no two agree. The shore folk's own telling, the oldest, is "The stair", in `08-the-shore-folk.md`.
These are the others.*

## The names

*From a pilgrim's guide to the wet country, copied many times over. This passage is in every copy, though the copies
differ in almost everything else.*

The road across the Sunken Bog has as many names as there are people who walk it.

The bog folk who live along it call it the Long Back, and they will tell you their own people laid it at the start of
things, out of the biggest stones they could find, so as to have a road that would not sink. They are proud of it,
and they keep it. Ask them why the stones are hollow in the middle and knobbed at the sides and they will tell you
that is how stones were made in those days.

Pilgrims call it Saint Uss's Causeway, after a saint who is in no calendar of any faith. The boatmen of the Fen call
it the Stair of the Drowned King, and the bog folk's children call it Old Coil and dare each other to walk it
after dark. The shore folk, who lived in the bog before they lived in the trees, call it the Long One, and say it was
a god.

Nobody knows what it was called first. The oldest bog folk say that if it had a name of its own, the name is under
the water with the rest of it.

## The stair of the drowned king

*Told by the boatmen of the Drowned Fen on the nights they sleep under the Kneeling Arch. A purifier of the Myriad
wrote it down in her day-book.*

Before the bog was a bog, there was a kingdom in the wet country, and it had a king. Nobody remembers the kingdom's
name or the king's. One spring the king heard something calling him from under the water, and he put on his crown and
all his rings and walked out along the Back, and his whole court walked behind him with bells in their hands, ringing,
so that whatever called him would know he was coming.

Where the Back goes down under the water, he did not stop. He walked down into the black water as if it were a stair,
and his court walked down after him, ringing, and the water closed over the last of the bells.

The boatmen say he is still walking. They say the stair goes down for as long as there has been a bog, and he has not
reached the bottom of it yet. On still nights, if you put your ear to the water beside the Back, you can hear the
court's bells, very far down.

And they say this, which is why they tell the story at all. If you ever see a crowned head in the water beside the
Back, do not reach down and help him up. He is only looking for the next step down, and he
will take you with him to show him where it is.

## Saint Uss

*From the same pilgrim's guide. The saint's day is kept by nobody, and every copy of the guide gives a different
day.*

Saint Uss was a holy woman who lived in the wet country long ago. She saw the pilgrims sinking in the bog, one after
another, on their way to wherever they were going, and she could not bear it. So she laid herself down in the mud and
gave her bones to be a road, so that the faithful would not sink.

One woman has only so many bones. The pilgrims say the miracle is that hers were enough. Every stone of the causeway
is one of her bones, they say, given again and again, and the road goes on as long as anyone needs it to. Pilgrims
kneel at the near end and kiss the first stone before they walk, and they walk barefoot, so as not to hurt her.

The guide adds one more thing, in a smaller hand. *Uss* is only what is left of the saint's name, a
longer one worn down by many mouths the way a step is worn hollow by many feet. What the rest of it was, nobody
knows.

## The counters

*A report sent to a bone-house of the Pale Order by two brothers sent to count the Back, signed with their numbers,
7,209 and 3,114, with the house's answer written under it.*

We have counted the bones of the road nine times, from the near end to the far, and we have nine counts, and no two of
them agree. The tallier counts higher each time he walks it. I count lower. We have checked each other's tallies
and found no fault in either.

We took up a bone where the road had broken and the bone had come loose. It is not one of the Mother's. The marrow is
still in it, and the marrow is black. It is warm in the hand, and it is heavy, and neither of us would carry it in one
hand, though it is small.

The tallier wishes to walk it a tenth time. I do not. We ask leave to come home.

*The answer, in the hand of the warden of the house:* Come home, both of you. Leave it uncounted. Do not count what is
not hers. Lay the loose bone back where you found it, and lay it with both hands.

*There is no record of the tallier coming home.*

## The pit

*Scratched into the altar slab at the lip of the pit of black stone, out in the open water where the Back coils round
on itself, among the old carved signs and in a much later hand.*

We fed him here.

The old ones said a thousand, and more than a thousand, every year, and you can see where it ran. We cut the gutters
and the gutters ran full, and he drank, and the water kept to its place, and the road stayed up out of it.

When we stopped, he did not stop wanting it. He only stopped being able to come for it.

Some of us have said it was never him we fed. They say the pit goes down farther than he was long, and that he only
lay round it the way a guard sleeps across a doorway. We do not know. We did not go down to see.

If you hear the light breathing, go back the way you came.
