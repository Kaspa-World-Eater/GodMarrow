# The Gone Peoples

*Other peoples were here before the Tithed, and they are gone, or nearly. What they believed comes down to us in what
they cut in stone, in what the Citadel's readers have made of their marks, and in what they have said, once or
twice, to people who wrote it down afterward.*

## The Choir

*Heard through the floor of the ninth gallery of the Rib Crypts, under the Long Candle. A sweeper of the Pale Order lay
with his ear to the flagstones every night for a winter and wrote down what he could make out. This is his fair
copy.*

They sing under the floor. It is very faint, and it never stops, and when you have listened long enough you begin to
hear words in it. This is what the words say, as near as I could make them out.

They were the part of the god that told it what it was. When it was alive, they sang behind its ribs, in the long
groove there, and the song was the god knowing itself. They sang in turns, so that the note was always held, so that
there was never a moment when the god did not know what it was. There were eleven hundred and one of them.

Then, once, they all drew breath together. They do not know why. Every one of them breathed in at the same moment, and
for that one moment nobody was singing, and That Which Cannot Be Named came in through the rest. They call it the
first silence. It is their holiest day and their worst one, and they sing about it more than anything else.

After that there was one of them. Or there were eleven hundred and one, and then one. I could not make out which.

They speak of us, once. The congregation that knelt in the god, they sing, answered badly, with names.

Now they are waiting. The god's out-breath is still going out, and when it has finished, there will be a rest, and
they mean to come in on the next line. They do not say what the next line is. I think they are practising it.

## The Angled

*Copied by a scholar of the Undercount from the plain script in the lowest halls under a bone-house of the Pale Order,
and sent in secret to the Citadel. The Order does not know the copy exists.*

Below the lowest stair the brothers use, there are older halls. Their stair risers come only to the knee, and the
treads slope a little, so that you arrive at the bottom leaning. The niches cut in their walls are too long for any
body I know. The Order does not go down there. I have been down there eleven times, and each time I have come up with
a nosebleed.

Count the corners of a hall down there and the count comes out different every time, and higher. The ones who cut the
halls left no name for themselves, only a sign at the head of every stair, all angles, more angles than its lines can
hold. I have not tried to copy it. The brother who went down with me the first time did, and he went blind in the eye
he drew it with. The Citadel calls them the Angled, because that is the one thing about them that everyone who has
stood in their halls agrees on.

Beside the signs there is a plainer script of straight cuts, and that I could read, in four years. They say they were
cut from the first of the room, and began cutting at once. They say, over the niches, *Lie down facing the stair.
Watch.* They say, in many places, *Our joints do not finish.*

There is one thing they will not cut. Wherever it should appear, they cut around it, and leave a space. *Do not say its
name*, they say. *We cut no name for it. We cut around it.* Every ring of carving they made has a gap in it the width
of a thumb.

Near the end of the last hall there is a pillar, and a seat cut at its foot, empty. Over it is the newest carving of
all, cleaner than the rest. It says *Now one of you.*

There is one more carving I must report, because I have seen its words before, on the inner face of a waystone, where
you can read them only standing in the pit. It says: *WE CUT THEM FACING IN, SO THE MOUTH IS KEPT BY WHAT IT HOLDS.*

## The court of white faces

*Told by the lantern-tenders of the lowlands, who see the Wick-Saints come to their lanterns at dusk. A copyist set
it down.*

In the warm place where the Tithed knelt, before the god died, there was a court of white faces high up in the dark,
and it sat in judgment on souls. The faces were smooth and white as glazed clay, and they watched what everyone
thought, and they found most of it wanting.

When the god died, the court fell. The faces came down out of the dark and lay where they fell, all over the Hide,
under the ash and in the crypts, still white, still watching. Nobody wanted to pick them up.

The moths found them. Moths go to anything pale in the dark. They found the white faces and settled on them and took
them up, and now they wear them. At dusk they come to the lanterns on the roads, swooping in their long arcs, and the
faces on them are the faces of the court.

The tenders say the faces still judge. If one of them hangs in the air and looks at you for a long time before it
comes, it has found you wanting, and you should not be there when it decides what to do about it.

## The Lifted

*Cut on the underside of a fallen city, where only someone looking up from below was ever meant to read it. A reader of
the Citadel copied it lying on his back in a trench the lamp-diggers had cut under the stone, and wrote his own notes
after it.*

In the beginning the world was heavy, and everything in it was held down. The rivers were held in their beds, and the
people were held to the ground, and the gods were held to the people who feared them. Then the first of us opened a
bone and found the weight inside it, and drank it, and was light.

*We were the first thing in the world that did not fall.*

*The reader's notes:* The Citadel calls them the Lifted, and their country Ithmar, which is what the peoples under
them called the light in the sky. They drew the marrow up from a mile down and burned it, and hung their cities in
the air where nothing could reach them, and lived there longer than anyone has counted since. The city I copied this
from lies upside down in the ash, and the words face the sky. The ones who cut them never thought anyone would read
them from above.

How the cities came down, the Citadel argues every winter. Most of the readers say the sovereigns made war on each
other for the last of the marrow, and drew it up from deeper than any shaft had gone, until the shafts ran dry and
there was nothing left to hold the cities up. They call it the Marrow War. The diggers who go under the grass for the
lamps that still burn there say the cities did not fall but were pulled, by something below that had been looking up
at them all that time. I asked the oldest digger what had pulled them. She said it was whatever had been holding the
rivers in their beds.

## The Unison

*From a clay tablet dug out of the mire at the foot of the great stepped tower. A reader of the Citadel made out its
marks, and wrote his reading beside a drawing of the tablet.*

They were one mind in many bodies, and they lived in the mire. They built the stepped tower and the causeway that
runs to it, and nobody has lived in either since. The Citadel calls them the Unison, which is the Citadel's name and
not theirs. Theirs, as near as the marks can be read, is a single mark that means *we*, cut once for all of them.

The tablet tells their beginning. At the beginning, it says, the whole of everything was one thought, a long one, and
they were inside it, thinking it together. It was dark, and close, and they were all together. It was the only time
they were warm all through.

They thought one thought at a time, all of them at once, and it took them a generation to think it. The tablet does
not say what came after, and it does not tell their end. It ends with a line of marks the reader could not make out,
repeated many times to the edge of the clay. The Citadel holds that it is the last thought they had, and that they
are still thinking it, somewhere, all of them, very slowly.

## The Sky-Climbers

*A rubbing taken from a stone at the foot of the stair nobody climbs, in the Heights. The stone is cut in a script
nobody now reads; this is the reading the Citadel accepts, though it argues about it.*

The sky is a mouth, open. We are inside it. Everything is inside it.

A people that knows it is inside a mouth does not stay. We have built the stair, and we will climb out by it, all of
us, and carry the children, and leave nothing behind for the mouth to close on.

That is all the stone says. The stair goes up into the cloud, and no one living has seen the top of it. The people who
lived at its foot climbed, all of them, and their houses were left with the doors open. None of them came down. The
readers call them the Sky-Climbers. Some say they were climbing toward the last of the cities that hung in the sky,
still up there in the cloud; some say they were climbing away from it.

## The tall ones at the door

*What one of the Emptied said of its own people, through a closed door, to a reader of the Citadel who did not open
it. He wrote it down that night and left the Citadel the next morning.*

It knocked at my door after midnight, three times, softly, and when I asked who was there it answered very politely.
It said it would not keep me long. It asked whether there was marrow in the house. I said there was not, and it
thanked me, and then it did not go away. It stood outside the door, and after a while it began to talk, as if it were
lonely.

It said its people had lived high up once, in the light, where nothing could reach them, and that it had been a long
way down. It said their lords had drunk from them, a little and then more, to fight their wars, until there was
nothing left in their bones at all. It said that was not dying. It said it remembered being full, and that it would
like to remember it better.

The common roads call them Marrow-Eaters, and say they crack the bones of the sleeping to suck out what is inside. I
had believed it. It did not sound hungry. It sounded like a guest who had been kept waiting.

It said they were tall and hollow, and that it would show me if I opened the door. I did not open the door. In the
morning there were marks on the outside of it, very high up, as if someone tall had leaned their forehead against
it for a long time.

## The Slayers

*From the walls of the temple deep inside the body, as the lamp-bearers of the Last Vigil copied them, a line at a
time, between watches. The lamp-bearers say the walls are warm, and that there is a thought still thinking in the
temple, and that it is better not to copy too much at once.*

Something was opened, and we came in where it was open. There was never a beginning. There was only a way in.

We leave no bodies. We have never had them to leave.

This place is a thought, and it is forgetting what is in it, one thing at a time. It forgets a name, and then the
thing the name was for. It forgets a face, and then the one who wore it. When it has forgotten everything that is in
it, it will be a thought that remembers no one. That is rest. We are helping.

*Under the copy, a lamp-bearer wrote:* The readers ask whether the Slayers mean the Silence. I cannot say. Their walls
never once mention it. They may mean the Silence, or its opposite, or something none of us has a word for.

## What answers iron

*Told by a miner of the white country to a copyist at the pithead, on the day he gave up the work.*

We bleed the first ore we bring up out of a new shaft. Everybody does. Iron is old blood, and you give a little back.
I had done it forty years and never asked why.

At the bottom of the deepest shaft I worked, the iron is warm. It is ridged and crusted like a scab, and it is warm,
and it grows. I have seen it branch in a night like frost on a pane. If you strike iron on iron down there, something
far below strikes back, once, and the timbers creak afterward as if something had leaned on them.

The last day, I put my ear to the ore. It took a long time, but there were words, very slow, one in a breath. *The
blood stood still, and grew heavy, and the weight of everything lay on it, and it hardened, and we woke in it.* Then,
as slowly, and very politely, it asked whether I had any infants at home.

I said I had none. I have two. I have not been down since, and I do not strike a nail at home after dark.
