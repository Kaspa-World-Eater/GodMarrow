# The Myriad

*The faith of the breath is called the Myriad, after the kami it keeps. Whoever still lives near the roads calls its
few keepers the Shrine Keepers, after the little wooden houses they leave along the roads, and does not stand near
those houses after dark. Their texts come from the day-book of the Well-Shrine in the Drowned Fen, the first of their
shrines, and from the last folders of paper figures in what is left of the reed camps.*

## The breath

*Taught to every novice on the night she is folded. Copied from the first leaves of the Well-Shrine's day-book, which
are so old the keepers turn them with a reed.*

Nothing you think stays in you. Think a thing long enough and it grows a kami, a kotodama, the size of the thing: a grudge
grows a grudge's god, a lie a lie's, a worry a worry's. It lives in your breath. Say the thing aloud, and the god goes
out of your mouth with the word and hangs in the air, looking for another mouth. Breathing in is how it gets in.

So hold your tongue. Breathe shallow among strangers. Cover your mouth when you speak of anything that matters. Never say
a grudge aloud, not even to a wall: said, it is loose, and someone will breathe it, and it will grow in them.

When the great one died, it breathed out its last, and everything it had ever thought went out on that one breath. That
is the wind. It has never turned. It is full of the dead god's kami, more than there are stars, and you have been
breathing them since the mourning you were born. Some of what you think is yours. Some of it is the dead god's. You will
never know which.

The kami are not kind. They fight, belief against belief, in the breath. Good. While they fight, they stay small.
Pray, if you pray, that they never agree.

## The shrines

*From the Well-Shrine's day-book, in the hand of an old keeper.*

A shrine is a house the size of a cradle on a post, with a paper door. There is nothing in it but what a keeper breathed
into it. When we take a breath from the sick, or the dying, or the grieving who ask us, we carry it in our chests as far
as we can bear, and then we breathe it into a shrine and seal the door, and the kami in it are shut in. We feed them.
Once a season we come back and whisper into the door something they can quarrel over, so that they never settle and
never grow.

The few who pass the shrines think they are holy. Let them. They bow to them, and leave what they can spare, and do
not open the doors, and that is all we need of them.

When a shrine is forgotten, and nobody whispers to it any more, its paper door begins to bulge outward at night, a
little at a time, as if what is inside were breathing out and could not stop. A keeper who finds one bulging must open
it, and breathe in whatever comes out, all of it, and carry it to another shrine. Novices are told the door bulges in
hunger. The old keepers say something else among themselves, and I write it here because I am old and it should be
written somewhere. They say that what is inside has started to agree with itself.

## The figure and the fan

*How the first keeper learned her work, as the last folders of the reed camps tell it. One of them, who could write,
wrote it down for the Well-Shrine.*

Breath gone foul is called miasma: breath so crowded with kami, so many of them rotten, that it sickens whoever
breathes it. It comes of a grudge kept past its season, a curse said into a well, a lie told over a grave and never
taken back. A house with miasma in it ails, and nothing ever grows right there.

The first keeper was a girl of the reed camps whose own camp had miasma in it, so badly the children coughed in their
sleep. She folded a paper figure by lamplight, with its arms left open, because a figure with its arms closed has
already refused. She had each of the sick breathe on it three times. On the third breath it leaned toward them a little,
as if it had been called, and she knew it had taken the foul breath in.

Then she did what no one had done before. She took the figure up to her own mouth and breathed it in, all of it, and
held it. It hurt. She tied a knot in a cord at her wrist so that she would know she was holding it. And she did not let
it go. She held it for a month, and learned that she could: she had stilled it. By the end of the month it had set in
her chest like steel. Then she took the fan from her belt and opened it one rib at
a time, because every rib is a road, and breathed it out, down the right rib, into the face of the man whose grudge it
had been. He did not get up.

The fan tells the breath where to go. Breath on its own only knows how to leave.

Every keeper since has done the same, with the same knotted cord at her wrist. When a novice comes to the Myriad, she is
folded: her birth-name is written into a paper figure, breathed on, and burned, and she takes another.

## The year the water gave it back

*From the Well-Shrine's day-book. The entries for the grey year are in many hands, and the count of steps differs
from entry to entry.*

The Well-Shrine stands at the one clean pool of the Drowned Fen. For generations, once a year, we kept the Cutting-Away.
On that night every keeper of the Myriad came to the pool and breathed out into the Fen everything she could not use:
every breath she had held that year and did not mean to spend. The Fen took it. The Fen took everything, and we started
the next year with empty lungs.

Then came the grey year, and the Fen gave it back.

The pool went grey. It went grey from the bottom up, in a single night, and the grey came up out of the water and lay
over the reed camps for days on end. Children who breathed it forgot how to sleep. The keepers who breathed it heard
voices in the reeds saying their own thoughts back to them, a little changed.

Below the pool, under the water, there is an older shrine, drowned: its bell, its pews, its altar, and a stair going
down to it. While the grey lay there, seven keepers went down the stair to find what had turned the Fen. They went down
one by one. One of them counted the steps going down as thirty-three, another as thirty-four. The one who came up last
counted thirty-six, and would not say what was on the extra steps.

Six of the seven came up horned. Their faces had gone long and hard, and the horns had begun at their brows, as they
come at a keeper's end, though none of the six was old. The seventh came up carrying a little girl in her arms. Nobody
had ever sent a girl down. Nobody knew her. She was not wet.

Some of us say the grey was our own generations of held breath, which the Fen could not hold any longer, and that
everything we ever gave it had been agreeing with itself down there in the dark. The Pale Order had been dredging the
Fen for its drowned bones, and some of us say the bones came up bent and turned the water. We have argued it for years,
and we will argue it for years more. The arguing is good. It keeps the breath in the day-book small.

Since then there has been no Cutting-Away. Each year now we keep the Small Cutting instead. Every keeper breathes the
year's waste into a little paper boat, and the boats are burned on a dry bank, and nobody breathes in until the ash is
cold. Under that year in the day-book, someone wrote: *Where does the year go now? It goes into us.*

## The ending of every keeper

*Spoken by the elder of the Well-Shrine to a keeper whose shrines had begun to bulge toward her. Another keeper, who was
in the room, wrote it down afterward.*

Every breath you hold leaves a little of itself behind. You know this. Count the knots on your cord, and then measure
the cord. It has grown longer than the knots you tied.

It goes slowly. The face goes long and hard first. Then the horns come in, slowly, the way a child's teeth come in, and
it does not hurt as much as you fear. Your hands stay your own for a long time.

One mourning you will stand up with your chest full of everything you ever took in, and you will not breathe out, and
you will walk down. Some of us walk down into the water. Some walk down the warm road under the Hide, and do not come
back up it. We call them kegare then, which is the word for the filth they carry, and we say it gently.

You will know your time is near when the shrines along the road begin to bulge toward you as you pass, the way they
bulge toward the dark. Yours have begun. We will fold a figure for you tonight, with its arms open.

## The breath drawn in

*The last leaf of the Well-Shrine's day-book is left blank but for this, written small at the bottom of the page.*

When the kami agree, the wind will turn.

Everything that went out will come back in: every breath, every word, every thought anyone ever let go of, drawn back
across the Hide in one long pull, and the breath of everyone breathing with it.

Hold.
