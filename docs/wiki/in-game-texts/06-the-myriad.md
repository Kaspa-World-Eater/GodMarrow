# The Myriad

*The faith of the breath is called the Myriad, after the small gods it keeps; the common folk call its people the
Shrine Keepers, because wherever a forgotten wayside shrine is set right, one of them has passed. Their texts come
from the day-book of the Well-Shrine in the Drowned Fen, the first of their shrines, and from the folders of paper
figures in the reed camps.*

## The breath

*Taught to every novice on the night she is folded. Copied from the first leaves of the Well-Shrine's day-book, which
are so old the keepers turn them with a reed.*

The great one breathed in, once, long. Every small god was inside that breath: the god of every stone and every ford,
of every bell and every spring, every crossing and every hearth. They were all warm in there together, and they were
one sound.

Then the great one began to breathe out. The world is the out-breath. Every stone and ford and bell you pass is a
small god riding it, on the way out.

On the morning the great one went still, every small god gave its name to the wind. They did it all at once, gladly,
the way you would throw your hat in the air. Each of them is proud of it still. That is why we bow to every small god
we pass, at every crossing and spring, and why we never ask its name. Asking would shame it. It gave its name away.

It matters less what breathes than that the breath is leaving. Everything is on its way out. Our work is to keep the
way clear.

## The guardian stones

*From the Well-Shrine's day-book, in the hand of a keeper of the stones.*

Every shrine has its guardian stone, set by the posts to face the road. When a shrine is forgotten, and nobody bows
there any more, its stone begins to turn. It turns toward the wall behind it, a finger's width a year, and no hand is
ever on it.

When a keeper comes to a forgotten shrine, she turns the stone back to face the road. It is heavy work. Then she
breathes on its face, close, until the dust on it stirs, and she ties a strip of white paper to the shrine's bell,
and bows, and goes on.

We teach the novices that the stone turns in grief, because nobody bows to it.

The keepers of the stones say something else among themselves, and I write it here because I am old and it should be
written somewhere. They say the stone turns toward the wall to listen to something on the far side.

## The figure and the fan

*How the first purifier learned her work, as the folders of the reed camps tell it. One of them, who could write,
wrote it down for the Well-Shrine.*

Breath that goes wrong is called miasma. Breath is meant to leave; that is all it knows how to do. But sometimes it
turns round in a narrow place and forgets the door, and goes round and round, and sours, and makes everything near it
sick. It comes from moving a boundary stone, or cursing a neighbour's well, or growing flesh without saying a word
over it. A house with wrong breath in it ails, and nothing ever grows right there.

The first purifier was a girl of the reed camps whose own camp had wrong breath in it, so badly the children coughed in
their sleep. She folded a paper figure by lamplight, with its arms left open, because a figure with its arms closed
has already refused. She had each of the sick breathe on it three times. On the third breath it leaned toward them a
little, as if it had been called, and she knew it had taken the wrong breath in.

Then she did what no one had done before. She took the figure up to her own mouth and breathed it in, all of it, and
held it. It hurt. She tied a knot in a cord at her wrist so that she would know she was holding it. Then she took the
fan from her belt and opened it one rib at a time, because every rib is a road. She breathed the wrong breath out,
down the right rib, at the man who had moved the boundary stone.

The fan tells the breath where to go. Breath on its own only knows how to leave.

Every purifier since has done the same, with the same knotted cord at her wrist. When a novice comes to the Myriad,
she is folded: her birth-name is written into a paper figure and burned, and she takes another.

## The year the water gave it back

*From the Well-Shrine's day-book. The entries for the grey year are in many hands, and the count of steps differs
from entry to entry.*

The Well-Shrine stands at the one clean pool of the Drowned Fen. For more than two hundred years, once a year, we
kept the Cutting-Away. On that night every purifier of the Myriad came to the pool and breathed out into the Fen all
the wrong breath she had held that year. The Fen took it. The Fen took everything, and the year went with it, and we
started the next one clean.

In the two hundred and fourth year of the Cuttings, the Fen gave it back.

The pool went grey. It went grey from the bottom up, in a single night, and the grey came up out of the water and lay
over the reed camps for a month. Children who breathed it forgot how to sleep. The keepers who breathed it heard
voices in the reeds speaking their own names back to them.

Below the pool, under the water, there is an older shrine, drowned: its bell, its pews, its altar, and a stair going
down to it. In the month of the grey, seven purifiers went down the stair to find what had turned the Fen. They went
down one by one. One of them counted the steps going down as thirty-three, another as thirty-four. The one who came up
last counted thirty-six, and would not say what was on the extra steps.

Six of the seven came up horned. Their faces had gone long and hard, and the horns had begun at their brows, as they
come at a purifier's end, though none of the six was old. The seventh came up carrying a girl of about nine in her
arms. Nobody had ever sent a girl down. Nobody knew her. She was not wet.

Some of us say the grey came from our own century of breath, which the Fen could not hold any longer. The Pale Order
had been dredging the Fen for its drowned bones, and some of us say the bones came up bent and turned the water. We
have argued it for years, and we will argue it for years more.

Since then there has been no Cutting-Away. Each year now we keep the Small Cutting instead. Every purifier breathes the
year's wrong breath into a little paper boat, and the boats are burned on a dry bank, and nobody speaks until the ash
is cold. Under that year in the day-book, someone wrote: *Where does the year go now? It goes into us.*

## The ending of every purifier

*Spoken by the elder of the Well-Shrine to a purifier whose stones had begun to turn toward her. Another keeper, who
was in the room, wrote it down afterward.*

Every breath you hold leaves a little of itself behind. You know this. Count the knots on your cord, and then measure
the cord. It has grown longer than the knots you tied.

It goes slowly. The face goes long and hard first. Then the horns come in, slowly, the way a child's teeth come in,
and it does not hurt as much as you fear. Your hands stay your own for a long time.

One morning you will stand up with your hands full of everything you ever took in, and you will walk down. Some of us
walk down into the water. Some walk down the warm road under the Hide, and do not come back up it.

We do not say *dies* of a purifier. We say she *ends*, and we say it gently. You will know your time is near when the
guardian stones begin to turn toward you, the way they turn toward the wall. Yours have begun. We will fold a figure
for you tonight, with its arms open.

## The last breath out

*The last leaf of the Well-Shrine's day-book is left blank but for this, written small at the bottom of the page.*

The out-breath will finish.

Every small god will settle like dust where it lies: on the stones, in the fords, in the mouths of the bells. Nothing
will be riding the breath any more, because there will be no more breath to ride.

Then a purifier who walked down into the water long before will come back up out of it, and she will breathe the
world in, once, as the last rite. The world will be folded like a paper figure, with its arms left open, and set down
on a step.

And nothing will come to breathe on it.
