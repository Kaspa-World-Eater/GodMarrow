# The Bleeding Maiden

*The faith of the Bleeding Maiden walks in processions. Its chapters stand along open wounds in the world, and each
keeps its Rule chained to the wall of its crypt. These texts come from the Rule, from what was chalked and cut inside
the crypts, and from what outsiders saw.*

## The wound

*The opening of the Rule, read aloud at the front of every procession on the Day of the Measure. Copied from the
chained book of a chapter on the salt road.*

In the beginning there was a wound. It was a wound in nothing, for there was nothing yet to be wounded. It opened,
and it did not close.

What came out of it was flesh. The flesh grew, because nothing told it to stop. It grew into the hills and the mires
and the warm ground, and into every living thing, and it is growing still.

The opening is the Bleeding Maiden. She is the first gift, the side laid open so that something could come out. The
growing is the Mother, whose old name is not said lightly.

The Mother has no mind. She does not choose and she does not refuse. She takes what she is given and makes from it,
and what she makes has no head unless someone tells her what it is for. So we give our blood back into the wound, a
measured portion, once a year, and we say the Words over it as it pours. The Words tell her whose blood it is and
what it is for. Blood given with the Words carries the giver's name down into her, and in time it comes back, as a saint
on the crypt stair. Blood given without the Words is given to no one.

Blood given is not blood taken.

What was wounded, the Rule does not say. Of the god's fall, which the lowlands talk about so much, the processions
remember nothing at all. The cup came down to us from somewhere, and we count our years from the first cup filled.

## The Day of the Measure

*Chalked inside the crypt door of a chapter, many times over, in many hands: "she asked a little more." Under the
chalk, a hood of that chapter wrote this account of the Measure on the wall.*

Once a year, on the Day of the Measure, the procession goes out from the chapter door to the wound. The Elder walks
at the front. We walk behind, in our hoods, each of us known by our place in the line: the ninth hood, the
twenty-ninth hood. When one of us goes into the wall, everyone behind moves up a place.

The bloodletter walks at the back. She carries the small blade, and she opens each of us in turn and keeps the tally
of what each has given. By the oldest rule, the one who opens others never opens herself. Her sleeves stay down and
her arms stay dry, for life. The reason is told to each bloodletter on the night she takes up the blade, and to
nobody else.

We walk the way the processions have always walked: the sway, the stop, the sway. The scourge falls nine strokes to a
verse. At the wound, the Elder fills the tin cup to its line from what we have given, and pours it from the lip of
the cup to the lip of the wound, and says the Words. Blood let on any other day of the year is spilled, and given to no one.
Then we walk home to the chapter door. A procession goes out from its door and comes home to it. It must have
somewhere to go.

On the eve of every Measure, the Elder raises the line in the cup by a thumbnail. Nobody remembers a year when she
did not. That is what the chalk on the crypt door says, in all those hands, one under another: *she asked a little
more*.

There have always been some among us who give more than the Measure. They open their own veins on other days and say
the Words forward as they bleed, and let the Mother come through them. They wear their penances as armour, nails and
thorns and the cilice, because a penance is only blood given slowly. We revere them, and we are afraid of them, and
the lowland children have a rhyme about them, about the Tall Red Hat.

## The unfinished face

*Told by the carvers who make the images for the chapters. A carver's apprentice wrote it down on the back of a
pattern.*

Every chapter has a niche over its crypt stair, and in every niche stands the Maiden. Her face is never finished. It
is left rough from the axe, the first cuts of a face and nothing more.

The carvers say the first carver of the first niche worked on her for a year, and every time he set his chisel to
her face it slipped. In the end he put the chisel down and left her as she was. When the Elder asked him why, he said
that she did not want a face, because a face can be named, and a thing that can be named can be called.

On the fast-day, every penitent kisses the rough face in the niche, and afterward says nothing about what they saw
there. Each of us has seen something. We do not compare.

## The saints on the stair

*A pilgrim's account of a night spent in a chapter of the processions, written the next morning at an inn on the salt
road.*

They warned me before I went down to sleep in the guest room. At night, they said, the saints come down the crypt
stair. From wherever they come from, they come down the stair and go into the carved images in the crypt, and
while they are down, they walk. The penitents go down after them two by two, with candles. If anyone speaks a name while the
saints are down, any name at all, a penitent must answer to it, and must answer kindly.

I did not believe them. I lay in the guest room with the door open a crack, and late in the night I heard footsteps on
the stair, slow and heavy, more of them than there were penitents in that chapter, and then candles going down after
them.

Then I heard a voice in the crypt say my mother's name. My mother has been in the ground nine years.

And a penitent's voice answered, *Yes, I'm here*, very gently, the way you would answer a child who has woken in the
night.

I left before it was light. I left a copper on the guest room's sill, I do not know for whom.

## The year the wound hatched

*Told by a salt-carrier who passed many times, before and after, through a chapter of the processions built along a
great wound in the ground. It was written down at its daughter chapter, which still sets out the old chapter's
tithe-cup at its closed gate once a year.*

The chapter was built down both lips of a long wound in the ground, as long as a morning's walk. Forty hoods
walked in its procession, and its gutters ran dark. Some people say they ran the colour of old brown ink, and that
anyone who calls them red has never been there. Others say red.

In its last year the wound would not take the blood. It had crusted over from one end to the other, a
crust like a scab, thick enough to walk on, warm underfoot. The Elder raised the line, and raised it again. On the
Day of the Measure the hoods broke the crust with picks so they could pour, and the Measure ran down into the crack
and the crust closed over it again before the cup was empty.

On the fast-day after, the crust split along its whole length in the night. What came out of the wound came out in
the hoods' robes. The robes had been hanging on their pegs in the crypt, as robes do between processions, and in the
morning they were walking: children, or the shapes of children, with nothing behind their eyes, forty of them in the
forty robes, in the order of the procession. At the front walked something tall, sewn together out of more than one
thing, and on its back it carried the Maiden out of the niche. I saw her. She had a face now.

The forty hoods were gone, and nobody found them.

The Rule in the crypt has new clauses since that night. They are written in brown ink, in a hand that leans the other
way. One says *The measure is all of it.* One says *The niche shall hold a face.* One says *The procession goes out,
and goes out, and goes out. It needs no door.* The last says only *She hears.*

The chapter at the wound is empty. The procession has never come home to it. Every year, on the Day of the Measure,
the daughter chapter sets the old chapter's tithe-cup out at its own closed gate, filled to the line, and in the night
someone in a hood takes it in.

## The filling

*The last page of the Rule, read once a year at the end of the Measure, in a whisper.*

The flesh will fill every hollow. It will fill every wound and every mouth, every grave and every room, and the gap
between one word and the next. When nothing is hollow anywhere, there will be no *then*. That will be the Mother made
whole.

The chapters keep the name of every knight of the Pale Order in their litanies, and say them all, every year, so that
she will know whom to take first when the time comes.

And the lowland children, who know nothing of the Rule, sing the last verse of their rhyme at the very end, when the
game is over and somebody has to be caught:

> *Who will open the Tall Red Hat?*
> *Mother will.*
