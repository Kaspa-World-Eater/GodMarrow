# The Gilded Peak

*The Gilded Peak was a monastery cut into a mountain of bone, which its monks said was the god's knee broken up
through the Hide. It listened to the Silence. It fell in the year 1098 of the common count. What is left of its
teaching came down the mountain with the few who lived, in the letters they carried, and in what the lowlands
remember of the monks who came down to them at dusk with their bowls.*

## The Mouth

*Taught to novices on the stair, a step at a time. The youngest novice of the Peak, called Sō, wrote it down years
after the fall, in the valley below the mountain, from memory.*

Before anything, there was the Silence. It lacked nothing. Everything that could ever be meant was already in it,
the way everything a sentence is for is already in the pause before it is spoken.

Then, in the Silence, a mouth opened, and the opening was the god. It drew one breath in, and it held it. Everything
that has ever been was inside that one held breath: the light, the ground, the dead, the lowlands, the mountain, you
and me on this stair. It was a small thing. The Silence it opened in did not notice it.

That is creation, my teacher said. It is a pause held too long.

For an age the god held its space open, and held us inside that space, and it grew tired. A held breath always grows
tired. When it could hold no longer, the Silence came back into the place where it had always been. The lowlands call
that the god's death, and they are afraid of it. The Peak called it rest. The god grew tired, and the Silence came in
to let it lie down.

My teacher said one more thing about it, every time, on the same step, and so I will say it here. Everything on the
Hide is a wound that has not yet been allowed to close. We are wounds too. We have tried to be clean ones.

## The two empty hands

*Painted inside the lid of a monk's bowl. The bowl was found in a lowland house below the mountain, where the woman who
owned it could not read and had it read to her.*

The first of us was a bell-caster. He struck a new bell to hear its note, and in the moment after the note died away
he heard what was behind it. He put down his hammer, climbed the mountain, sat down at the top, and did not speak
again for nine years. When he did speak, he taught this.

The Silence has two hands, and both of them are empty. One hand is open. It is Radiance: the light at noon that
leaves no shadow, which takes things away by showing all of them at once. The other hand is closed. It is Absence:
the dark, which takes things away by showing none of them. Both hands are empty, and both are holy.

So we gilded. Gold is Radiance made solid. A thing covered in gold shines so that it is looked at and not seen, and
on the Peak, to be looked at and not seen was holiness. We laid gold leaf on every seventh step of the stair, on the
faces of our dead, and on the masters who were ready.

And we chanted with one breath: one syllable, then a silence as long as the syllable. The syllable is a fence, the
bell-caster taught, and the silence is the field it marks. The lowlands believed our chant kept the breath-sickness
off the mountain's slopes. It may have. Something did.

No novice listens to a silence alone. There is always a shoulder against yours. A teacher walks one step behind and
below his novice on the stair, to catch the fall.

## The bowl

*Told in the lowlands below the mountain, where the monks came down with their bowls at dusk. A copyist from
the plains wrote it down from an old woman who remembered the bowls.*

At dusk the monks came down the mountain in fours, with small brass bells, and each of them carried an empty bowl.
They stopped at every door, and they said the same thing at every door: *Put a name in it.*

You could whisper anything into the bowl. People whispered the names of what they wanted gone: a grief, a debt, a
fear, a man. The monk would bow and walk on, and you would forget it. In the morning you would not remember what you
had whispered. You would only feel lighter, and not know why.

The monks carried the names up the mountain, and at noon the abbot gave them to the open hand. He stood on the east
stone and tipped each bowl out over its lip, into the light, and the names went wherever names go.

There was a widow among us whose husband had died in the spring, and she could not bear it. She put his name
in the bowl. In the morning she did not know him. She did not know she had ever had a husband. But every fast-day
for the rest of her life she walked out to the same grave in the barrow field and sat beside it until dark, and when
anyone asked whose grave it was, she said she did not know, and that her feet knew the way.

The forgetting had no edge. That is what frightened people, in the end. You could not put in only the part of a
thing you wanted gone. And the bowls grew heavier every year, though they were always empty when the monks came down.
Nobody knows where the names went: whether they are forgotten, or only kept somewhere none of us can reach.

## The tenth night

*Written by Sō, the youngest novice of the Peak, years after the fall, in the valley below the mountain, where he
washes the dead.*

In the spring of the year the Peak fell, the monks began to die in their rooms at night. They died one or two a
night, and in the morning there was nothing in the room but a little black glass and a little dust. For nine days the
abbot counted them. On the ninth day there were nine of us left who were not gilded.

The gilded masters had been dying differently, if it was dying. The oldest of them were gilded while still breathing
and set in the niches of the inner sanctum, a hundred and eight niches, the last on the left always empty and kept.
For years before the end, dark tracks had run down from the gilded masters' eyes every night, and every morning we
wiped them away, and the pilgrims said the masters wept for the living.

On the ninth evening the abbot wrote a letter and gave it to me. I was the youngest and the quickest down the old
water-cut. He told me to take the letter down to the valley and not to look back. I did not look back.

That night was the tenth night. From below we heard the great bell over the courtyard ring, a long time, though
it had never once rung where it hung; it was only ever heard ringing down in the valley. Nobody in the valley slept.

In the morning I went up. The courtyard was black glass, smooth and whole from wall to wall, and when I put my hand on
it, it was warm. It is still warm, at noon and at midnight. There was nobody in the rooms, and nothing.

The doors of the inner sanctum were open. Who opened them, I do not know; I saw nobody go up. Others tell it
differently. A traveller swears a monk handed him a lantern on the stair and went up past him. The beggar who sits at
our door says he walked out of the empty niche himself, and laughs when he says it. The saints were still in
their niches, gilded, a hundred and seven of them, and the last on the left was empty as it had always been. The dust
in front of it had footprints in it, going out.

Over the sanctum door someone had cut words into the stone in the night: *THEY WERE NOT WEEPING FOR US.* A traveller
who came later told me it reads *UNDER THE GOLD THEY WERE STILL LOOKING*. I have read it myself many times since. I
will not say which it is.

## The Empty Hand

*Told in the lowlands, and written down by a copyist.*

Long before the name meant any one of them, the lowlands called every monk of the Peak an Empty Hand, because they
came down the mountain carrying nothing but a bowl, and the bowl was always empty.

Since the fall, the name means something else. An Empty Hand is any monk of the Peak who has heard the space between
words and now carries nothing at all. They still walk the roads. What one of them strikes does not leave a body. It
leaves smooth black glass, or a little dust, or nothing, as if it had never been there. A black flame follows each of
them, a little behind, and no one has ever seen it give heat.

Every faith fears them. The Pale Order says the Peak leaves glass where it should leave bone. The processions of the
Bleeding Maiden hate the Silence more than they ever hated the Peak. The circles of the Veiled Crone bar their doors to
anyone who speaks its name, and the Shrine Keepers send purifiers to hunt them. The Empty Hands do not seem to mind.
They do not seem to mind anything.

## The pause completed

*The last office of the Peak, as the monks who were below the mountain when it fell still keep it. A lowland reeve
heard one of them say it over a grave and wrote it down that night.*

The held breath will be let go.

What remains will be the Silence the breath was held in, which held the meaning all along. Nothing will be
destroyed. The sentence will be finished, and a finished sentence has been said; it does not need to be said again.

We who were below the mountain when it fell keep the last office for those who were above. When the end comes, we
will be the empty space outside it. We will hold nothing, so that nothing is kept wrongly.
