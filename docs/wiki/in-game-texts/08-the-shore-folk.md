# The Shore Folk

*The people of the village above the black sand left little writing. What survives was painted inside canoes, carved
round the posts that face the Red Water, cut into the temple's stones, or told by their keepers to the few outsiders
who reached them and written down by those outsiders afterward.*

## The Red Water

*A keeper's telling, given to the children on the night they were first taken down to the black sand. A lowland
trader who wintered with the shore folk wrote it down; his copy was found among his things at an inn on the forest's
edge.*

Sit where you can see it. Don't go nearer. This is near enough.

Before there were people, and before there were trees, there was the Fallen One. It came down out of somewhere
higher than the sky and lay down, and the land is its body. Put your hand on the ground in the forest, under the
moss: it is warm. That is the Fallen One, still warm.

The forest grew out of its back. You have seen how young cedars grow out of an old trunk that fell before your
grandmother's grandmother was born. They stand in a row along it, drinking from it and holding it in their roots.
That is how the great trees stand on the Fallen One, and that is why they are so tall. They have been drinking from
it longer than anything has been counted.

But the Fallen One did not lie down whole. As it fell, it was struck, or it struck something on the way down. The
keepers have never agreed on which, and the oldest of them would not say. It came down with a wound in its side, the
side that faces away from the trees, and it bled. Blood runs downhill. It ran past the edge of the trees and past the
edge of the land, and it pooled at the low edge of everything, and it is lying there still. That is the Red Water.
Look at it. All of that is blood.

It has no other side. In the time of the first keepers, when the people still made many canoes, seven canoes went
out on the Red to find where it ended. They paddled toward the place where the grey comes down to meet the red. One
canoe came back at the end of the summer, with three still living in it. Their paddles were worn to stubs, and they
were red to the shoulders, and it never washed off. The youngest of them lived to be old, and all her life she said
the same two things: that there was nothing out there but more of it, and that the farther they went, the warmer it
grew. The other six canoes never came back. Since then nobody has put a canoe on the Red. The canoes you see on the
sand are older than that, and nobody touches them.

Watch the edge. It comes up the sand and goes back. Twice a day it comes up, and twice a day it goes back down, and
it always has. That is the Fallen One's heart. It is still beating, very slowly, because it has not finished dying.
Lie down at the water's edge on some still night and put your ear to the wet sand, and you will hear it. Everyone
does that once. Nobody does it twice.

The sand is black because the blood burns what it touches. It does not burn like fire. It burns slowly, over more
years than there are. Where the Red lies against the Fallen One's edge, it has burned the edge black, and the water
has ground the black edge fine. That is the sand you are sitting on.

In the old days we gave our dead to the Red. We laid them in canoes with their faces to the sky, and when the water
was going back down we pushed them out on it, so that the heart would carry them back into the Fallen One's blood and
they would be part of it again. We did that for longer than anyone remembers.

Then one winter the dead came back. They came up out of the Red at night, red to the eyes, and climbed the black
sand, and stood at the edge of the trees looking up at the village. The next night there were more. They did not
come in. They stood there until the grey came, then went back down into the water, and every night after that they
came up again, until the keepers understood what they wanted, which was to come home.

That was when we began to burn our dead. We give them to the fire on the night they die, before they are cold,
every one, so that the Red can never have them. And we give nothing else to the Red: no blood, no tears, no word
spoken over it. Whatever goes into the Red comes back.

That is why we live in the trees and not on the sand. That is why the wall faces the water. And that is why we burn
the people we love.

Now get up. Don't turn your back on it until you are in the trees.

## The stair

*Carved round the eight boundary stones of the temple, one part to a stone, so that the whole of it can only be read
by walking once round the temple. Rain has taken the edges off the letters on the water side.*

Before the people came to the trees, they lived down in the low wet country, where the Long One lay. He was a god,
and he was long, longer than a day's walk; nobody ever saw both his ends at once. He lay in the black water with his
back above it, and the people walked on his back from one dry place to another. He let them, because they fed him.

The stones say plainly what they fed him, and so will I. Once a year, at the place where he coiled round on himself,
the keepers of that time opened the throats of the chosen over a pit of black stone. The blood ran down the gutters
into the dark, and the Long One drank. In return the water stayed where it was, and the people's road stayed above
it.

There was a keeper whose daughter was chosen, and she would not give her. On the night of the giving she took the
girl, and everyone who would follow, and walked out along the Long One's back away from the pit, toward the high
ground. They did not stop walking until they were among the great trees, on dry ground, with the Long One's back far
behind them.

He did not follow; the stones say he could not leave the water. But the keeper knew he would remember them. So she
built a house for the keepers on a high platform, and up to its door she built a stair, and on both sides of the
stair she carved the Long One, his body running down the rails and his heads rearing at the foot, as if he were
climbing to the door. That way, she said, he would see himself already at our door and believe he was still being
kept, and he would not come looking.

She set the rule that the stair is swept every morning, every step, before anything else is done, so that the Long
One carved on it is kept clean and knows he is minded. The keepers swept it every morning after her, longer than
anyone can count.

Down in the low wet country, with no one to feed him, the Long One starved. A god that nobody feeds does not lie
down quietly. It breaks apart, and what it breaks into is worse than what it was. The bog is what is left of him. His
back is the road the bog folk walk now, his bones lie everywhere in the black water, and nobody down there remembers
his name.

The last stone says: *Sweep the stair. He is not as dead as the bog folk think.*

## The houses of the trees

*Told by the shore folk to their children. A purifier of the Myriad who visited them wrote it down in her day-book,
and added a note of her own, because she did not agree.*

When the forest grew out of the Fallen One's back, something came up with every great tree: its spirit, the part of
the tree that knows it is a tree. The spirits live high up in the crowns, where nobody goes. They are not unkind, but
they are curious, and they are lonely up there in the dark. A spirit with nowhere to live will come down and live in
the nearest warm thing, and the nearest warm thing is usually a person, and usually a child.

So when the people first came into the trees, the first keeper carved a post for the foot of every great tree near
the village. On top of each post she set a little house with a roof like her own house and a door the size of a
hand, so that the tree's spirit would have a house of its own and stay in it. Every morning the people hung a flower
on each house, and every evening they lit a light in each door, so that the spirits would know they were thought
of.

There was a family who cut down a great tree to make a canoe and forgot to move its house first. The house fell with
the tree and broke, and the spirit had nowhere to live. That night it came down into the family's youngest child.
The child stopped speaking. She did nothing all day but look up into the crowns, and at night she climbed, higher
than any child should be able to, and in the morning they would find her asleep in the branches, sixty spans up,
with her eyes open. The keepers built a new house for the spirit and carried the child to it and waited all night,
and in the morning the spirit had gone into its house, and the child came down. She never climbed again, but she
never stopped looking up, either.

The purifier's note: *The Myriad teaches that every tree and stone has its small god, that each small god gave its
name to the wind when the great one went still, and that all of them are riding the last breath out of the world.
These people have the right of it in their way, but they have built houses to keep the small gods in, and a small god
kept in a house cannot leave when the breath goes out. I did not tell them so. They have enough to fear.*

## The teacher's dance

*Woven into the border of a fighter's cape of cedar bark, in a pattern the keepers could read and outsiders cannot.
This is a keeper's reading of it, as the lowland trader wrote it down.*

When the dead first came up out of the Red, the people had no fighters. They were carvers and canoe-makers, and they
knew the adze and the knife and nothing else. On the first night the dead came, a carver went down to the sand with
her adze to drive them back.

Before she went, she stopped at the top of the path and danced. She did it slowly, with her eyes closed: every
motion her teacher had taught her, the way to hold the adze, the way to stand to the wood, the way to lean into a
cut. She did it so that if she died on the sand, her teacher, who was dead and had gone to the fire, would know her
by the way she moved, and would come for her before the Red could.

She came back up the path at dawn with her adze worn blunt.

After that, every fighter of the shore folk learned a teacher's dance and danced it before going down to the sand or
up on the wall. No two dances are alike, because no two teachers are. The drum and the pipe play while they dance.
When a fighter dies, the drum plays their dance once more, slowly, so their teacher knows them, and then the fighter
goes to the fire.

## When the tide stops

*Cut round the top of the memorial pole on the headland, under its little roof. The faces on the pole look out to
the Red; so does this.*

One day the Red will go back down the sand and not come up again. It will go down past the rocks and past the
stacks, farther than anyone has ever seen the sand go, and it will keep going, back up into the Fallen One's side,
and the wound will close.

Some keepers say that then the Fallen One will get up. The forest will slide off its back like rain off a roof, all
the great trees at once, and the spirits will have nowhere left to live but in us. And everything the Red ever kept
will be standing on the dry black sand, looking up.

Other keepers say the Fallen One will not get up. They say the tide stopping only means it has finished dying at
last. Then no more dead will come up out of the water, because there will be no water. The dead will lie down for
good, and the living will have nothing left to burn. The fire will go out, and that will be the end, and it will be
quiet.

Which keeper you believed depended on which family you were born into. Both families carved their telling on this
pole, and both faced it out to the Red, so that the Fallen One would know they were watching.
