# The Veiled Crone

*The circles of the Veiled Crone keep the soul. They meet in round lodges built where, they say, her threads come up
through the skin of the world, and they sit in rings from dusk until the grey hour saying her names. Their texts are
kept in the lodges and copied by their readers. Some came out with the Unveiled, who left.*

## The first thread

*Said by the keeper of a circle when she lights the one candle, before the names begin. Copied from a reader's book
kept in one of the lodges.*

Before her, everything was like a dry wall in a shut room. It was dry in every season, it did not move, and nothing
held it.

Then she came, and she drew the first thread out of nothing, and she tied the first knot, and the knot was the world.
Every life is a length of that one thread. She spins it out of the knot, and she weaves it into her cloth, and when a
life is over, its thread goes through the eye of her loom and into the cloth for good. Nothing is lost. It is only
woven in.

She has many names, and we say them all, because a name said is a thread held. We call her the Veiled Crone,
Weaver, Grandmother Spindle, the Knot and the Tangle. She has four faces. She is the Spinner, who draws the thread
out. She is the Weaver, who sets it in the cloth. She is the Unraveller, who takes back what was woven wrong. And she
is the Hollow, the eye of the loom that every thread goes through at the end. The Hollow's name is never spoken aloud. We
hum it, with the mouth shut.

When the god of the lowlands died, the threads of the world went slack. The lowlands tell of a murder, or an
unsaying. We tell it more simply: the hand at the treadle tires. The circles were set to tighten the threads again,
and that is what remembering is for. A thread that is remembered stays taut.

The keepers do not say the next part in front of novices, and the novices always learn it anyway. Someone had tied a
knot before she began.

## The circle of names

*From a letter written by a girl to her mother on the morning after her first night in a circle. Her mother kept it
all her life.*

They sat us in a ring on the floor of the round lodge, forty-one of us, with the eldest in the middle. The eldest
wore the coat of glass. It is an iron coat sewn all over with small hollowed mirrors, and every eldest before her
sewed more of them on, until it weighs what a second body weighs. She could not stand up in it without two of the
menders lifting her. She did not need to stand.

At dusk the keeper lit one candle and said the first name. The woman beside her said it after her, and the man
beside her, and so on round the ring, mouth to mouth, until it came back to the keeper from the far side. Then the
keeper said the next name. We did that all night, every name of hers, again and again, until the grey came.

I do not know how to tell you what happened, Mother. Somewhere in the middle of the night I stopped being able to
tell the names from myself. I was not saying them any more. They were going through me, the way a thread goes through
a needle, and I was the needle, and I was also the thread. When the grey came and the keeper put the candle out, I
remembered my own name again, and it felt like a coat I had taken off and could not get comfortable in.

The eldest says that is what remembering is. She says the soul and the remembering are one thread, and that we
spend our lives forgetting it. They say that where the greatest lodge stood, you can see the threads in the lake ice
on a still night, running under the surface toward the shore. I would like to see that.

## The law of the mirror

*Told in every circle as the reason for the oldest law. A grinder of glass, who made the hollowed
mirrors for the lodges, wrote it down.*

A soul cannot pass its own reflection. Every circle holds this, Covered and Unveiled alike, and every circle tells
the same story of how it was learned.

A reader's son died young. On the way out of the house his soul crossed the yard, and in the yard there was a pail of
rainwater, and the soul looked into the pail and saw its own face, and stopped. It did not go on. It stood in the yard
looking down into the pail, all that night and all the next day, and the reader could see it from her window. It
stood there through the winter. In the spring she could not bear it any longer. She took a piece of grey felt and laid
it over the pail, and set a stone on each corner so the wind would not lift it, and when she looked again the yard
was empty. Her son had gone on, through the eye of the loom, into the cloth.

So when anyone dies, we cover every mirror in the house, every pail, every window and every still water, with grey
felt and a stone on each corner, until the dead have gone through. A soul that meets its own face stops to look.

Some things show nothing in a mirror at all. The servants of the Silence have no face for a glass to give back. No
lodge has ever let one past its door alive.

## The loose winter

*From a lodge's day-book, kept that winter in several hands. The last hand shakes.*

In the winter of the year 1106, the dead did not go through.

We covered the mirrors as we always have. The felt was laid right, and the stones were on the corners. But the dead
came back up the valley anyway, every night for forty nights. They came to the doors of the houses they had died in
and stood there and asked the same thing, over and over, quietly, of whoever opened the door: *Which way?* Nobody
could tell them. They went from door to door. By the end of the winter they were at the lodge doors too.

On the thirtieth night the eldest of our circle stood up in the coat of glass, without the menders, which she had
not done in years. She said: *There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes.* Then she sat down, and she
would not say anything else, and in the morning she was dead, and her soul did not go through either.

After that, some of the circles took the felt off. They let the dead look at their own faces in the glass, and the
dead stopped there, and were still, and stayed. Those circles kept them, held in the glass, and carried them. The
circles that did this call themselves the Unveiled. The ones who would not call themselves the Covered, and say the
Unveiled are thieves of the dead.

The coat of glass did not go into the ground with our eldest. It stood up on its own in the lodge, full of the kept,
and it has not fallen down since.

Since that winter the Covered send their dead through faster and more fiercely than before, and they burn any glass
they find uncovered. "The loom is there," they say, "and saying so is the work." The Unveiled walk the roads with
needles and hollowed mirrors, and the kept go round them in the dark like small cold lights. Widows pay the Unveiled
for a little more time with the dead, and the Unveiled take the copper.

## The reading-room

*The last page of a circle's reading-room book, in the scribe's hand. The page was found on the reading-stand by the
next circle to come, the ink gone pale.*

There are seven of us in the circle tonight, and the keeper has lit the candle, and we have begun the names. I am
writing because the keeper told me to write down whatever happened tonight, whatever it was.

Something is standing by the east wall. It came in without the door opening. It has no face that I can see, and in the
glass on the reading-stand it shows nothing at all. The candle's light is leaning toward it, all of it, the way a
flame leans in a draught, though there is no draught.

Brother Hal says it was here before. He says the Crone held this room open inside it, for as long as there have been
rooms, and that now the holding is over, and it has come back to stand where it always stood. I do not know how he
knows this. He is not afraid. I am afraid.

The ink is going white where I write, near the edge of the page nearest the wall. I can hear us breathing, seven of
us, and I can hear a breath under ours, drawn in and not let out.

## The still winter

*Said at the Folding, at midwinter, when the circles knit one row into the year's cloth for every death of the year,
and the cloth is folded and put away. A reader's book.*

We knit one row for every death. A death-row has no knot in it, because the thread has gone through. When the year's
rows are done, we fold the cloth and lay it with the others, and we say this over it.

One winter, every thread that ever went through the eye will come back up its valley at once. Every soul that ever
died will stand at its own door, in the snow, and ask which way.

The Unveiled say that then the dead will stop at their own faces, at last, and be home. The Covered say the loom will
finish its cloth and fold it, as we fold ours tonight.

Either way, nothing will move again. The candle will stop burning down.
