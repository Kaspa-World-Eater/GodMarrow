# The Tithed

*The common people of the lowlands call themselves the Tithed. They keep few books. Their myths are told at banked
fires, by mothers and grandmothers, and some were written down by the copyists, who heard them at many
fires and set down what the tellers agreed on.*

## The Kneeling

*Told by the grandmothers of the lowlands on Kneel-Night. A copyist heard it at nine fires and wrote down
what the nine agreed on.*

We were inside something warm, and we were kneeling, and we were singing. That is where we start, because that is as
far back as anyone remembers.

It was not like anywhere now. There was no night there. The light lay evenly over everything and came from nowhere,
and the air smelled of being held. The grandmothers call it the Held Space, and they say we were there a long time,
though nobody can say how long, because nothing there was ever counted.

We were not the first ones in it. Others were there long before us. There were singers somewhere behind the walls
who never showed themselves, and builders with long hands who cut stairs into the walls and never spoke to us. High
up in the dark sat a court of white faces that watched what we thought. None of them liked us much. We sang anyway.

The god we knelt inside was three things at once: a soul to weave its fates on, a bone to hold it up, and flesh to
fill it out. The children say it like this:

> *Soul for the thread and Bone for the frame,*
> *Flesh for the filling, and none for the name.*

What its own name was, nobody kept. And near the end, the grandmothers say, the god set something down among us,
small and folded, the way a woman banks a coal in ash so there will be fire in the morning. They say it lies nearest
the top in a newborn, where the head is still soft, and that is why you hold a newborn's head in your hand and not
anywhere else.

Then it came. We do not say its name. It pressed against the outside of the warm place the way cold presses at a tent.
It took the little names first: the name of the smallest finger, the name of one eyelash, the name of the hour before
morning. We sang louder. It took more. And one night, though there was no night there, the god forgot how to be three
things at once. The warmth went out of the walls, and the light went out with it, and everything we were kneeling inside
was dead.

We could not see. We took hold of each other's belts in the dark, so that nobody would be lost, and we stood up and
walked. We walked through soft places, and then hard places, and then places that pulsed under our feet. We walked a
long way, toward a door that was warm and wet and opened and shut, and when it opened we went through it. That was
the mouth. We came out on the god's last breath, into a grey light, and fell on the ash of the Cheek, and kissed it,
because it was ground.

Children always ask whether everybody came out. The first time a grandmother tells it, she says all of them. The
second time, she says most of them.

That is why, once a year, on the night the ash is wettest, we kneel round a banked fire from dark until the grey
comes, without singing. When the grey comes we stand up at once and walk one ring round the fire holding each
other's belts. That is why we knot our belts at the back, so that whoever walks behind us has something to hold.

## The fourth note

*Written by a copyist about something that happened on the Moor in his grandfather's time. He heard it
from three people. They agreed on everything but the young man's name.*

The song we sang inside the god had four notes. We keep three of them, low, each going down from the one before.
The fourth goes up, the way a voice goes up at the end of a question, and nobody sings it. Every child is told never
to sing the fourth note, and never to sing at all on the Moor at night. Most children are not told why.

There was a young man who tended the lanterns on the Moor road, and one night he was out late with a lantern that
would not stay lit. To keep himself brave in the dark he sang the three notes, softly, over and over. Then, because
nobody had ever told him why not, he sang the fourth.

Out on the ash, something sang it back.

He told his mother afterward that it was not an echo. It came from many places at once, low and far off, a great
many voices, and they did not stop at the fourth note. They went on into a fifth, and a sixth, and a line of the song
that nobody alive had ever heard, and the voices were coming nearer while they sang it. Then he saw them at the edge
of his lantern's light. They were coming toward him on their knees across the ash, with their faces lifted and their
mouths open, singing.

He ran. He left the lantern where it was and ran all the way to the stones at the crossroads, and he lived to be old,
and he never sang anything again, not even at his children's namings.

In the morning his father went out to fetch the lantern. It was still burning where he had dropped it. All round it
in the ash, in a ring, were the marks of knees, close together, every one of them facing in.

That is why we do not sing the fourth note. The ones who did not come out still know the tune, and they want the rest
of the song.

## The salt on the sill

*Told by a mother to her children, as it has been told in her family for longer than they know. A brother of the
Pale Order, quartered in a lowland house one winter, asked about the salt on the sill and wrote down her answer.*

When we went in to kneel, we carried salt with us. Nobody knows where we got it, because we do not know where we were
before we went in. But we had it, and we carried it in, and the god never had any salt of its own.

Everything that comes up out of the god now is made of the god. We are the only things that are not. That is why they
want us. Some of them want to take us back inside. Some of them want to put us on the way you put on a coat, and kneel
the way we knelt, and be us. They are homesick. They do not hate us. That would be simpler.

The first winter on the Cheek, a child was born in the open, the first born outside the god. On the third night the
things came to the shelter where the mother lay with her child, and they stood round it in the dark and breathed. The
mother had nothing but the little salt she had carried out. She laid it in a line across the door, and along the
gaps in the wall, and in a ring round the child, and the things stood outside the line all night and did not cross
it. In the morning they were gone.

So we put salt on the doorstep, and on the sill, and round the bed where a child sleeps. We hang a lantern at the
head of every cradle. And a mother gives her child its true name in her own mouth, and says it aloud only once, by
the hearth, on the day the child first walks. After that it stays behind the child's teeth for life. That Which Cannot
Be Named took the god apart one name at a time, and the dead call the living by their names. A name that is never said
cannot be called and cannot be taken.

## The lantern that sighs

*A letter from the tender of the lantern round to the copyists, who kept it with their books. The
tender had been asked how the lanterns came to be.*

No lantern on any road burns oil. You know that much. Every one of them is lit with a soul that chose to be kept
rather than go on down with the last breath: a pilgrim who fell on the road, a mother from the crypt niches, somebody
nobody remembers. The soul is the flame.

The first who kept one were the Wick-Binders. They were a guild, long ago, and the faiths hunted them out for it.
Nobody has been a Wick-Binder for longer than anyone can count, but every lantern on every road is theirs still.

A kept soul chooses whom it follows. It cannot be struck, and it will not be ordered. It goes where you go, a little
behind and a little to one side, and when you fall it carries you back to the lantern-stones and stands you up again.
Each time, it keeps a little of you. That is what it burns. It might be a way of whistling, or the name of a sister,
or the second verse of a harvest song. You will not know what until you reach for it and it is gone. We write it on
every lantern's base: *Dust on the glass. Wipe it. It is someone.*

You asked about the one that sighs. There was a woman in grey who walked the Moor road for years, and her lantern
carried her back more times than anyone has counted. Every time, it kept a little. In the end there was nothing left
of her to carry back, and the lantern came back to the stones without her. Nobody took it up, because nobody could
light it; it was already lit. It stands at the crossroads now. It breathes. The flame draws down, slowly, and then
lifts, slowly, the way a sleeper breathes.

Her name was Aveth. That is all I know of her, except one thing the old tender told me when I was a girl, which I
have never forgotten: she was kind about the road.

## Somebody always sits up

*Said at deathbed vigils in the lowlands. This is how an old woman of the families who bury at the Barrows said it to
her grandson, who wrote it down after she died.*

We have no end of our own. The faiths each have one. The brothers in the white mountains say their Mother will set
the world down at last. The processions say the flesh will fill every empty place until there is nothing empty left.
The circles say every soul that ever died will come home up its valley in the same winter. The shrines say the last
breath will finish going out. I have heard them all on the road. I do not know which of them is right, and nobody has
asked me.

What we know is smaller than that. The lanterns go out one at a time. Every lantern keeps a little of everyone it
carries back, and souls do not last forever, even kept ones. Every year there are a few less lanterns on the roads
than the year before. One day there will be one lantern left, on one road, and one of us under it.

And somebody will sit up with it, the way we sit up with the dying, and the newborn, and the dead before the
barrow. Somebody always sits up.
