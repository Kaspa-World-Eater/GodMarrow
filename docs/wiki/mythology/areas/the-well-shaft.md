# The Well-Shaft

*Act I · zone `well_shaft` · under the ground · creatures of levels 10–13 · borders: the Drowned Village · in the
game: the old procedural zone, to be remade by the bog's method · the village above: `drowned-village.md`*

Down the village well, rung by rung, into the dark the village drew its water from. The shaft is the well in the Sunken
Square: forty turns of rope deep in the village's day, lined with stone and set with iron rungs, its old rope still
hanging taut down the middle. Below where the village dug, the shaft goes on, and the rungs go on with it, made of bone
and warmer the deeper they go. It coils as it goes down, like a throat, and the water that rose out of it to drown the
village still goes up: it creeps up the walls and drips upward from the floor. Bells toll in it at dusk. It is the
deepest place in the wet country, and the god shows plainly: on the surveyor's chart the Fen lies at the throat, and
this is the throat itself (a proposal). It should feel close, round and breathing, a long way down.

**The one image:** iron rungs going down a round stone shaft with a rope hanging taut down its middle, and below where
the iron stops, rings of warm bone going on into the dark, with drops of water rising past the lantern.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### The rungs

*As the well-sinkers of the lowlands tell it to their apprentices before they are let down their first shaft. A
copyist wrote it down from an old sinker who would not give his name.*

Every one of us hears about the well in the drowned village before we go down a shaft of our own, and this is how it is
told.

The village sent for us when it was young. The master who sank its well went down an arm's length at a time, as we
still do, and at every arm's length he set an iron rung in the lining, so that he could always climb out. At forty
turns of rope he struck water, cold and good, and he came up, and the village paid him, and drew its water for longer
than anybody remembers.

When the water came up dry and drowned them, our guild sent its oldest master down that well to see what had become of
a shaft of ours. The well was empty; the water had all gone up out of it. He climbed down past the old iron, rung by
rung, and at the bottom, where our master had stopped, he found the shaft going on down, and the rungs going on with
it.

They were bone. They were set at the same spacing as ours, an arm's length apart, as if whoever set them had learned it
from us, or we had learned it from them. They were warm under his hands, and the deeper he went the warmer they were.
The shaft began to turn as it went down, the way a stair turns in a tower, and its walls came round him in rings. The
water was going the wrong way down there. It crept up the walls past him, and drops of it fell upward off the rungs and
struck him under the chin.

He climbed down as far as his light lasted and never reached the end of the rungs. When he came up he would not say
what he had heard, except that something down there was breathing, slowly, and that at dusk something rang. He never
set another rung. He made the rule we all learn before anything else: set your own rungs, every one, and count them
going down, and if there is one more when you count them coming up, leave that shaft and never go back.

Some of us say the bone rungs were set by diggers older than any of ours, before there was anyone to dig for. Some
say the shaft is the god's own throat, and the rungs are the rings in it, and our master only followed them down. A few
of the old ones say nothing, and set their rungs closer than an arm's length, so that nothing else's will fit between
them.

### The bells at dusk

*What a bog guide told the pilgrims she was taking across, while they waited at the edge of the drowned village for
the bells to stop. One of them wrote it down.*

There. You hear it? Those are the village's bells, and it is dusk, so they are ringing. We wait here until they stop.
Nobody crosses the square while they ring.

The belfry fell so long ago that its stones have gone green, and you can see it lying across a lane under the water.
The bell-frame is empty, and nobody has ever found the bells. And every dusk they ring, down under the square, and the
sound comes up out of the well as if the well were a throat and the bells were somewhere in its chest.

The old woman in the reed hut says it is her sister ringing them, who stayed on the chapel roof when the water came and
went down to their mother, and has rung for evening ever since.

The boatmen say the bells got up and walked when the tower fell, the way bells have done in the barrow country, each
with somebody's head shut up inside it, and that they went down the well because it was the only road left them.

The Shrine Keepers say every bell has its small god, and a bell's god is its sound, and these are ringing down there
for the names they gave to the wind on the morning the great one went still.

I say they ring for dusk, the way bells always did, because nobody ever told them to stop. My mother walked the bog all
her life and never crossed the square while they rang, and nor do I, and that is reason enough.

Count them with me. Seven. Sometimes eight. There is no telling how many bells a village that size had, and nobody
left to ask but the ones under the water, and they are busy.

There. They have stopped. Now we go.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Drawn from:** deep hand-dug wells lined in stone (the castle wells of Carisbrooke and Beeston); the spiral ramps
  round the open shaft of St Patrick's Well at Orvieto, and the spiral stair of the Initiation Well at Sintra, their
  landings looking into the void; and, below them, the inside of a throat: the windpipe's rings, the folds of the
  throat, the glands along the neck.
- **The shape of the zone:** the well's own shaft is its spine, an open round shaft with iron rungs in its wall and the
  rope hanging taut down the middle. The walkable way coils round it as a ledge or ramp, with openings that look into
  the shaft. Near the top, round chambers open off the coil: cisterns the village cut sideways in dry summers when the
  well ran low.
- **Three depths**, the god showing more the deeper:
  1. dressed stone lining and iron rungs, rusted dark in the wet centuries before the water went dry;
  2. raw rock, then the meeting with bone: the last iron rung, and an arm's length below it the first bone one;
  3. the Coiling Deep: walls in close pale rings like a windpipe's, warm, smooth, a little glossy, and the air moving
     in and out.
- **The water going up:** the dry water creeps up the walls as a clear film and drips upward off ledges and rungs;
  drops rise past the lantern and vanish up the shaft; where it gathers, it lies against the undersides of things. Every
  floor is dry.
- **The floor is never bare:** what the village dropped down its well over the centuries (rings, shoes, a child's
  knife, bucket staves, a broken bucket, rope ends), stone grit and flakes of rust; lower down, bone grit and fine dust
  in the hollows between the rings.

**Landmarks.**
1. **The Well-Head.** *Shape:* the top of the shaft, a disc of grey sky far overhead. *Cause:* the rope; the first iron
   rungs worn bright on their tops; the scrape-marks of a thousand buckets spiralling down the lining. *Material:*
   dressed stone, iron. *Light:* the sky's disc thrown as a hard pool on the far wall (the moon's at night), its source
   out of sight above the curb. The zone's first lantern-stone stands on the first landing.
2. **The First Rung.** *Shape:* a ledge where the stone lining ends and the bone begins. *Cause:* the old well-hook is
   caught here, its barb driven into the bone and the bone closed round it as flesh closes round a splinter; the rope
   rises from it, taut, all the way to the square; beside the last iron rung, rows of scratched tallies where those who
   came down counted their rungs. *Material:* cut grey stone meeting warm, pale bone. *Light:* the second
   lantern-stone, and from far below the Pyre-Saints' flicker on the undersides of the bone rungs.
3. **The Coiling Deep.** *Shape:* the bottom of the coil, a round chamber where the walls come round in close rings,
   its floor sloping into a narrowing throat that goes on out of reach; nobody has found the end. *Cause:* the floor
   scuffed in rings where the bells turn; the rising drops thickest here. *Material:* ringed bone, smooth and warm.
   *Light:* pale swellings set in the ringed walls give a faint cold light, barely glowing, like the bog's pustules;
   never red. The third lantern-stone.

**Light and weather.** No weather; the shaft breathes, slowly, in and out. The sky's disc at the top; the lantern as
the key light; the Pyre-Saints' fire moving far below; the cold glands at the bottom. No fog, no red light.

**The god showing through: plainly, the most in the wet country.** The bone rungs, the rings of the throat, the warmth,
the breathing, the glands, the water falling up.

**Creatures.** The game's mix: Bloatlings 45, Drowned Husks 41, Wick-Saints of the Canopy 11, Bellwethers 8, Mire
Vein-Borers 6, Pyre-Saints 6, Gravebloats 4.
- **Bloatlings:** the most; small walking stomachs that come up the coil in numbers and burst; on the narrow ledge a
  burst closes the way. By one telling, the village's buckets brought them up. **Gravebloats** are few.
- **Drowned Husks:** villagers who went down the well after the water left it, and the curious since, standing on the
  ledges.
- **Wick-Saints of the Canopy:** hanging in the open shaft under the well-head, spiralling down to the lantern.
- **Bellwethers:** the village's bells, by one telling; they toll at dusk, and the names cast round their rims run
  backward. On the coil, keep the wall at your back: one that charges past on the open side goes over the edge and is
  heard tolling all the way down (a proposal).
- **Pyre-Saints:** burning far below, seen from above as a moving glow; on the ledge their burning footprints close the
  way, and the rising water does not douse them. **Mire Vein-Borers** keep under the cisterns' silted floors.

**Sound.** The rope creaking under its strain; iron rungs ringing under the boot and bone rungs dull; the small pluck of
drops leaving the floor; the shaft breathing; bells tolling up from below at dusk; the Pyre-Saints crackling far down.

**Keep out.** A literal mouth, tongue or teeth (rings, warmth and breath only); wet flesh (Act III's); water standing on
any floor; fog; red light.
