# The Tree-Hollow

*Act I · zone `tree_hollow` · outdoors · creatures of levels 6–7 · borders: the Pilgrim Road · in the game: the old
procedural zone (104 × 104 yards), to be remade by the bog's method · its trees: `hollow-wood.md`*

Off the Pilgrim Road, in a dell among the pale trees, stands one trunk as wide as a chapel, hollow, and open on the side
that faces the road. Its inner walls are ribbed like the inside of a wrist, and they pulse, faintly. In a knot in that
wall a candle burns that has not run down in eight hundred years. Scratch the wall and a clear cold thing runs out,
almost water. Somewhere in it the bark has closed over something, and nobody agrees what.

It is a small, still side place, a dead end off the road where pilgrims have always stopped to shelter and stare: a
chapel with no faith in it. The god shows here as a pulse. The woodcutters say this tree is the god's wrist.

**The one image:** a pale trunk as wide as a chapel, its hollow open like a door, and deep inside, in a knot in the
ribbed wall, one small steady flame.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### Still burning

*The note at the front of the copyists' book of the candle, written by a copyist when the book had to be
rebound. Each year one copyist walks down the Pilgrim Road to the great hollow tree and writes one line in it.*

The book is older than its binding and older than this scriptorium, and nobody knows who began it. It has a line for
every year, and nearly every line is the same two words: *still burning*. I have counted eight hundred and some. The
first lines are in a hand none of us can read, the letters all leaning left, but we know the words by their shape,
because they are the same shape as ours.

The custom is that on the first grey morning of the year a copyist walks down the road to the hollow tree, goes in,
looks at the candle in the knot, and comes home and writes the line. It has been my turn three times. The candle is no
bigger than your thumb. It stands in a knot at the height of a man's heart, with the wood drawn back from the flame all
round, and its light is small and does not flicker, even when the wind is loud in the trees. Some early copyist cut a
notch in the wood beside it at the top of the wax. The wax still stands even with the notch.

Who lit it, the book does not say; its first line says only that it was burning. The pilgrims say a saint lit it on her
way down the road and asked the tree to keep it for her until she came back up. The woodcutters say nobody lit it: the
tree grew round it, as the trees of the pale wood grow round the things the god dropped. The shrine-folk bow to it as
they bow to any small god, and say nothing.

Three lines in the book are different. One, long ago, says *still burning* in a hand no copyist of that year would own
to. In another year the copyist wrote *still burning*, and under it, *someone else was here*. And the line for the year
the Fen went grey says only *leaning*.

I will walk down again if I am asked. I would rather not be asked.

### The wrist

*Told by a woodcutter of the pale wood to a copyist who sheltered with him in the hollow tree through a storm. The
copyist wrote it down by the candle's light.*

Put your back to the wall. Here, between the ribs. Now wait.

You feel it: slow, slower than your own. Every tree in the wood has it if you are patient, but in here you need not be.
My grandfather said this tree is the god's wrist. When the god came down, he said, it lay with one hand up by its face,
the way a man sleeps with a hand by his cheek, and this is where the hand meets the arm. That is why the walls are
ribbed like the inside of your wrist, and why it beats so plain. You put two fingers to a man's wrist to know if he is
living. You put your back to this.

If it is the wrist, you will ask, where is the hand? Everybody asks. The god had many hands, my grandfather said, and
kept none of them. You will have seen them on the road, walking on their fingers. They come here at night, more of them
than anywhere in the wood, and stand about outside, the way a man goes back to look at the house he was born in and
never knocks.

Do not scratch the wall. You will want to. A clear cold stuff runs out, almost water, and the scratch is closed before
you have wiped your finger. The other trees weep warm. This one weeps cold.

And do not sleep against the seam: that long smooth place behind the candle, taller than a man, where the bark has
closed over something and healed pale. Some say a pilgrim slept against it once, and the tree took him in. Some say a
saint asked to be kept, and was. The copyists say there was a second door there once, and the tree shut it. It is warmer
than the rest of the wall. I have never put my ear to it, and I will not start tonight.

The little lights drifting about in here are spores. They drift toward your warmth. They mean nothing by it; they only
want warm.

Lie down on this side, by the opening. The storm will be gone by morning. It always is, in here.

## For building it

The zone is the old procedural map. Remake it by the bog's method: causes first, one landmark by the pit method, every
object a new design.

**The land.** A dell in the pale old growth, with one giant at its heart. Real models for the giant: the Chapel Oak of
Allouville-Bellefosse in Normandy, a hollow oak said to be eight hundred years old or more, with two small chapels built
inside its trunk; the fire-hollowed giant sequoias a person can walk into; the ancient yews that hollow with age and
live on.
- **The dell:** a basin sixty to eighty yards across, ringed by vein-trees whose combed roots all run down into it
  toward the great trunk. Open ground between them, no clumps, the few young trees dead.
- **The great trunk:** fifteen to eighteen yards across at the butt (a chapel's width), buttressed, broken off far above
  and out of frame, so the hollow rises into the dark like a chimney. The opening faces the road, four yards wide and
  seven high, rolled round with smooth woundwood lips into a rough arch. Inside, a room ten yards across with a floor of
  old bark and soft heartwood crumble.
- **The floor is never bare:** deep pale litter, peeled bark, husks of fallen veins, moss and twigs; white caps on sound
  ground, blue round the great trunk where the ground is hollow; inside, crumble, old wax drips below the knot, and
  pilgrims' leavings (a button, a strip of cloth).
- **Objects, by cause:** the lantern-stone at the dell's lip, where the side path leaves the road; the waystone among
  the giants; the shrine a wayside shrine of two posts and a lintel at the opening; the two chests as pilgrims' caches.

**Landmarks**, each by the pit method:
- **The hollow.**
  - **Shape:** the chapel-wide trunk and its tall arched opening.
  - **Story by cause:** a great limb tore away centuries ago, the heart decayed and fell out, and the tree rolled
    woundwood round the wound until it stood as an arch; pilgrims' names cut in the inner wall long ago have healed into
    scars.
  - **Material:** pale bark outside; inside, a bare wall ribbed in vertical cords like the tendons of a wrist, smooth
    and faintly glossy.
  - **Light:** the candle. **Age:** older than the road's stones.
- **The candle in the knot:** at heart height in the inner wall, a knot-hole with the wood drawn back from the flame in
  a smooth ring; a thumb-sized candle, and beside it an old copyist's notch, even with the wax. Its flame never
  flickers, even when the wind moves everything else. It is the zone's one warm light besides the lantern, small and
  warm yellow, seen from outside only as a glow on the inner wall.
- **The seam:** a long smooth healed scar in the inner wall behind the candle, taller than a man, pale and warm, the
  bark closed over something. Never opened, never explained.

**Light and weather.** Overcast in the dell, moonlit at night. Inside, the candle and the lantern. A little grey comes
down the hollow from the broken top far above, and in rain a thin fall runs down the inner walls in films (stemflow
inside a tree). Outside, the canopy shelters, drips fall heavy under the giants, and stemflow runs down the flutes. The
spores are a few motes drifting slowly toward the pilgrim's warmth, lit only where light catches them: never a haze,
never fog.

**The god showing through.** As a pulse: the ribbed cords inside the hollow swell slowly every few seconds. Also the
cold clear weep where the wall is struck, the roots converging on the trunk, and the hands drawn to it.

**Creatures.**
- **Tithe-Hands** (14): drawn to the trunk at night, more than anywhere in the Wood; put a buttress at your back.
- **Husks** (17): stand like posts in the dell. The hollow's opening is the one doorway in the Wood: fight them there,
  one at a time.
- **Gasps** (10): breath snagged in the hollow's still air. They keep off the candle's light, and the lantern and the
  candle together hold them back.
- **Wick-Saints** (4) and **Wick-Saints of the Canopy** (4): circle the candle and never touch it; the Canopy kind drop
  from the crowns round the dell.
- **Weepers** (4): shoot from behind the giants on the dell's rim; their needles stick in the bark.
- **By material:** the trunk is full cover, and inside the hollow every shot stops on the walls. Fire takes the dell's
  dry litter and the crumble inside, never the living wall.

**Sound.** Outside, the wood's ticking and the drips. Inside, the pulse close and loud, like your own blood heard with a
hand over your ear; the storm muffled; the candle silent.

**Keep out.** Fog or clouds of spores; red light; the furniture of a chapel (no altar, benches or seated figure: it is a
tree people shelter in); the seam opened.

**Open questions.**
- **Inside or around:** the game's line puts the zone "inside a trunk as wide as a chapel", but 104 yards square cannot
  fit inside one trunk. The proposal here is the dell, with the hollow as its heart.
- **The candle's use:** none, or the zone's shrine?
