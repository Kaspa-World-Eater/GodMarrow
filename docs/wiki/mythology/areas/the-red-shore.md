# The Red Shore

*Act I · the start: the act's town and the wild lands round it · names not yet chosen · not yet in the game (the
Ashen Moor's bare camp stands in for it) · built so far: the temple hall, as the 3D trial
(`tools/landkit/passes/thai_temple.md`, pass 6, B-) · the shore folk's myths, the Red Water's among them:
`../../in-game-texts/08-the-shore-folk.md` · their serpent: `../../in-game-texts/09-the-long-back.md`*

The game begins at the edge of the world.

**The Red Water.** The land ends in black sand, and beyond the sand lies the Red Water. It is an ocean of blood going
on forever under a grey sky. The people here do not know it is a sea. The shore folk believe it is the blood of the
Fallen One, the body the land is made of, run out of a wound in its side. The tide is its heartbeat, and whatever
goes into the Red comes back. The dead of the whole world wash up out of it onto the black sand.

**The village.** Above the sand, hidden in giant trees on the first terrace past the bluffs, stands the shore folk's
village:
- a ring of round hide huts around a great fire pit, inside a cedar palisade;
- a small, old temple hall with a serpent stair;
- carved posts with little houses on them at the feet of the giant trees.

Winter after winter the dead and the things that come up out of the Red besieged it. The wall broke, the last of the
people died on the temple's platform, and now no one lives here. The only soul left is a wandering merchant who is
picking over the huts and the dead, a little mad, and laughing at everything the pilgrim does not know.

**The area** runs from the water inland the way a real temperate rainforest coast does, band by band:
- the black sands and the sea stacks;
- the drift logs, the dunes and the wind-bent spruce;
- the bluffs and the river mouths;
- the old growth, with the village in it.

Much of it is the sand and the coast. **The culture** is the shore folk's own: a fusion of Native American forms (the
cedar plank, carved posts, canoes and woven bark of the Northwest Coast) with Thai ones (tiered roofs, flame-carved
gables, the serpent stair, spirit houses). **The mood** is Dark Souls: a fallen people's last fire at the edge of the
world. The village is safe, as Diablo II's camp is; the danger is everything round it. The god shows only as a hint.

**The one image:** the pilgrim small on a black beach, the red going out to the end of the world, and behind, up
through the bent trees, the smoke of a fire that is still burning the dead.

### The keeper's account

*Cut with a knife into the inside of the temple door, low down, as if by someone sitting with their back to it. The
lines crowd together as they go. The shore folk wrote little; this is the longest thing of theirs anyone has found.*

I am the last keeper of the stair, and I am cutting this into the door because there is no one left to tell it to.

We were never troubled in my grandmother's time, or in hers. The forest kept us, and the Red kept to the sand. Our
dead went to the fire, nothing of ours went into the water, and nothing that was ours came up out of it.

Winters ago now, in the wet season, the dead began to come up out of the Red again. They were not our dead. They
came from somewhere far along the water, from wherever else people give their dead to it. They came up the black
sand at night in the old way, red to the eyes, and stood at the edge of the trees and looked up at our fire. Some
still wore pilgrims' cloaks, and some carried staffs. In the morning they went back down into the water, and the
next night there were more of them, and then one night they did not go back down.

After the dead came the other things, the ones that were never alive the way we are: things that crawl up out of the
surf on their fingers, things with no faces, things that come up through the sand where it is soft. My mother said
the Red had forgotten whose shore this was. The old women said we had let the stair go unswept and brought it on
ourselves, and they swept it twice a day after that until they died.

We built the wall higher on the water side. We split cedar for it, and they gnawed through the cedar, and we split
more. When the cedar near us was gone we pulled down the empty huts and used their poles, then the doors, then the
boards from the back of the temple, and at the last the old canoes, which nobody had touched since our grandmothers' time.

Our fighters went up on the wall every dusk. Before they went up, each one danced the slow dance their teacher had
taught them, so that if they fell, their teacher would know them and come for them before the Red could. We played
the drum and the pipe on the wall all night, so the fighters would not hear the dead calling up to them from the
sand.

Every night someone fell, and every night we gave them to the fire before they were cold. After the first winters we
never let the fire go out. We burned the dead of the wall, the old who died in their beds and the children who did
not wake, and at the end we were burning the huts to burn the dead.

The wall went tonight, at the wet end, where the posts had gone soft. We came back to the temple, because the stair
is the last thing in the world they will climb. There are eleven of us on the platform. The fire is still burning in
the ring; I can see it through the gap in the door.

Whoever reads this: give us to the fire. Do not let the Red have us.

### A letter that was never sent

*Written by a pilgrim to her brother in the lowlands. It never reached him. The merchant sold it, with the satchel it
was in, to the next pilgrim who came.*

I found the village the old trader told us of, and it is as he said, except that he said there would be people.

You come to it from the sand. I walked two days along the black beach with the Red on my left hand. I will not try to
tell you what that is like, except that it does not end, and it comes up the sand and goes back as if it were
breathing. On the second morning there were footprints coming up out of it that did not go back down. Where a stream
comes down to the beach, a path climbs up through the bent trees, and at the top of it, in the big trees, is the
village.

The wall is down in a dozen places, mostly on the side that faces the water. Inside it the huts stand in a ring,
round and roofed with hides, most of them burned to their poles. In the middle of the ring is a pit as wide as a
chapel floor, and there is a fire in it, and there are dead on the fire. It was burning when I came and it is burning
now. In all my time here I have not seen anyone put wood on it or carry the dead to it, yet each morning there are more of
them on it than the night before.

There is one man here. He is nobody from the village; he says so himself, and laughs. He carries a pack bigger than
he is, and he goes from hut to hut, and down to the sand at low water, taking what is left: knives, bells, little
carved figures, the corded bands the fighters wore on their arms, hides with writing inked on them. He laid it all
out on a hide by the temple steps and asked me what I would give for it.

I asked him who keeps the fire. He asked me whether I knew what I was standing on. I said a village. He laughed so
long he had to sit down on the temple stair. Then he stood up quickly, as if the stone had been warm, brushed
himself off, and would not sit there again.

He knows the names of the dead. He says them when he picks up their things, the way you would say the name of
someone in the next room. I asked how he knew them, and he said they had told him. I asked when, and he said
"Tomorrow," and laughed at me again.

Nothing comes over the wall at night. I can hear them out on the sand, but they do not come in. He says there is
nothing left in here that they want. He was looking at me when he said it, and I have not slept well since.

At the foot of the big trees round the clearing, little houses stand on carved posts, roofed like the temple, for
the spirits of the trees. Most of them lean, and the flowers hung on them have gone to brown string. One of them has
fresh flowers on it. He says he did not put them there. I believe him, and I wish I did not.

### A note in the margin of a map

*Written beside a lowland map of the Hide, where the land ends and the mapmaker painted a red wash down to the edge
of the sheet and over it. The note is signed with a mark only.*

Every faith that has heard of the Red Water has a name for it, and no two agree.

The processions of the Bleeding Maiden call it the Open Side. They teach that the world began as a wound that opened
and did not close, and some of them say this is that wound. A few have walked the whole way to the black sand to
kneel at its edge with their arms held out. Even they give it nothing, because the shore folk told them what comes
back.

The Pale Order calls it the Unfinished. To the brothers it is everything in the world that has not yet been laid to
rest, waiting. A brother who went to look said he could not count the waves, and was ashamed of it, and went home.

The circles of the Veiled Crone hold that a soul cannot pass its own reflection, and so they cover every mirror in a
house where someone has died. They say the Red is a mirror nobody can cover. The dead who go into it see their own
faces and stop, and that is why they come back.

The Shrine Keepers say that the last breath of the world is going out, and that the Red is where it goes when it is
spent, the colour breath turns when there is nowhere further for it to go.

The grandmothers of the lowlands say it is only the god's blood, run out of it when it died, and that it has been
trying to get back in ever since.

The mapmaker painted it to the edge of the sheet and then over the edge, as if he could not find where to stop.

## For building it

**From the water inland.** The area follows a real temperate rainforest coast (the Olympic coast, its Rialto, Ruby
and Second Beaches) band by band, so each part grows out of the one before it. The sea is the one thing that is not
of the real world.

1. **The Red Water.**
   - Blood to the horizon under a grey sky, with no far shore.
   - **Colour:** its own, under grey light: a deep red-brown, near black in the troughs and under cloud shadow, dark
     wet crimson where the light lies on a swell. Nothing glows; the no-red-light law holds (the red is the water's
     own colour, and nothing lights it).
   - **Movement:** thick and slow. Long swells, heavy breakers that fold late and fall slowly, dark foam that clings.
     It moves on a dying heartbeat (Derek, 2026-10-09): a surge, a weaker second surge, then a long stillness, slower
     than any sea. The blood is thick and viscous: no chop, no spray, a skin on the surface, a sagging roll instead of
     a curl, the swash a slow blunt tongue that leaves a coat draining back.
   - **The tide,** its pulse, twice a day. At low water it bares rock benches with pools of clotted red and
     red-brown kelp lying in wracks.
   - **Where streams cross the sand,** clear water fans into the red in streaks.
2. **The black sand.**
   - Fine black sand, glossy and red-black where the surf has just left it, dry grey-black above the tide.
   - Cobble bars of rounded black stones.
   - Tide marks of dried red film.
   - The washed-up dead along the strand: pilgrims' cloaks, staffs, the drowned.
   - Footprints coming up out of the water.
   - Above the tide line, the shore folk's old canoes, upturned, grey and split, from when the dead were given to the
     Red.
3. **Sea stacks and headlands.**
   - Dark basalt stacks standing out in the surf, the red breaking round their feet, wind-shaped spruce on their
     tops.
   - Arches and rock benches.
   - On the headland, memorial poles of stacked human faces looking out to sea, each capped with a little tiered
     roof.
4. **The wrack and the bone jam.** At the storm line, where a coast would pile drift logs, the Red has thrown up giant
   bones (Derek, 2026-10-09: "Turn the logs into bones"): long bones with knuckled ends, ribs, some arching out of the
   sand, and vertebrae, piled crosswise, old and pitted, taller than the pilgrim. Whose bones is never said.
5. **The backshore.** Dune grass, beach pea and salal, cut by small clear streams.
6. **The spruce fringe and the bluffs.**
   - Sitka spruce sheared by the wind, flagged and **leaning out toward the Red**, their seaward sides dead and grey.
     On a real coast the trees lean inland. Here the one wind of the world, the god's last breath going out (nothing
     it carries ever comes back), blows from the land out over the water, so the whole fringe leans to sea as if it
     were being pulled. An uncanny thing to get right.
   - Salal and evergreen huckleberry dense beneath them.
   - The bluffs eroding, roots hanging from their lips, fallen trees down their faces.
   - A path climbing where a stream comes down.
7. **The river mouths.** A river braids across the sand to the Red, its clear water staining red where they meet,
   with drift logs jammed in its mouth.
8. **The old growth.** Inland the trees go up, with long corridors between the giants, never clumps
   (`godmarrow-forest-feel`):
   - Sitka spruce and western red cedar, 60 to 80 m tall and 3 to 4 m across, crowns always out of frame;
   - bigleaf maples hung with moss;
   - sword ferns to the waist, nurse logs, deep moss.

   **The floor is never bare** (Derek's "ground stays alive"): needles, cones, bark strips, twigs and fallen limbs, in
   many tones.
9. **Beyond (a proposal):** deeper in, the trees turn pale and warm, and that is the Hollow Wood.

**The village.** On the first terrace above the bluffs, hidden from the sand by the fringe.
- **The huts:** a ring of round huts of bent poles, hides lashed over them, a smoke hole at the top.
  - The hides are pale and hairless, from the things killed at the wall; there is nothing else in the world to skin.
    Inked writing runs along some of them.
  - Each hut has a carved cedar door post of stacked faces.
  - By cause: some burned to their ribs, some fallen in, some patched with hides of another colour.
- **The palisade:** split cedar posts, sharpened and lashed, highest on the water side.
  - Rebuilt again and again with hut poles, doors, temple boards, a carved gable panel upside down, and at the last
    the old canoes, set on end.
  - By cause: its base is gnawed and scored on the outside; whole lengths lean or lie flat; the wet end is soft and
    black where the water stands, and that is where the last breach is.
  - The gate faces the path down to the sand, flanked by carved face-posts.
- **The fire pit of the dead** (by the pit method, `tools/art_study/reports/05-pit-of-offering.md`):
  - **Shape:** a wide ring of fieldstones set in clay, with a low ash mound built up inside over years, and a worn
    ramp where the bearers carried the dead up.
  - **Story by cause:**
    - the bearers' side is worn smooth;
    - the downwind stones are scorched further;
    - calcined bone shows in the ash at the edges;
    - the newest dead lie wrapped in cloth on a pyre of split cedar.
  - **Material:** fire-cracked stone, cedar ash, white calcined bone, soot.
  - **Light:** the only warm light in the village. The deep embers light the smoke's underside, the faces of the huts
    and the wet ground; rain hisses and steams on the hot stones.
  - **Age:** every winter of the siege, burning without a break.

  It is this game's bonfire.
- **The temple hall,** as its brief (`tools/landkit/passes/thai_temple.md`):
  - a raised platform with the serpent stair;
  - whitewashed brick under stucco;
  - three tiers of swept roof with flame-carved gables;
  - eight boundary stones round it, with the stair's story cut into them (`../../in-game-texts/08-the-shore-folk.md`);
  - the last stand's spears and shields on the platform;
  - the keeper's account inside the door.

  **The fusion, for its next passes:**
  - the porch posts as carved cedar faces;
  - eye-like ovoids carved among the gable's flames;
  - the serpent on the rails carved in ovoids and flames together.

  **Inside:** a square of black stone at the centre of the floor, polished by knees, with one candle burning before it
  that no one living could have lit. It is a hidden source, seen only by its light on the floor and the door's edge.
  Bentwood boxes holding the ashes of the dead are stacked in the wall niches, painted and gilded. What lies under the
  black stone is never said.
- **Spirit houses:** small temples on carved posts at the feet of the giant trees, with tiered roofs and tiny gables
  and a door the size of a hand.
  - Most lean, their roofs broken, garlands gone to brown string, small figures fallen and green with algae.
  - One has fresh flowers.
- **The merchant's corner,** by the temple steps:
  - the dead's things laid out on a hide: knives, bells, corded bands, carved figures, inked hides, a beaten copper
    plate;
  - his pack, taller than he is, hung with more.

  He has no lantern, the only soul on the roads without one, and he does not seem to mind.
- **The defenders' things,** strewn where they fell:
  - leaf-bladed spears and round shields of hide on cane;
  - cedar-bark capes with woven borders;
  - wide conical hats of woven root;
  - the corded bands their teachers tied on their arms and heads.

**The fusion: a guide.**
- **From the Northwest Coast** (the Olympic coast's own peoples' forms):
  - cedar plank and post;
  - carved posts of stacked human faces;
  - canoes with tall prows;
  - cedar bark woven into capes, mats and conical hats;
  - bentwood boxes;
  - beaten copper plates as wealth;
  - ovoid and U-form carving.
- **From Thailand:**
  - tiered, swept roofs;
  - flame-carved gables and finials;
  - the serpent stair and rails;
  - spirit houses;
  - black lacquer and gold leaf;
  - bells and garlands;
  - protective script inked on the skin;
  - the teacher's dance before a fight, to drum and pipe;
  - burning the dead.
- **Where they meet:**
  - spirit houses on carved face-posts;
  - canoe prows carved as the serpent;
  - tiered roofs over plank walls;
  - gables carved with flames and eye-like ovoids together;
  - memorial poles capped with little tiered roofs;
  - inked script in ovoid frames.
- **The rules:**
  - **No animal forms anywhere.** Only human faces, the serpent and insects: no ravens, eagles, orcas or bears from
    the one side, no birds, lions or elephants from the other.
  - **Copy no real nation's crests, stories or ceremonies.** Take the forms and make them the shore folk's own.
  - **The tone is Dark Souls:** weathered, desolate, grand in ruin, sorrowful, with every object telling what
    happened.

**Light and weather.**
- **Always overcast.**
- **Drizzle acts by material** (`godmarrow-weather-in-world`):
  - hides darken and shine;
  - eaves and hut edges drip;
  - ruts puddle;
  - steam rises where rain meets the pit stones;
  - smoke flattens under the canopy.
- **The wind goes out over the Red,** always: the world's one wind, never onshore. The breakers throw a fine red spray
  that stains the seaward faces of the stacks and the drift logs, but the wind takes it back out, so the stain stops a
  few yards up the sand: a story by cause.
- **Never a fog layer.**
- **The warm points:** the pit, the hidden candle.

**The god showing through: a hint only.**
- the pale root, warm and slowly beating, grown round the head of the stone guardian at the temple door;
- the temple stair warm under the hand;
- the ground under the pit's ash too warm for the fire alone;
- on the shore, the giant bones the Red throws up where drift logs should lie, and a low pulse in the wet sand at the
  water's edge.

Nothing more; the god shows more the deeper you go.

**Creatures.** None inside the wall: it is safe, and nothing comes in.
- **On the sand:** the dead the Red gives back (the drowned, pilgrims, strangers from far along the water), things
  that crawl up out of the surf on their fingers (Tithe-Hands), faceless things (Weepers), and things that come up
  through soft sand (Vein-Borers).
- **In the forest:** what has come inland.

The game's mix for the new zones is to be set when they are built.

**Sound.**
- **On the shore:**
  - the surf, slow and heavy;
  - the hiss of thick foam drawing back over sand;
  - the low pulse at the water's edge;
  - the wind in the bent spruce.
  - **No gulls, no shore life at all.** A beach with nothing calling over it is the eeriest thing here.
- **In the village:**
  - rain on hides and the canopy dripping;
  - the fire's crackle and the hiss on the stones;
  - the giant trunks creaking far off;
  - insects at dusk;
  - the merchant muttering the names of the dead.

**Music:** the new main theme (Derek, 2026-10-08). It is Middle-Eastern in the manner of Diablo II and dark, with a
touch of Muay Thai's sarama: the pi java's reed, the glong khaek's two drums, the ching. In the world, it is the drum
and pipe the shore folk played on the wall.

**The merchant, for whoever writes him.** He seems a little mad and he mocks the pilgrim's ignorance:
- he laughs at the wrong moments, answers questions with questions, and tells a different story each time;
- he knows things he should not, such as the names of the dead;
- he talks about his own life: the roads, the sand at low water, who he has sold to;
- he is cruel, and now and then oddly gentle;
- he never teaches the pilgrim how anything works (no tutorials, ever).

**Keep out.**
- **Story and quests** (Derek: "we're not writing story or quests or anything. We're just building the world").
  Everything here builds the world; nothing is a hook.
- **Real religious images:** no seated figure in the hall (it would read as a Buddha), and no real script.
- **Sea life:** no shells, barnacles, fish or birds; the Red holds nothing alive but kelp.
- **Fog.**
- **Light:** no red light (the Red is red by colour, never by glow).
- **Corny markers** over the merchant.

**Open questions for Derek.**
- **The names:** the area, the village and the merchant; and whether the sea's common name is the Red Water.
- **How many zones the start is:** perhaps the sand, the fringe and bluffs, and the old growth with the village.
- **How it joins the rest of Act I.**
- **Which way the sea lies on the map:** west, like the Olympic coast.
- **Which town services the village has** with only the merchant: buying and selling, and perhaps repair. Healing,
  the stash and a waystone are open.
- **Whether the old camp's people** (Maren, Ysolde, Brannoc, Esk) appear later in the act.
- **The Ash Shore (a proposal that ties the two coasts):** far along the coast, the same blood lies under a skin of ash
  and shows grey, "a grey sea that does not move like water".

**Derek's ideas for later (2026-10-09):** "I like the idea of the coast culture believing that the mountains are a
great dead beast of behemoth of a thing, and from its wounds the red flows. So we can have like a lot of waterfalls or
something. I mean blood rivers that flow into estuaries."
- **The bible's telling** (`../03-the-age-of-terrors.md`, 2026-10-09): the mountains inland are the body of the One That
  Unfolds, the Thrae the herald killed; the shore folk call them the Fallen One. **The shore folk's telling:** the
  mountains inland are the body of a great dead behemoth, and the Red runs out of its
  wounds, down the land, to the sea (a rival or a deepening of the Fallen One's wound in its side).
- **For the land:** blood falls down the bluffs and the cliffs; blood rivers cutting the terrace and fanning out into
  estuaries across the black sand; where they meet the Red, the slow mixing of the two.
