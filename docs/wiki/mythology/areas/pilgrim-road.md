# The Pilgrim Road

*Act I · zone `pilgrim_road` · outdoors · creatures of levels 6–8 · borders: the Hollow Wood, the Drowned Fen, the
Tree-Hollow · in the game: the old procedural zone (184 × 128 yards), to be remade by the bog's method*

The old road down. It runs through the Hollow Wood and on down toward the Fen, and it was there before
the Wood, before the faiths and before anyone counted. In the year 206, people who found it already lying there set
numbered stones along it. The figures go down the farther you walk, and nobody will say what they count toward.
Its oldest stretches are paved in something no one laid, and here and there the paving is black glass.

This is the act's long walk: a road that is always going down, even where it seems to climb, sunk between high banks and
then let out across open heath, with kneeling posts, wayside saints and lantern-stones along it, and mourners with no
faces keeping pace in the verges. The god hardly shows. There is only the road itself, too old and too hard, and the
ground growing warmer as the figures fall.

**The one image:** a sunken road going down between banks hung with roots, a numbered stone at its edge with a low
figure cut in it, and black glass in the paving catching the lantern.

### The numbering of the road

*The preface to the copyists' register of the Pilgrim Road's stones, kept in their scriptorium. Each copyist
who takes up the register adds a line below it; the preface has never been changed.*

Every faith on the Hide will tell you it cut this road, and every one of them is wrong. The road was here first. It goes
through the pale wood as if the wood were not there, and the roots turn aside at its edge, as a hand turns aside from a
hot stone. Under the dust it is hard and dark and close-fitted, and nobody has ever found the mark of a tool on it.

The stones came later. They were set along it in one autumn of the two hundred and sixth year after the god died, by
people who found the road already lying there and wanted it numbered. Who those people were, the faiths dispute as they
dispute everything. The brothers in the white mountains say their own first brothers set them, because nothing should go
uncounted. The grandmothers of the lowlands say it was the first children born on the ash, so that their own children
would know how far they had come from the mouth. The shrine-folk say the stones were set so that the road's small god,
having given its name away, would have a count to hold instead. There is no record from that autumn.

The figures go down as you walk, a stone at a time, and by the time the road leaves the pale wood you
can count what is left on your hands. None of the faiths will say what the stones count toward. I asked a brother of the
white mountains, and he said "to the end of the count," which is no answer. I asked an old pilgrim, and she said "down,"
which may be one.

There is a stone that says one. A traveller who spent a night beside it says the ground past it looked like the ground
before it, only warmer. The boatmen of the Fen say it stands at the water's edge, where the road goes under. The
pilgrims say it stands at the head of the warm road the horned ones walk down. I have not found it, and I am too old now
to look.

Copy the figures as they stand. If one seems out of its order, the stone is right and you are wrong.

### The glass in the road

*Told by an old walker at a roadside hearth on the Pilgrim Road to a girl making her first walk. The girl wrote it down
years later, when she had walked it many times herself.*

Look down when you walk, child, and you will see it: black glass in the road, a stretch here and a patch there, smooth
as still water and set in as close as the stones. At night it holds your lantern's light better than any stone. Every
walker agrees on that much: lantern-light off the black glass is the surest road on the Hide. When you cannot see the
way, look for the glass, and follow it down.

How it came there, nobody agrees. The ash-pickers of the Moor say it is blood. Where one of the things that come up
bleeds into the ash, the ash turns to glass, and they say this road was fought over in the first years, and every patch
is a place where something died on it.

My own teacher said otherwise. She once sat out a night on this road with a barefoot monk, one of those from the
mountain that fell, who carried an empty bowl and a lantern that burned black. He asked her for a name, and she nearly
gave him one, and he did not press. In the morning he was gone, and where he had sat there was a patch of black glass in
the shape of a man sitting, still warm. It is three stones down from here. So she held that the glass is where such men
have sat, and that they have walked this road longer than anyone knows.

The oldest walkers, the ones who say the road was here first, say the glass is the road itself: that the whole road is
glass underneath, and the stones and the dust have only grown over it, the way skin grows over a splinter. Where the
glass shows, they say, the road is coming up through.

I do not know which of them is right. I know it is warm under the hand, all of it, and that the lower you go, the more
of it there is.

### Where the singing stops

*A letter from a pilgrim to the copyists, who ask walkers to write to them of anything on the road their
register does not hold.*

You asked for anything on the lower road that your register does not hold. Here is one thing, and I do not know what to
make of it.

We were twelve, walking down out of the pale wood and singing as pilgrims do on this road by day, the long walking-song
whose verses go round. We sang past the kneeling posts, where each of us knelt and found that our foreheads fitted the
hollow worn in the post. We sang past the stone where they say Sister Un's reliquary rested a night on its way below,
the one no dust will settle on. Somewhere below that, we stopped singing.

Nobody decided to. We were in the middle of a verse, and then we were walking without it, all twelve of us, and when I
noticed I could not remember stopping. Nothing marked the place.

At the next lantern-stone an old walker told us every company stops there. In her mother's time, she said, a choir of
forty came down from the lowlands meaning to sing straight through. They sang louder as they came near, and then they
were walking in silence like everyone else, and their master wept because he could not say when they had stopped. Nobody
starts again on the lower road, she said, and you do not miss it.

I went back alone the next morning to find the place. There is a stretch of about thirty paces where the grass on the
verges does not move when the wind blows, and the insects are quiet. I stood in the middle of it and opened my mouth to
sing the verse we had stopped in, and found I did not know how it went.

The road goes down from there, as it always goes down, even where it seems to climb. I am writing this at the bottom of
it. Someone back up the road will have to teach me the verse again.

## For building it

**The land.** A real holloway, with an older road under it.
- **The road:** close-fitted dark polygons with no tool marks, after the natural pavements that look laid (the Giant's
  Causeway, Tasmania's tessellated pavement); heaved and tipped by roots and frost (the locked craggy floor,
  `ground.craggy_height`); dust and leaf drift in the joints, moss on the stones' crowns; black glass in patches, more
  of it the lower the road goes.
- **The holloway:** where it goes down, the road has sunk between banks two to six yards high, as the holloways of
  Dorset and the Weald sank under centuries of feet and rain: roots hanging from the banks, ferns on their faces, the
  groves' crowns meeting overhead.
- **Either side:** a few pale vein-trees at the top end, leaning away from the road; then the Hide's ordinary broadleaf
  groves (oak, ash, holly, hazel gone wild) and open heath; rushes and wet ground toward the Fen.
- **The floor is never bare:** on the road, dust, drifted leaves and the pilgrims' dropped things (a strap, a button, a
  badge); off it, leaf litter, bracken drifts, heather cushions, tussock grass, and tall grass on the verges.
- **Objects, by cause:** lantern-stones a day's walk apart where the ground is kind and nearer where it is not; two
  wayside saints, their faces kissed away; shrines of two posts and a lintel at the forks; the waystones off the road;
  the chests as pilgrims' caches.

**Landmarks**, each by the pit method:
- **The numbered stones:** squat cylinders a yard and a half high, the figure cut on the road face, lichen sized by age;
  three to five in the zone, the figures falling as the road goes down. One has a clean top that no dust settles on
  (Sister Un's). No light of their own: the lantern rakes the cut figures.
- **The glass:** patches set flush in the paving, smooth and warm, rippled where they meet the stone; one in the shape
  of a seated man, three stones below a hearth-ring. Their light is borrowed, the lantern and the moon doubled in broken
  dabs.
- **The kneeling posts:** waist-high grey posts with a hollow rubbed smooth by foreheads at kneeling height, and two
  knee-hollows worn in the step before each: centuries of one motion.
- **The still stretch:** thirty paces with nothing to mark them, where the verge grass does not move in the one wind and
  the insects fall quiet. It is shown only as a pause in the world's living layer and a gap in the sound.

**Light and weather.** Overcast by day, moonlit at night, darker under the holloway's roof of crowns. The lantern is the
key light; the glass doubles it; the lantern-stones make a line of small warm lights going down the road. Rain runs down
the holloway as a stream along the road's edges, as real holloways do, drips from the roots on the banks, beads on the
glass and darkens the paving. No fog.

**The god showing through.** Barely: the road itself, too old and too hard; the roots turning aside; the ground warmer
as the figures fall; once in the zone, a vein breaching the verge.

**Creatures.**
- **Weepers** (55): mourners keeping pace in the verges and shooting from behind stones, posts and saints. Their bone
  needles shatter on stone, so the stones are cover both ways.
- **Husks** (48): standing like posts beside the milestones until three gather. Fight them in the holloway, where the
  banks make a doorway.
- **Tithe-Hands** (29): along the tops of the banks, dropping in behind; put a bank at your back.
- **Gasps** (12): through the banks, in the holloway's still air.
- **The Stalker Crone** (9): in the tall grass of the verges, where the Wood thins. Walk the middle of the road.
- **Vein-Borers** (3): only in the soft verges, never under the road, which is the refuge.
- **Wick-Saints of the Canopy** (2): drop from the groves' crowns.

**Sound.** Footsteps hard on the old paving and close in the holloway; wind in the verge grass; insects; far below,
bells from the Fen. In the still stretch, nothing at all.

**Keep out.** Fog; red light; carts and anything that pulls them; signposts or written names (the road is numbered,
never named on it); letters of any real script; a new-looking road.

**Open questions.**
- **The stone that says one:** where it stands, and whether the game ever shows it. The lore keeps three placements as
  rival tellings (here, and in `root-deep.md`).
- **The Hide's ordinary trees:** broadleaf groves here, against the start's spruce and cedar; and the game gives this
  zone the moor's theme.
- **Where the road begins,** now that the Moor is a bare camp.
