# The Broken Bridge

*Act I · zone `broken_bridge` · outdoors · creatures of levels 10–13 · borders: the Root Deep · in the game: the old
procedural zone (152 × 132 yards), to be remade by the bog's method*

Beyond the Root Deep the old road climbs out into a rift where the Hide lies split open to the sky, and along the bottom
of the rift runs a water clear and slow and thick. An old stone bridge crossed it once. It is broken in the middle now,
and both halves still reach for each other. At the near end are the cold hearth of a river camp, a ferry landing with no
ferry, and a crowd of the dead at the water's edge, facing the far bank. Old Garrow kept the fire here, and he called
the water a vein.

It is the end of the road as far as anyone goes: grand, sad and open, a long view down the rift and across the water to
a bank nobody reaches. The god shows strongly. The rift's walls are the Hide cut through, and the water is what runs in
the god.

**The one image:** two halves of an old stone bridge reaching for each other over a clear, slow vein, and on the near
bank the dead in a crowd, facing the water, waiting.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### Old Garrow's fire

*Told at the banked fire on the Burnt Heath by the woman who keeps it, as her mother kept it before her, to a pilgrim
who had asked about the bridge. He wrote it on the back of his road-tally.*

Garrow kept the fire at the Broken Bridge longer than I have kept this one, and he lied about everything, and he was
good at it. I will tell you what he told me, and you can sort it.

He said the bridge was the last good thing on the old road down. Before it broke, he said, pilgrims crossed it singing
and went on into country nobody up here has seen. There is a camp at the near end, where people waited afterward, when
there was a ferry, and he kept its fire because somebody should. He sat by it every night and told the waiting ones the
news from up here, whether they wanted it or not.

The waiting ones I believe in, because pilgrims who have been down there say the same. They stand at the water's edge
below the camp, a crowd of them, in the clothes they set out in, facing the far bank. They waited for the ferry, and the
ferry stopped coming, and they went on waiting. Garrow said they were no trouble if you did not stand among them, and
that they lift their heads whenever they hear a bell.

He called the water a vein, and if you said river he would stop you halfway through the word. "That is a vein," he would
say, "and you are sitting on its bank." It runs so clear you can see the bottom, he said, and slow, and thick, and it
goes past in long pushes, the way your blood goes when you hold your breath. Put your hand in it and it is warm, and you
feel the push in your fingers, and you never want to do it again.

The last year of his life he came up here to see the seven in the far corner of the Heath. Before he left the bridge, he
said, he raked out his fire and laid it for morning, kindling crossed on the ash, so he would only have to put a light
to it when he got back. He went out to the seven, and came back to my fire, and lay down here with his feet toward the
corner, and I let him. He never went back down.

The boatmen who have been there tell it otherwise. They say the fire at the bridge went out generations before Garrow
was born, its ash already laid for a morning that never came, and that Garrow only sat beside it and told everyone it
was burning. Garrow would have liked that. It is a better lie than any of his.

Either way the ash is still laid, and nobody has put a light to it. I would not.

### The last name

*Told by an old stonecutter of the lowlands to his apprentices, on the day each of them cuts a name for the first time.
One of them wrote it down.*

Our people built half of that bridge. I will tell it as my master told it to me.

The road down was there before anybody, as every road is. At the vein it stopped, and on the far bank it went on again
as far as anyone could see. So the people who walked it then sent for masons, and two crews came, one to each bank.
Nobody now knows how the far crew got across. My master said they were already there and cutting when ours arrived, and
that they sang at their work, and our crew sang back across the water, and that was how the two halves kept time.

They built toward each other, arch by arch, for longer than any of them lived. When a mason died, the next one cut his
name on the parapet and took up his place. That is why the names run as they do, the oldest at the bank and the newest
out toward the middle.

One evening the singing from the far bank stopped in the middle of a line. Our crew called across, and nobody answered.
They could see the far half standing in the last light, with its scaffold on it and its tools lying where they had been
set down, and nobody there. They never heard from the far bank again.

Our crew went on building alone, out as far as the stone would hold without the other half to lean on, and a little
further, and then they stopped. The last of them was cutting his name on the parapet when they stopped. He got half of
it cut. He put down his chisel and walked back up the road, and nobody knows his name, because he never finished it.

The boatmen tell it otherwise. They say the bridge stood whole for a hundred years, and pilgrims crossed it, until one
spring the vein came down in a single great push and took the middle out. After that there was a ferry, with a bell on a
post at the landing to call it, and the last ferry went over at dusk full of people and never came back, and the ones
who had missed it are standing on the bank still. And the pilgrims who knew Garrow say the half-cut name is his, begun
the year he went up to the Heath.

You will hear all of it. I believe ours, because the names are ours. Now take your chisel and cut your name, and finish
it. That is the whole of the rule: whatever you start, cut it to the end.

## For building it

The zone is the old procedural map. Remake it by the bog's method: causes first, one landmark by the pit method, every
object a new design.

**The land.** A rift in the Hide, open to a strip of sky far above, with the vein in its floor. Real models: Þingvellir
in Iceland, the long rift of Almannagjá between walls of layered rock, and the Silfra fissure, whose water is clear a
hundred metres down; the Pont Saint-Bénézet at Avignon, a medieval bridge whose middle the floods took, so that it ends
in the river, with a small chapel on one pier; the old ferry crossings with a bell on a post at the landing.
- **At true scale:** the rift floor the whole width of the zone, its walls rising out of frame; the vein thirty to forty
  yards across; the bridge of five arches, its crown nine yards above the water, its deck five yards wide, the gap
  fifteen yards over the deepest push.
- **The banks:** moor on the rift floor (the zone's game theme): heather in cushions, grey grass in tussocks, ash blown
  down from above, bracken on the drier slopes, one or two dead trees.
- **The rift walls:** the Hide cut through, layered like the cut edge of a thick hide: a dark crust of turf and ash, a
  pale layer of fibres, a yellowed layer, the dark wet below; root-threads hanging from the cut.
- **The vein:** clear, slow and thick; its bed visible, pale and ribbed, with folds across it like the valves inside a
  vein; the current coming in long slow pushes; pale threads waving in it. No weed and no life.
- **The floor is never bare:** heather litter, ash drifts, stones fallen from the walls, the ferry's broken gear,
  pilgrims' things set down where they waited.
- **Objects, by cause:** lantern-stones at the camp and where the road comes up from the Deep; the waystone back from
  the water; the statues as worn saints along the approach; the altar apart, older than the bridge.

**Landmarks**, each by the pit method:
- **The bridge, in two halves.**
  - **Shape:** the near half three arches and a springing, the far half one and a half, each ending in ragged voussoirs
    over air.
  - **Story by cause:** the names on the near parapet, oldest at the bank and newest by the break, the last half cut; on
    the far half, scaffold poles grey with age and tools lying on the deck (the masons' telling), while the broken edges
    are scoured smooth as if by water (the boatmen's). Build both, and let neither win.
  - **Material:** grey stone with lichen sized by age, the mortar washed out of the joints near the water.
  - **Light, from a hidden source:** the vein's slow pushes throw rippling light up onto the arches' undersides, as real
    water lights the underside of a bridge, pulsing on the beat.
  - **Age:** older than the oldest name.
- **Garrow's camp:** a ring of fieldstones on a terrace above the landing; the hearth raked and laid for morning,
  kindling crossed on cold ash, never lit; a lean-to of old ferry planks; a seat worn smooth. The one hearth on the road
  that is cold.
- **The landing:** a stone slip into the vein, a post where the bell hung, the ferry rope frayed off into the water.
- **The waiting dead:** a crowd at the water's edge below the landing, facing the far bank, in travelling clothes gone
  to rags. In the game they are the zone's Husks, standing like posts until three gather.
- **The bridge shrine:** two posts and a lintel at the near bridgehead with an empty hook, its guardian stone turned
  almost to face the water. The shrine-folk say such a stone turns to listen to something on the far side.

**Light and weather.** A strip of grey sky far up; the rift floor in shade most of the day, the moon on it only near
midnight. The lantern is the key light, and the vein's rippling light plays on every arch and on the walls near the
water. Rain falls straight down the rift, drips from the parapets, darkens the stone, rings on the vein's slow surface
and stains down the walls. The wind runs along the rift. No fog over the water.

**The god showing through.** Strongly: the vein in the open, warm and pushing; the Hide in section in the rift walls.

**Creatures.**
- **Tithe-Hands** (64): the zone is theirs. They come up out of the vein on their fingers, along the banks and out along
  both halves of the bridge. Put a pier or the parapet at your back.
- **Husks** (16): the waiting dead. Do not walk among them.
- **Wick-Saints of the Canopy** (11): hang under the arches and drop.
- **Pyre-Saints** (7): burning footprints on the heather; the vein douses them, so draw them to the water's edge.
- **Gasps** (6): drift under the arches.
- **Bellwethers** (4): toll twice and charge along the deck. Step aside before a pier and they strike stone; at the
  broken end a charge carries one over into the vein. When they toll, the waiting dead lift their heads.

**Sound.** The vein's low, slow surge in long pushes, thick water moving without splash; the wind along the rift;
fingers ticking on stone; the Bellwethers' tolls echoing from wall to wall; the waiting dead silent; at the cold hearth,
nothing.

**Keep out.** Fog over the water; red water or red light (the vein runs clear); a ferry or a ferryman (the Ferryman
belongs to another river, `../../in-game-texts/10-the-old-powers.md`); any way across; a fire in Garrow's hearth.

**Open questions.**
- **Where it lies:** the proposal here is a rift where the road comes up out of the Deep into the open air; the game
  reaches the bridge only through the Root Deep.
- **Its levels** (10–13) are lower than the Root Deep's (13–16), the only zone that leads to it.
- **The far bank:** the lore leaves it unknown. Show it only across the water, or not at all?
