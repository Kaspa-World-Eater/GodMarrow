# The Smugglers' Hold

*Act I · zone `smugglers_hold` · under the ground · creatures of levels 5–9 · borders: the Lower Catacombs · in
the game: the old procedural zone, to be remade by the bog's method · the Pale Order's Laying, which forbids
everything done here: `../../in-game-texts/02-the-pale-order.md`*

Under the catacombs lies the warren where bone was stolen out of the ossuary and sold. The Pale Order lays every bone
with its name and forbids any bone to be sold; the smugglers sold it anyway, as saints. They broke into the galleries
from below, took the walls down from behind so that the faces still stood, packed what they took in salt so it would
not get up, gave every bone a saint and a holy death (most of the saints made up), and carried them out by the old
salt-runners' tunnels to lowland chapels, healers and pilgrims, who paid in copper. The trade ended long ago and its
way out has fallen in; the only way in now is down from the Lower Catacombs. The knock on the false floor still gets
an answer. It should feel like a thieves' place gone quiet: cramped, salt-white, full of crates and lifted planks, with
the god's own hands come up through the floor. The god barely shows: a warmth and a give in the ground under the
floors.

**The one image:** salt heaped to the beams, a ledger open on its stand, and one plank in the floor lifted a finger's
width, as if something underneath had knocked and was waiting for the answer.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### What the bone-runner told the purifier

*A purifier of the Myriad took this down in her day-book after she had breathed the wrong out of the old man who told
it. She kept the words, she wrote, because words do not go out with the breath.*

You want it from the beginning, so here it is.

My father ran salt, and his father before him, down to the camps in the reeds with the toll left off. That is how we
knew the cellars. Under the old diggings there are rooms the salt-runners cut long ago, and they go further in under
the bone-galleries than anyone above knows. One night my uncle broke through a back wall at the end of them, and the
wall behind it was bone.

We sold none the first year. We were afraid of it. Then a chapel on the barrow road wanted a saint and had no copper
for a true one, and my uncle sold them a thigh-bone in a painted box, and told them it had belonged to a holy woman
who carried water to the dying in a year of thirst. He made her up on the walk there. They wept over it and paid him,
and the next spring three more chapels sent to ask for saints.

So we took the walls down from behind, a course at a time, so that the faces stood and nobody above could see. We
packed what we took in salt, because bone left lying in the dark gets up, as everybody knows, and salt keeps it lying.
We kept a ledger by weight and by saint. A skull was worth three thigh-bones. A hand was worth a skull, if every finger
was there. When I was old enough I wrote the saints' lives myself, and gave every bone a name and a holy death. I never
gave one of them its own.

Then the knocking began. We had a knock for the false floors, twice and then once, so that whoever was in the pit below
would know it was us. One night my uncle knocked, and the knock came back from under the floor, twice and then once,
and none of us was down there.

We left that floor alone. A month later it knocked first. My uncle lifted the plank to look, and something took hold
of his hand from underneath, and he would never say what. After that he would not lift a plank, or let us. We left the
salt and the crates and the ledger standing open, and went up the salt-runners' way, and pulled it down behind us.

You have breathed the wrong out of me. I can feel the place where it was. I want you to know that I am still not sorry
about the chapels. They wanted a saint and they got one, and they were kinder for it. I am sorry about the names.

### The finger of Saint Ysmay

*Found folded in a painted box on the altar of a chapel on the barrow road. On the front of the slip is the saint's
life as it was sold; on the back, in another hand, the chapel keeper's note.*

The slip reads: *Here is the finger of Saint Ysmay, who carried water. In the year of the great thirst, when the wells
gave up dust, she carried water to the dying in two pails on a yoke, a day's walk out and a day's walk back, until her
feet bled into the ash. When she could carry no more she lay down by the road with the last pail beside her and would
not touch it, and the next traveller who came that way had it and lived. Pray to her for the thirsty and the lost. She
will carry you.*

On the back the keeper wrote:

We kept her on our altar thirty years. Women with sick children prayed to her, and some of the children lived, and I
will not say that was nothing.

Then a carter told us that the chapel at the next crossing had her finger too, and so did the one past that, and a
chapel in the lowlands had her whole arm. We did not believe him until we went to look.

The year the knights came through counting, one of them asked to see her. He took the bone out of its box in two
hands, turned it to the window, and weighed it in his palm a long time. Then he told me it was the long bone of the
left little finger of a man, from the lowlands, and that it had been taken out of a wall by somebody who knew how to
take a wall down from behind. I asked what we should do. He said a bone prayed to that long has a name now, whether or
not it is the right one, and he would not be the one to take it away. He wrote a number on this slip and went out
quickly.

People on the road say the bone-runners sold worse than saints: bone ground fine for healers' salves, sold by the
pinch; bone sold to the knights themselves at their low gate, to make their count come out higher every year, the way
it does; and the mothers out of the crypt niches under the moor, a rib at a time. I do not know. I know what they sold
us. We keep her still.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Drawn from:** smugglers' caves and cellars (the caves at Hastings, the sandstone cellars under Nottingham, the
  tunnels behind Cornish coves); the chambers of the Wieliczka salt mine, every timber crusted white; mine adits with
  timber sets; and the relic trade of old Europe, bones sold in painted boxes with written slips, many of them false.
- **Two kinds of digging:** above, the old salt-runners' cellars, square rooms cut true with flagged floors and a chute
  down from where the salt came in; below and beyond, the smugglers' own workings, low rough tunnels (about 2.5 yards
  high, 2 to 4 wide) through brown-grey earth and soft rock, propped every few yards with timber sets (two posts and a
  cap), opening into bays.
- **The breakthroughs:** tunnels that end in the back of a catacomb wall: the loose bone heaped behind a sermon face,
  seen from behind, the face held up with props, gaps where courses were drawn out.
- **The false floors:** planks over pits, some lifted, some sagging; crates and sacks in the pits.
- **The salt:** heaped to the beams in the Salt Store, crusted white on every timber, crunching underfoot; crates of
  bone packed in it.
- **The floor is never bare:** salt grit and drifts; splinters, packing straw, rope ends; finger-bones and teeth
  spilled from the crates; saint-slips, written and blank; flakes of cheap gilt; a copper in the straw; footprints in
  the salt dust, and the prints of fingers.
- **The way out:** the oldest tunnel ends in a slope of rubble and broken timbers where the salt-runners' way was pulled
  down.

**Landmarks.**
1. **The False Floor.** *Shape:* the first room down from the catacombs, planked over a wide pit, one plank lifted a
   finger's width. *Cause:* one plank's edge dented in twos and ones by knocking knuckles; its underside scored; a crate
   dragged half over it and left. *Material:* oak gone grey and crusted with salt. *Light:* a faint steady light comes
   up between the planks, as if a lamp were still burning in the pit; nobody is seen to tend it. *Age:* the dents worn
   soft.
2. **The Ledger's Bay.** *Shape:* a bay with a tall standing desk, the ledger open on it, racks of painted boxes.
   *Cause:* the last entry broken off mid-line; boxes half painted; a pot of gilt dried hard with the brush in it;
   saints' slips in bundles, one written and the next blank. *Material:* plain wood under cheap gilt and brown paint;
   rag paper. *Light:* one of the zone's three lantern-stones hangs over the desk. *Age:* the ink faded brown, the gilt
   flaking.
3. **The Salt Store.** *Shape:* the largest chamber, salt heaped to the beams in white drifts, crates half buried, the
   pale ends of bone showing where a crate has burst. *Cause:* the salt poured down the old chute and slumped where
   crates were dug out; across the drifts, tracks of five-fingered prints going down into the floor. *Material:* salt,
   grey-white and crystalline, its glints held back, few and larger, as the pit's obsidian taught. *Light:* a thread of
   grey daylight down the old chute, its top out of sight, dusting the crests of the heaps. *Age:* a yellowed crust on
   the oldest heaps.

**Light and weather.** No weather, and the salt keeps everything dry. The lantern is the key light; three lantern-stones
(the False Floor, the Ledger's Bay, the Salt Store); the light under the floor; the thread down the chute. Salt is the
brightest thing here: keep it from glaring. No red light, no fog.

**The god showing through: a hint.** The ground in the pits under the false floors is warm and gives a little under the
hand, like a chest drawing breath. The knock. Nothing more.

**Creatures.** The game's mix: Tithe-Hands 30, Husks 25, Vein-Borers 13, Bloatlings 13, Wick-Saints 7, Gasps 4,
Wick-Saints of the Canopy 4, Weepers 3, Stalker Crones 2.
- **Tithe-Hands:** the god's own hands on their fingers, come up through the false floors; their prints are in the
  salt. Packs circle and dart in from behind: put a crate stack or a salt heap at your back.
- **Husks:** people who came down after the bone-runners for whatever was left, and stayed standing among the crates.
- **Vein-Borers:** under the earth floors and the pits; never under the salt-runners' flags.
- **Wick-Saints**, both kinds, come to the lantern-stones and the light under the floor; the few **Bloatlings, Gasps
  and Weepers** keep to the tunnels and behind the stacks.
- **Stalker Crones:** between the stacks, at the edge of the eye; they come when the pilgrim's back is turned.
- **By material:** crates and packing straw are dry and burn fast; salt heaps are soft low cover; the planks of a false
  floor break under force and drop whoever stands on them into the pit (a proposal); the timber props never break.

**Sound.** Salt crunching underfoot; timbers creaking under the weight above; a sack slumping; fingers tapping on
planks; now and then, from under a floor, two knocks and then one; through the breakthroughs, faint, the Matron
counting.

**Keep out.** Heaps of gold (it was a copper trade); pirates' flags and trappings; sacks of hide; real saints' names;
red light; fog.

**Open questions.**
- Salt as a ward: should an unbroken line of salt turn the god's pieces, as the lowland salt on a doorstep is meant to?
- The levels: the Hold's creatures (5–9) are far weaker than the catacombs' above (15–18), though it is reached only
  from them. Raise them, or reopen the salt-runners' way to a shallower zone?
