# The Drowned Fen

*Act I · zone `fen` · outdoors · creatures of levels 10–15 · borders: the Ashen Moor, the Upper Catacombs, the
Drowned Village, the Root Deep, the Pilgrim Road · in the game: the old procedural zone, to be remade by the bog's
method · the Myriad's myths, the grey year at the Well-Shrine among them: `../../in-game-texts/06-the-myriad.md`*

Below the Moor the land goes down into a wide country of pale reeds standing in water so clear it hardly seems to be
there. The old books call it the god's lymph. It does not wet: an arm put in to the shoulder comes out dry, and a
little lighter. Every reed bends the same way, whatever the wind, toward something nobody agrees on. Boatmen take
their fares at the Iron Hook; the Shrine Keepers keep their first shrine, the Well-Shrine, at the one clean pool; under
the deep water beyond it lies the Drowned Nave, its bell still hung. The Kneeling Arch bows out of the shallows toward
the edge of the world, and in the western pool one wet reed turns to point toward home. It should feel pale, quiet
and still. On the surveyor's chart of the god's face, the Fen lies at the throat.

**The one image:** a sea of pale dry reeds all leaning one way, a boat that seems to hang in the air over water too
clear to see, and far below its hull, a roof of slates.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### Pay at the Hook

*What a boatman told the pilgrims in his boat as he poled them across to the Well-Shrine. One of them wrote it down
that night in the reed-camp where they slept.*

Sit low and keep your hands in the boat. Trail them if you like; they will come back dry, and a little lighter. We say
the Fen takes the wet first, and then the rest. Nobody I know has found out what the rest is, and the ones who might
have are not in the boat.

You pay at the Hook, and not before. Before the Hook the Fen may still want you back, and a man should keep his copper
until he knows. There it is, leaning out of the shallows: black iron taller than a man, bent over at the top into a
barb, its foot down in the bed with something holding it. Every year the Shrine Keepers row out and hang a paper figure
on the barb, folded with its arms open, and every year the old ones slide a little further down the iron, under the
water. They go down as far as you can see, all of them dry, and the oldest still have their arms out.

Whose hook it is depends on who poles you. The reed-camp women say it is the well-hook out of the drowned village,
carried out here by the water when it spread; the old woman in the reed hut, whose father drew that well, says his
hook is in the well still, and laughs at them. My father said the knights from the white mountains left it, the year
they came dredging for our dead with hooks and chains, when one hook caught on something below and they cut the chain.
My grandfather said it was here before any village or any knight, and that is why we pay at it.

Don't look over the side past the Hook. The bed drops away, and you can see roofs, and further on one roof bigger than
the rest with a stair going down beside it. The Shrine Keepers count that stair at thirty-three steps; the children in
the camps sing thirty-four; I have heard thirty-six, from a little girl with no one near her. I keep a notch on my pole
for that roof, and I have cut it three times. Some crossings it is a hand deeper than I left it, and some a hand nearer.

That's the Hook. Copper in the box on the post. A boat has no side, and every boat has a fare.

### The bowed arch

*From the day-book of a purifier of the Myriad who wintered in the reed-camps: what the boatmen told her about the
arch, and then what a pilgrim told her.*

The boatmen sleep tied to the arch, because no boat tied there has ever been found in the morning cut loose or empty.
A thing that kneels, they say, is keeping watch.

It was the rose-arch of the chapel in the drowned village, and it stood straight until the water came up and reached
it. Then it bowed, slowly, all one evening, the way an old man gets down on his knees, and stopped, and has gone no
further since. It bows toward the edge of the world, where the sky sits lowest. Every night, the boatmen say, it is a
hand nearer to kneeling, and every morning it is back where it was.

Why it bows, they quarrel over. Most say it kneels toward the edge of the world in prayer, as their grandmothers did.
The old woman in the reed hut says it is looking for something past the edge, and waiting to be sure before it bows any
further.

This winter a pilgrim came through the camps who had walked up from the black sand at the land's end, where the shore
people lived in the great trees until they died. She stood in my boat under the arch a long while, and then told me it
bows the way she had come, exactly, toward the red water. Our elders teach that the red water is where breath goes
when it is spent, and that nobody living has seen it. I asked her what it was like. She said that it does not end, and
that it breathes.

### The reed in the western pool

*A reed-camp grandmother's telling, given to the children the night before they are first taken out to the western
pool. A purifier who had been a foundling of the shrine wrote it down, and added what happened when she stood there.*

Out in the western pool there is one reed that is wet.

Every other reed in the Fen is dry. You can stand in the water to your waist all day and
come out as dry as you went in, and the reeds are the same, dry from root to tassel, so dry they rattle. But in the
western pool one reed stands by itself, green as a new shoot, and your hand comes away wet from it. It is the one place
in the Fen where the water remembers how to be water.

Everything drifts to it. The paper figures you set on the water float high and go west against the current, every
one, and gather round its foot.

Stand by it, and it turns its head, slowly, the way a sleeper turns over, and points toward the place where you were
born. Pilgrims come from everywhere to stand by it and weep. A man from the moor once stood by it and laughed for joy,
and went home that same day.

The old woman of the reed hut has stood by it. It points down.

The purifier wrote under it: *I was left on the step of the shrine as a baby, in a hard frost, and nobody came back for
me. When I was folded I stood by the reed, as every novice does. It turned, and pointed past me across the water, at
the shrine gate. The other novices it pointed far away, to homes they had left. I do not know whether that means
the gate is where I was born, or that the gate was all the home it could find for me.*

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Drawn from:** the Everglades' sheet of clear water through saw-sedge; the spring-fed fens of Norfolk, with pale marl
  pools; the Broads' reed beds and lone churches.
- **The water:** clear, still and flat, knee-deep over most of the Fen and 1.5 to 3 yards in the channels, over a pale
  marl bed with drowned things lying sharp on it. Seen through, never mirrored; no sound, no wake. Above the waterline
  everything has weathered; below it everything is as clean as the day it went under.
- **The plants:** common reed in vast beds 3 to 4 yards high; saw-sedge, reedmace, bog-rush; bladderwort in the pools;
  yellow flag on the margins; alder carr toward the Wood. The reeds are dry, bleached and papery, and every stem leans
  the same way whatever the wind (a proposal: toward the Nave's ring).
- **People's things:** board walks on piles; the reed-camps' stilt huts, mostly empty; the Fen stair where the Moor
  road comes down, with the Fen-stair ring, the zone's waystone, half drowned beside it. The four shrines are the small
  gods' wayside shrines; the two statues, guardian stones turned a little from the road.
- **The ground stays alive:** cut reed, stems, tassels, paper scraps and rope ends on the walks; litter on the bed.

**Landmarks.**
1. **The Well-Shrine.** A round pool, clearer and paler than the rest, where the water wells up in a slow boil of white
   sand; the shrine on piles at its edge, its gate in the shallows, the guardian stone a finger's width off the road,
   the step worn hollow by sitting. Grey timber, reed thatch, white paper. *Light:* a lamp nobody sees, behind the
   paper walls, glowing down into the pool.
2. **The Drowned Nave.** Under the deep water beyond the shrine: a broken slate roof, the bell still hung, pews, an
   altar with a water-glass, the stair; behind the altar a ring of stones leaning in like teeth, dry ash rising and
   settling, and a road going down. The reed-camps say it was the drowned village's chapel; the Shrine Keepers say it
   was under the water before any village. *Light:* dull amber from the ring.
3. **The Kneeling Arch.** A rose window's stone frame alone in the shallows, bowed forty degrees toward the land's end,
   its piers cracked on the bent side, iron mooring rings worn bright, lichen above the water. *Light:* a boatman's lamp
   hung under the crown, hidden by the arch's face.
4. **The Iron Hook.** Black iron taller than a man, every year's paper figure hung down its shank under the water, the
   oldest deepest; rust-brown above the water, black below; a fare-post and its box beside it. *Light:* a shuttered lamp
   in the post's hood, so the figures show white in a column going down.
5. **The Reed That Points Home.** One green reed in the round western pool, paper figures gathered at its foot, turning
   slowly toward whoever stands near: the one wet highlight in the Fen.

**Light and weather.** Overcast by day, moonlit at night, as in the bog. The lantern's pool goes down through the water
onto the bed. Rain wets the reeds and timbers; on the Fen it beads and rolls in the wind (a proposal). No fog, no
mirror, no red light.

**The god showing through: more than at the start.** The water is its lymph. In the deep channels the marl gives way to
pale skin, pored, with fine hairs swaying like weed, and pale smooth swellings rise from the bed, warm and slowly
pulsing (a proposal: the throat's glands).

**Creatures.** The game's mix: Drowned Husks 179, Wick-Saints 73, Mire Vein-Borers 45, Gravebloats 35, Bloatlings 34,
Pyre-Saints 32, Weepers 26, Mire Gasps 20, Bellwethers 13, Wick-Saints of the Canopy 8.
- **Drowned Husks:** the dead given to the water and pilgrims who waded too long, standing in the shallows until three
  gather. **Wick-Saints** come to the lamps at dusk; those **of the Canopy** keep to the carr.
- **Mire Vein-Borers:** their bubbles show through the clear water a moment early; never under the walks. What
  **Gravebloats and Bloatlings** spill floats unmixed on the dry water.
- **Pyre-Saints:** the dry reed beds go up like straw; draw them into open water, which closes over their flame (a
  proposal: the dry water douses, but leaves nothing wet, so the reeds still burn; in rain they only smoulder).
- **Weepers:** reeds do not stop needles; stone and the shrine's piles do. **Mire Gasps:** the wrong breath the Shrine
  Keepers breathed into the Fen for two centuries, still coming up. **Bellwethers:** heard across the water first; put
  an arch pier at your back and step aside on the second toll.

**Sound.** The dry rattle of the reeds; no lapping or splash, so wading is near silent; the pole knocking on the Nave's
roof; paper strips ticking on bells; now and then every bell in the Fen striking once together; insects at dusk.

**Keep out.** Fog; a mirror or black water (the bog's); any life in the water; real shrine crests or script; red light;
anything wet but the one reed.

**Open questions.**
- Does the dry water douse fire, as proposed? Does rain bead on it?
- Is the Nave ever entered, down its stair?
- The game hangs the Reed's lantern in the Drowned Village; the lore puts the Reed here.
