# The Hollow Crypt

*Act I · zone `crypt`, the Hollow Crypt, which pilgrims also call the Rib Crypts and the old tongue calls Dur-nang, "the
inside grave" · under the ground · creatures of levels 9–12 · borders: the Ashen Moor, the Fallen Monastery, the Hollow
Wood · in the game: the old procedural crypt map (109 × 109) with the Carrion Warden's room, to be remade by the bog's
method · the singing under the floor: the Codex's "The Hymn Under the Long Candle"*

Under the edge of the Moor the Hollow Crypt goes in between the god's ribs: nine galleries, one to each space between
two ribs. The ribs are its walls, curved walls of old bone that lean in overhead until the lantern loses them. The
Tithed of the Moor laid their mothers in niches cut in those walls, until what the grandmothers call the Great Standing,
when one morning every niche was empty. After it the Carrion Warden, who had been set to guard the place, forgot what he
was guarding and began to gather up whatever the Tithed laid there, and the Moor has laid no mother in it since. The
flagstones lift and settle under the boot, slow and even, because something below is breathing, and on some nights there
is singing through the floor.

It is the most inside place on the act's near side, the first where the god is plainly the building. It should feel
enormous, reverent and close at once: a grave as big as a great hall, made for mothers.

**The one image:** a gallery between two ribs that curve up out of the light, empty niches in rows along both, and the
flagstones lifting under the pilgrim's feet.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### Why the mothers went inside

*Told at a banked fire on the Moor by a grandmother to a girl about to be married, as the grandmothers there tell it. A
copyist who sat at the same fire wrote it down from memory the next day.*

When we came out of the god, we came out of the warm into the cold, and the mothers carried the children out. You know
that part. What you don't know is that the mothers were the ones who wanted to go back.

So when the first of them died, out here on the cheek, her children carried her back in. Not all the way; they couldn't
find the way all the way. They went in under the edge of the Moor, between the ribs, as far as they could go and still
find their way out again, and they laid her there in the dark where it was warm. That was the first mother of the inside
grave. After her every mother of the Moor was laid there, in a place cut for her in the bone. The fathers went into the
barrows, and the mothers went inside. They had carried us, and the god had carried them, so they went back to be
carried.

There was a candle burning at the door the first time they carried a mother in. Nobody had lit it. It is still burning,
and we call it the Long Candle, and it leans toward you when you come, as if it wants to hear who you are. My
grandmother said that meant somebody had been in there before us, keeping a light. She would never say who.

Then, in my grandmother's grandmother's time, the sextons went down one morning to lay a mother and found every place in
all nine galleries empty, and not a bone left. That is the Great Standing. Some say the mothers got up in the night, all
together, and walked out. If they walked out, I say, where did they go? Nobody on the Moor ever met them. My grandmother
said they went in, further, the way we never could, because they were tired of waiting to be carried. Others say they
were taken, and look at the knights when they say it, or at the brothers in the house below the crypts, who were always
counting. Most will not speak of it at all.

After that the sextons laid new mothers in the empty places, and then the Warden began to walk, and you know that part
too. Now the mothers go into the barrows with everyone else. I will go into the barrows, and so will your mother, and so
will you, girl, unless somebody changes it. Don't let them tell you it is the same.

### The last sexton

*Set down by a copyist from the words of the last sexton of the Hollow Crypt, an old man who would no longer go past
its threshold.*

When I was a boy and my father was sexton, the Warden stood inside the first gallery, just past the Long Candle, and did
not move. He was very tall even stooping, and his head was shut up in an iron cage, and he stood with his arms hanging,
the way a post stands. My father carried the mothers past him and laid them in their places and swept and came out
again, and the Warden never so much as turned his cage. We called him the Warden because nobody knew any other name for
him.

Nobody can tell you who set him there. The grandmothers say our own people asked him to stand there, long ago, to keep
the mothers from the knights' carts. The knights say their Order set him there, to keep the mothers until they could be
counted. My father thought he was there first, before any mother, and that the first children carried their mother in
past him because there was no other way in.

The year after the Great Standing, the sextons laid three new mothers in the empty places. In the morning the Warden was
not at the door. We found him in the ninth gallery, walking. He walked all that day and all that night, up one gallery
and down the next, his cage turning from side to side as if he had lost something and could not remember what. The three
new places were open, and the three new mothers were gone.

He had them on his back. He had sewn a sack out of grave-cloths, and they were in it, and he carried it the way a man
carries his own bedding, with a hand hanging out at the seam. Every mother we laid after that went into the sack. When
it was full he hung it from hooks he drove into the ribs of the ninth gallery, and sewed another, and went on walking.

So we stopped. I was the last to lay a mother in there, and I took her out again the same night, before he came round,
and carried her to the barrows. I have not been past the candle since.

Once a year I go to the Counting Wall by the door, make a mark, and light the candles there, as my father did. I don't
know what we are counting. I know he is still keeping them. I think it is the only way he remembers.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Inside the god, under the Moor's edge.** Draw the ribs from the human rib cage: each rib flattened and curved,
  smooth outside, its lower inner edge grooved where the vessels ran (the anatomists' costal groove; the lore's "long
  groove behind the ribs" lies below the galleries). Ten ribs bound the nine galleries. The niches come from the
  shelf-graves of the Roman catacombs.
- **True scale:** each rib a wall of bone 4 to 6 yards thick; each gallery 15 to 25 yards across, curving down and away
  from the mouth; the ribs lost in the dark overhead at 15 to 20 yards; niches in rows of three up the rib faces, each
  2.2 yards long.
- **The floor is never bare:** flagstones laid between the ribs, lifting and settling on a slow breath as real height
  (the ground log's one true detail), grey grease and dust in their joints; a walked line polished down each gallery;
  salt at every niche lip; grave-cloth rags; wax runs and stubs; chalk dust under the Counting Wall; ash blown in at the
  mouth.

**Landmarks.**
- **The Long Candle** (the threshold of the first gallery):
  - *Shape:* one candle as thick as a man's waist on a mound of its own runs wider than a cart, its small flame leaning
    toward whoever comes.
  - *Story by cause:* the mound is centuries of runs, each a pale skin over the last, the oldest yellow-grey and hard as
    stone; the flags round it are worn by those who knelt; the candle is no shorter than anyone remembers.
  - *Light:* the one warm standing light in the crypt; it reaches the gallery's mouth and nothing beyond.
  - *Age:* older than the Tithed's coming out, the grandmothers say; its wax gone grey and amber.
- **The Counting Wall** (in the mouth, before the candle): a smooth face of rib covered, as high as an arm can reach,
  with tally marks in groups of a hundred and eight. Votive stubs stand on the ledge below, and lanterns burn low and
  steady before it, as if listening.
- **The Weeping Rib:** a slow bead of clear, warm water seeps from a crack, runs down the bone in a dark glossy line and
  has worn a basin in the flags. The grandmothers say it weeps for the mothers; a knight said a cut bone is a mourning
  bone.
- **The Empty Reliquary of Sister Un:** at the head of the stair that goes down from the ninth gallery into the dark
  where the singing comes from, a stone box on a shelf, its lid beside it. No dust settles where it rested: a clean
  shape on the shelf, a clean path down the stair, and the top of a milestone on the Pilgrim Road, where it lay a night
  on its way (`pilgrim-road.md`). Nobody can say who Sister Un was. The Shrine Keepers claim her, so do the Veiled
  Crone's circles, and the grandmothers say only that she was carried down and never up.
- **The Carrion Warden's floor** (the far end of the ninth gallery): the ribs hung with sacks sewn of grave-cloths, on
  iron hooks driven into the bone; the nearest niches torn open; a ring worn into the flags by his round, swept clean by
  his feet and his chain; a sexton's broom and lantern where they were dropped. It is the darkest place in the crypt,
  and its arena is his walked round, worn into the stone.

**Light and weather.** The lantern is the key light, and the game's one lantern stands in the mouth. Weather reaches the
mouth only: ash drifts in on the Moor's wind, and rain seeping down from the Moor runs in dark lines down the ribs and
drips from their curve. Blacks are shapes: the ribs' tops overhead, the stair down. No fog, no red light.

**The god showing through: the building is the god.** The ribs are the walls and the floor breathes; where a flag has
cracked and lifted, the dark, dry flesh between the ribs shows. No eyes and no veins: the bone and the breath are
enough.

**Creatures** (the game's mix):
- **Husks (32).**
- **Ossuary Wardens (25):** knights of bone behind shields of skulls, saluting one another and never the pilgrim. Whose
  they are is the same question as whose the Warden is. They block from the front; the galleries are wide, so go round.
- **Gravebloats (21):** the god's pieces that want us back inside; in the niche rows, let them burst among the Husks.
- **Gasps (20):** through the ribs as if bone were mist. They shy from light, so the Long Candle is safe ground.
- **Choristers (14):** veils that sing as they drift, a dirge that drags at the feet, thickest at the stair head. Some
  say they are the singing under the floor, come up where it is thin.
- **A Kneeler (1):** in the mouth, on its knees, facing in.
- **The Carrion Warden:** the master, walking his round with the sack on his back. He slams and charges, and when hard
  pressed calls up the dead, Wardens and Husks.
- **Objects:** three waystones (rings of fang-stones come up through the flags, the locked craggy floor round them), one
  lantern, two chests. Bone and flagstone break shots; grave-cloths and sacks burn.

**Sound.** The flags lifting and settling, a long grinding sigh of stone on stone; on still nights, singing through the
floor, three low notes going down and then a waiting; the Wardens' shields knocking as they salute; far down the ninth
gallery, a chain dragging.

**Keep out.**
- Food framing about the Warden: no butcher's tools, slaughter hooks or aprons, no gnawed bones. He gathers and keeps.
- ~~"Ivory" in the game's entry line~~ (now "dry as an old tooth").
- The Choir as figures: it is only ever heard.
- Marrow Duelists, which belong to the catacombs.
- Fog; red light.
