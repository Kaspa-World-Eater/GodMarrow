# The Burnt Heath

*Act I · zone `burnt_heath` · outdoors, 152 × 152 · creatures of levels 4–6 · borders: the Sighing Ridge, the Ash
Shore, Fern Gully · in the game: the old procedural map (`burnt_heath_s1001` to `s1020`), to be remade by the bog's
method*

Off the Ridge the land runs down into a wide heath burned to black wire. It burned for a year after the god died, and
nobody lit it, and it has never quite stopped: the wind off the Ridge drives every fire across it the same way, so the
Heath is a patchwork of burns of every age in long tongues that all point downwind, toward the sea. Pilgrims cross it
by one road and sleep at one banked fire. In the corner the road bends away from, seven great horned carcasses lie in a
ring, all facing the same way, and no lantern will go nearer than its edge.

The Heath is the god's skin, burned, with one old wound under the seven that closed. It should feel wide, dry, smoky
and watched: there is always a fire somewhere on it.

**The one image:** black wire heath to the edge of sight, one small banked fire with sleepers round it whose feet all
point one way, and far off in the corner, seven great horned shapes lying in a ring.

## Found in the world

### Three nights at the Heath fire

*A letter from a pilgrim to her sister in the Moor camps, written at the keeper's fire on the Burnt Heath and carried
back up the road by a carter. Her sister kept it in her salt tin.*

The fire is by the road, short of the fork, banked so low you cannot see it from twenty paces, with a reed tent behind
it and the sleepers lying round it. The keeper is called Aud, as her mother was and her grandmother, and she tells her
rules to everyone who sits down.

The fire stays banked, and nobody feeds it but her. Once a man fed it an armful of heath-brush to be kind, and it went
up so high you could see the far corner of the Heath by it, and everybody sat very quiet until it came down. She would
not say what they saw. You sleep with your feet toward the road. If you wake with them pointing some other way, you turn
over, and you don't tell her which way you were lying. If someone calls your name from outside the tent, you answer
only if they say it wrong. "Your own people say your name wrong," she said. "They always did."

At the fork stands the saint, a stone woman with no hands. Pilgrims leave a pinch of ash in her lap for a safe road, and
in the morning the lap is clean, and Aud has never swept it. When Aud's mother was a girl the saint stood twenty paces
back from the road. It is twelve now. Aud paces it every spring and says nothing, as her mother did.

At the edge of the Heath, cages hang on posts. The ones in them came up out of the rings wrong. You step into a ring
whole and come up somewhere else whole, mostly; these came up with a hand too many, or with somebody else's way of
looking at a fire. They walk out to the edge by themselves and ask to be hung up, and they ask so nicely that it is
done. They ask Aud for a pinch of ash in a cup, the way the pilgrims give it to the saint, and they thank her for it.
The one with the extra hand thanks her twice. She counts the cages at dusk and again at dark. Mostly the numbers agree.

On the third morning I woke with my feet pointing at the far corner. I turned over before she saw, or I think she
didn't see. She was looking at all our feet when I sat up, and she said nothing.

I am going on in the morning. I am telling you which way I was lying, so that somebody knows.

### The seven

*Set down by a copyist who walked down to the Heath to hear the tellings of the seven from the people who
first told them, and found them all dead but the keeper of the fire. He wrote what she gave him.*

In the corner of the Heath that the road bends away from, seven great horned carcasses lie in a ring, each bigger than
a tent, each with its limbs folded under it the way a person kneels, and every one facing the same way, into the wind.
Round them the skulls are heaped like stones cleared off a field, and those skulls are smaller, and nobody knows whose.
The ground inside the ring is darker than the Heath and flatter than ground lies, and nothing grows on it.

The keeper gives four tellings of the seven. Old Garrow said they were the god's first thoughts, sent out walking to
carry something, and that they lay down when they got where they were going. Brannoc the smith's girl said they knelt
to something coming up. A knight of the Pale Order sat at the fire and said they lay down to hold a door shut with
their weight, and then he went out and counted their bones twice. He wrote the first number
in his book and would not say the second. The keeper knows it all the same: he dropped his book in the ash going out,
and she read it before she gave it back, and the second number was the first with one more figure on the end. The
keeper's mother had it in six words, and the pilgrims carry those away: the ground between them was opened once, and
closed.

Garrow went out to the ring in the last year of his life. He left his lantern at the edge, where it stopped, and went
in without it. He came back at grey light and said there was a seat under the ground there, grey, of the same stone as
the plinths of the rings and stained at its foot, and someone sitting in it with his hands on his knees, the way a man
waits at a hearing for his name to be called: very patient, waiting to be asked. He said there were seven places at its
feet, all empty, each the shape of one of the seven lying down. Then he lay down by the fire with his feet toward the
corner, and the keeper let him.

She believes the part about the patient one. Garrow lied all his life and lied well, she says, but he was never
patient a day of it, and he could not have made that up. She does not believe the empty places. She has paced the
ring, and there are seven. There have always been seven.

### How the Heath caught

*Two tellings of the year-long fire, the carters' and the keeper's, written one under the other on a single leaf by
someone who heard both and would not choose.*

The carters who cross the Ridge say that in the year after the god died the Moor was hotter under its ash than it has
been since. One night the wind over the Ridge came harder than it had ever come, one long breath that did not stop,
and it lifted hot ash off the Moor in sparks and carried them over the crest. The heath below was dry, and it caught,
and the wind fed it for a year. That is why every burn on the Heath runs the same way, toward the sea: the
wind drove the first fire, and it has driven every fire since.

The keeper tells it with her hand flat on the ground. Nobody lit the Heath, she says, and nothing carried fire to it. It
came up. Her grandmother's grandmother said the Heath burned the way a candle burns, from underneath, and the heather
was only the wick. Dig a spade's depth where the burn is blackest and the earth is warm and greasy in the hand, and if
you leave the hole open overnight it is smoking by morning. The wind, she says, only ever told the fire which way to go.

Both tellings agree that the Pyre-Saints walked in the burning that year, and walk still. The carters say they were the
faithful of some procession who walked into the fire to pray it out, and the fire took their prayers and kept them
burning. The keeper says nobody has ever known whose faithful they were, and that anyone who tells you is selling
something. Someone once chalked the milestone at the fork, then took a stone and cracked it across the figure. The
carters say it was the keeper's mother. The keeper says she does not know who, and that on the Heath, not knowing the
distance is a mercy.

## For building it

**The land.** Real heath after fire: the Dorset heaths Hardy called Egdon, which burn in dry summers and smoulder for
weeks in the peat, and the northern heather moors, burned in strips into a quilt of ages.
- **The burns, by cause:** every burn a long tongue pointing downwind, each age with its own ground: fresh black char
  with white ash ghosts in the shapes of what burned (chapter 2); then fire moss in red-brown and olive mats and
  fireweed in magenta spikes; then young heather; then old heather burned to wire, black stems a hand to a knee high.
  Charred gorse and birch snags stand among them.
- **The shallows:** peat pools in the hollows, edged with cotton grass. Every burn bends round them, and the road keeps
  by them.
- **The floor stays alive:** charcoal stems in many blacks and greys, white and grey ash, cinders, fire-moss green,
  seedlings, lichen, stones, bone chips. Never a flat black.

**Landmarks, by the pit method.**
- **The seven.** The old map's homage to Diablo II lives on only in their arrangement.
  - **Shape:** a ring about 30 yards across; seven carcasses 7 to 9 yards long, limbs folded under like kneeling
    people, heads raised toward the Ridge, great horns sweeping back from each brow; skull heaps between them.
  - **Cause:** hide gone to grey leather, split along the backs where bone shows; ash banked upwind; fires parting
    round the ring, so a long tail of old, unburned heather runs from it downwind, the only old heath on the map; the
    inner ground flat, crossed by one faint puckered seam where it closed.
  - **Material:** horn flaking at the tips, grey leather, bone, small white skulls, and inner ground dark, faintly
    glossy and warm, like old scar.
  - **Light:** the lantern stops at the edge, so inside the ring the pilgrim walks by the sky alone. The only sign of
    what lies below: the inner ground steams in rain, and nothing else on the Heath does.
  - **Age:** older than the fire, which burned round them. No likeness to any real creature: the old map's carcass and
    skull sprites go.
- **The keeper's fire,** short of the fork: banked in fire-cracked stones, a tallowed reed tent, the sleepers' hollows
  worn into the ground, all feet to the road. Low, never high.
- **The saint** (a statue): a handless stone woman with a lap of ash, twelve paces from the road, the older road bed
  faint twenty paces out.
- **The cages:** gibbet cages on posts at the Heath's edge, the ones in them alive and quiet; one swings with no wind.
- **The ash-circle** (the altar): white ash where a Pyre-Saint knelt to burn, its centre fused to dark glass in the
  shape of two knees, still warm.

**Light and weather.** Overcast, blacks and greys under a pale sky. Smoke rises in thin threads from smouldering
patches and leans with the wind, never pooling. Rain hisses on the smoulders; the char darkens and shines. Cinders lift
in the pilgrim's wake and follow a little way. Warm light comes only from real fires, amber, never red. The lantern is
the key light.

**The god showing through: more than on the Ridge.** The warmth; the greasy black earth under the deepest burns, which
smokes when opened; the seam in the ring; the seven.

**Creatures.** Weeper 28, Husk 18, Wick-Saint 15, Bloatling 13, Wick-Saint of the Canopy 12, Vein-Borer 6, Pyre-Saint 2.
- **Weepers,** the most, are the Heath's mourners, keeping their distance behind stones, carcasses and cages.
- **Wick-Saints** of both kinds come to fire as moths do, over smoulders and burning Pyre-Saints and any fire built
  high, which is why the keeper banks hers.
- **Husks** and **Bloatlings** keep to the road; **Vein-Borers** come up through soft peat; the two **Pyre-Saints**
  are rare, and known in every telling.
- **In a fight:** old heather burns fast downwind, but fresh char is a firebreak; the peat pools douse a Pyre-Saint;
  stones and the seven stop shots; smouldering ground burns the feet, drawn plainly.

**Sound.** The wind with its pauses; wire heather crackling underfoot; rain hissing on hot ground; cages creaking;
moths at the fire; at night, the road moving, which the keeper says sounds like a sheet drawn slowly off a bed.

**Keep out.** Any likeness to a real creature (the old map's sprites were cattle), and the word "herd" in the world; the
Hollow King (parked); fog; red light; crows; the old generator's shared landmarks.
