# The Bog-Witch's Shack

*Act I · zone `bogwitch_shack` · outdoors · creatures of levels 7–10 · borders: the Sunken Bog, the Fallen Monastery ·
in the game: the old procedural zone (104 × 104 yards), to be remade by the bog's method · the bog it stands in:
`sunken-bog.md` · the bog's serpent: `../../in-game-texts/09-the-long-back.md`*

Where the Sunken Bog reaches its landward edge and the old road begins to climb toward the Fallen Monastery, one hump of
firm ground stands out of the black water, and on it a crooked shack. Smoke goes up from its chimney, and there is no
fire in it. The stone before its door is worn into two hollows by knees. This was the house of Hesk the Tallow-Mother,
the bog-witch who sold cures for years off a life and rendered the drowned into candles that walk. Whether it is still
her house, nobody agrees.

The place should feel like the end of a bad errand: a long wade through the bog's margin toward the one dry thing in
sight, and the dry thing is worse than the water. The god showing through is the Hide's, more than at the start; the
serpent god's bones belong to the bog next door.

**The one image:** a crooked black shack on the only hump in the bog, its smoke rising straight up through the rain, and
before the door a stone worn into two hollows by knees.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### The Tallow-Mother

*Told by a bog guide, one of those who keep the Long Back, to pilgrims waiting out the rain at the bog's landward edge.
One of the pilgrims wrote it down.*

There are a dozen Hesks in the wet country. When we lower our voices, we mean this one.

She lived on the hump at the land end of the bog, in the shack you can see from here when the rain thins, the one with
the smoke going up from it. My grandmother's grandmother knew her as an old woman. So did my grandmother. That tells you
most of what you need to know.

She sold cures, and her price was years. A year for a fever, three for a birth gone wrong, more for things she would not
name. You knelt on her doorstone and asked, and she wrote what you owed in her ledger, which is written on skin, and
nobody who has seen it will say whose. Then she gave you the cure, and it worked. People forget that part when they tell
it. Every cure she ever sold worked.

People disagree about what she did with the years. My mother said she lived on them, and that is why she was old for so
long. The pilgrims say she put them into her candles.

Because she made candles. The bog keeps its drowned. You will have seen them in the turf banks, if you looked: a hand in
the cut face, a shoulder, brown and whole, a hundred years down. Down there in the wet, where no air gets in, the fat of
the drowned goes pale and hard, like wax. The turf-cutters call it grave-wax and will not touch it. Hesk paid them to
bring it to her. She rendered it in her vat and poured it, and her candles burned slow and gave a good light, and some
of them got up and walked.

You will see them on the hump, the ones that burn as they go and leave their footprints smoking in the wet. The pilgrims
call them saints, martyrs out of the burnt country who walk wherever there is something to burn. We call them Hesk's
candles. We have the better right to name them; we see them every night.

Is she dead? An old woman who had been a warden once told my mother she felt Hesk go out, all her candles at once, like
a tooth pulled. But the candles still walk. The smoke still goes up from the chimney, and nobody lights a fire under it.
And the doorstone is still being worn. I have seen it wet in the hollows of a morning, when nobody I know had been up
there.

I will take you to the foot of the hump, if you have something to ask her. Nobody guides you up it. You go up alone, the
way people always did, and you kneel.

### A year for the fever

*A letter from a woman of the Monastery road to her sister in the lowlands. It was never sent. It turned up years later
in the lining of a pilgrim's coat, far from the bog, and nobody knows how it got there.*

Sister,

You will have heard that Aled lived. I want you to know what it cost, and that I do not grudge it.

The fever went on nine days, and nothing from the road did any good, and on the ninth night I wrapped him and carried
him down into the bog. The guides would only take me to the foot of the hump. I went up alone in the dark, with him
burning in my arms.

The shack is smaller than you would think, and blacker. The boards are dark and slick to the touch, as if they had been
greased. Charms hang from the eaves on strings, knuckle-bones and teeth and a child's shoe, and every one of them was
turning, slowly, though there was no wind.

Before the door there is a flat stone with two hollows worn in it, side by side. I knelt, and my knees went into them as
if they had been worn for me.

She did not come out. The door opened a crack, and a voice asked what I wanted, and I told it. It asked how old I was,
and I told it that too. Then it said one year, and I said yes, and something inside wrote for a long time.

I saw in past the door. It was warm in there, and light, and there was no fire. A great iron vat stood on a cold hearth,
and the stuff in it was moving, slowly, as if it were on the boil. There were candles everywhere, on the shelves and on
the floor and hanging by their wicks from long rods, hundreds of them, pale as tallow, and none of them lit. The ones on
the floor stood in rows, like people waiting to be called.

A little clay bottle came out to me through the crack. I wet his lips with it, and he was cool before I was down off the
hump.

Here is what I want you to know. Everyone says she takes the year off the end of your life, so that you die a year
sooner. I believed it, and I made my peace with it on the way down.

She took it out of the middle. I knew as soon as I was home. I can count my life up to the spring I was married, and
then I am married a year, and I do not know how I got there. You were at the wedding. Tell me about it in your next
letter, because I cannot.

I do not grudge it. He is asleep in the next room, and he is cool.

## For building it

The zone is the old procedural map. Remake it by the bog's method (`sunken-bog.md`,
`tools/landkit/passes/spine_path.md`): causes first, one landmark by the pit method, every object a new design.

**The land.** The edge of a real raised bog where it meets firm ground: the Irish midland bogs (Clara, Mongan) and the
peat moors of the Somerset Levels.
- **The margin:** the wet moat round a raised bog, where bog water meets ground water. Rushes and sedge, black pools
  lying level, alder and willow carr on root-islands, most of the small trees dead and grey.
- **The hump:** a low island of firm ground about thirty yards across and three or four yards above the water. Heather
  and purple moor grass on its crown, the shack on top, turf stacked to dry on its flanks.
- **The turf banks:** cut by hand into the peat, faces a yard or two high, spade-stepped, black and wet. In some of them
  the drowned show, as real turf-cutters find bog bodies: a hand, a shoulder, brown and whole.
- **Bog-oak:** black, iron-hard trunks and stumps dug out of the peat and left lying, older than anything standing.
- **The floor is never bare:** sphagnum in hummock and hollow (rust, olive, ochre), cotton grass, bog asphodel, sundew,
  heather, turf crumbs and spoil, and near the shack dropped wicks and spatters of wax.
- **The road out:** beyond the hump, old flagstones under the peat, climbing toward the Fallen Monastery.
- **Objects, by cause:** the lantern-stone where the guides stop, at the hump's foot; a waystone on the bog side and one
  on the road; a forgotten wayside shrine by the turf banks; the statue a saint at the foot of the hump path, its face
  kissed away; the altar apart on the bog side, older than the shack.

**The landmark: the shack**, by the pit method (`tools/art_study/reports/05-pit-of-offering.md`):
- **Shape:** crooked and leaning downhill, about five yards by four, eaves at two yards, a tall chimney of stacked stone
  and turf leaning the other way.
- **Story by cause:**
  - built of boards from drowned houses and of bog-oak, so no two match;
  - every board dark and slick with tallow;
  - charms on cords from the eaves: knuckle-bones, teeth, a child's shoe;
  - the doorstone, a flat slab worn into two smooth hollows side by side by generations of kneeling, wet in the hollows;
  - inside: the rendering vat on a cold hearth, rods hung with half-dipped candles, moulds, candles standing in rows on
    the floor, and the ledger somewhere, never shown clearly.
- **Material:** tallow-black wood; pale grave-wax, a little translucent at its edges; iron gone black; turf.
- **Light, from a hidden source:** no fire anywhere. A warm light leaks from the cracks round the door and the shutter
  and falls across the doorstone's hollows. Nobody sees what makes it; the candles inside are all unlit.
- **Age:** older than three generations of guides. The doorstone is the oldest thing on the hump.
- **Its wrongness, as living layers:** the smoke rises straight up, untouched by the one wind; the charms turn slowly
  against it; the vat's surface moves with no fire under it.

**Light and weather.** Overcast by day and moonlit at night, the black pools mirroring both. The lantern is the key
light. Rain rings on the pools, runs in fat beads down the tallow-slick boards (water will not sit on tallow), darkens
the turf stacks on their weather side and fills the doorstone's hollows. The smoke ignores the rain as it ignores the
wind. No fog over the water.

**The god showing through.** More than at the start, less than in the bog's heart:
- the drowned in the turf banks;
- the hump, dry when nothing round it is, and warm underfoot;
- the god's pale tendrils round the oldest bog-oak, with a dim pustule or two;
- of the serpent god, only a vertebra or two breaking the water on the bog side.

**Creatures.**
- **Drowned Husks** (18): the bog's kept dead, up out of the pools and the turf banks. Fight them on the narrow turf
  paths, where they come one at a time.
- **Pyre-Saints** (12), "Hesk's candles" to the bog folk. Their fire hisses in the wet. Their footprints light the dry
  turf stacks and the heather on the hump, which burn long and hot; any pool puts them out, so here the water is the
  safe ground.
- **Gravebloats** (6): walking stomachs, drawn to the smoke; let them burst among the Husks.
- **Wick-Saints** (5): the moths that wear white faces, come at dusk to the light in the shack's cracks.
- **Weepers** (4): faceless mourners standing at the hump's edge, facing the door, as if waiting their turn to kneel.
  Their bone needles break on bog-oak and on the shack's walls.
- **By material:** turf stacks give waist-high cover and burn; bog-oak stops shots and will not burn; water douses.

**Sound.** The suck and release of mud; the vat's slow bubbling with no fire under it; the charms clicking as they turn;
tallow dripping; Pyre-Saints hissing in the wet; insects over the water; far tolling from the bog.

**Keep out.**
- Kitchen framing: no kettle, pots or table. The vat is a chandler's.
- The witch of tales: no pointed hat, broom or cauldron over flames.
- Fog over the water; red light (the light leaking from the shack is warm yellow).
- Hesk herself, shown plainly.

**Open questions.**
- Does Hesk appear at all: never, as the zone's one named unique, or as a voice behind the door that trades in years?
  The lore leaves her fate open.
- Should the tallow-soaked shack ever burn when the Pyre-Saints reach it, or is its never burning part of its wrongness?
