# The Drowned Village

*Act I · zone `drowned_village` · outdoors · creatures of levels 6–9 · borders: the Drowned Fen, the Sunken Bog,
the Well-Shaft · in the game: the old procedural zone, to be remade by the bog's method · the bog beyond it:
`sunken-bog.md`; the well beneath it: `the-well-shaft.md`*

A village stood here, and still stands, a little under the water. Nobody now remembers its name. Long ago its
well-drawer let his iron hook down the well in the square, and it caught, and what came up was water, clear as a held
breath, and it kept coming, to the sills, the lintels and the bell. It is the Fen's water and does not wet, so the
drowning never finished: the people under it went on. Their doors stand ajar, the way you leave a door for somebody
coming home late; a shroud waits half woven on a loom; at dusk bells ring under the square, though the belfry fell long
ago. The foreman's daughter of the Widow's Stumps was kept here. Part of the god: the skin over its throat, with the
throat itself below, down the well. At the far edge the clear water goes black and becomes the Sunken Bog. It should
feel like a held breath, everything stopped in the middle of the day, sadder than frightening.

**The one image:** doors standing ajar along a drowned street under clear water, one window lit just above the water,
and Wick-Saints battering at its glass.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### The well, and what came up

*The old woman of the reed hut at the Fen's edge says she is the last who can give the village as it was. A pilgrim
who sheltered a night at her cold hearth wrote it down.*

Sit on that side, away from the bowl. I keep a cloth over it.

We had wet water then. You could drown in it, and children did, in the mill-race, and we carried them out through the
lych-gate like anyone else. Our well was in the middle of the square, forty turns of rope deep, and the bucket came up
cold and heavy and wet, and you could see your face in it the right way round.

My father drew the well. He had an iron hook for it, the long kind with a barb, and in a dry summer he would let it
down past the end of the rope and drag the bottom for whatever had fallen in: a ring, a shoe, a child's knife. Once he
brought up something he would not show us. He carried it back to the well in both hands and let it down again, and did
not speak for the rest of that day.

The spring the water rose, he let the hook down and it caught. He leaned on it, and the rope went slack, and then
tight, and then slack, as if something down there were breathing on it. He pulled. What came up out of the well was
water, only water, clear as a held breath, and it kept coming, up to the doorsteps, and the sills, and the lintels, and
the bell. My father let go of the rope where he stood. The hook is down there still. The rope still goes down into the
well, and it is still tight.

The water was dry. You know that. So the drowning never finished. The water closed over the village like a lid, and
those under it went on.

My sister and I were up on the chapel roof, and the arch bowed under us. When the water stopped rising I climbed down
onto the reeds, and she stayed. She said she could see our mother through the water, at the loom in the weaver's
house, at the shroud for the miller's mother, and she wanted to go down and hand her the thread. I could see our mother
too. She was throwing the shuttle with her left hand.

They are all still there. Go to the edge of the square and look down. The doors stand ajar, every one. The shuttle is
still warm; I put my arm down through the water once and touched it. When a pilgrim leans over to look, they all stop
what they are doing and look up, to see if it is me.

Every dusk there is a lamp in the weaver's upper window, just above the water. My mother lights it. They leave the
doors open and the lamp lit for whoever is coming home late, and I know who that is.

They tell me the belfry fell a thousand years ago. I rang it. Both of those are true, and I will not choose between
them. The bells still go at dusk, down under the square. My sister rings them.

My mother always said I parted my hair on the left. I part it on the right. Mothers misremember.

Leave the door as you found it when you go.

### The foreman's daughter

*Told on the Ridge road and in the lowlands below it. A carter's wife told it this way at the Fen's edge, and a
copyist travelling with the carts wrote it down.*

Everyone on the Ridge road knows the three stumps on the crest, and the foreman who died there with his daughters'
hands in his, one in each. Something came on his caravan in the dark. The Ridge says Husks, or else the wind, which
sounds like breathing up there and sometimes is. He did not let go of either girl, and when they were found there were
three warm stumps on the crest where they had stood, a big one and two small ones, and they are warm still.

That is how the Ridge tells it, and it will tell it no other way. Down here we tell one thing more. One of the girls let
go. Her name was Nell, and she lived.

She ran. She came down off the Ridge and kept going until she came to a village where every door stood open, the way
you leave a door for somebody who is coming home late. She was somebody coming home late. She went in.

The drowned kept her a long time, some say longer than a girl has years to give. Nobody can say what they wanted with
her. My mother thought they only wanted somebody to come home, with all their doors standing open for it, and Nell was
the first who ever did. My grandmother thought the water knows who has let go of a hand and keeps such people close;
the old woman who lives at its edge let go of her own sister's, she said, and look where she lives.

She came out in the end, and the tellings do not agree how. Some say she walked out on her own one dusk while the bells
were ringing, dry as paper, in a dress of a kind nobody had sewn in a hundred years. Others say the drowned let go of
her one morning, the way you let go of a hand. All of them agree on what she asked: that nobody be told she had let go.
So the Ridge does not tell it. There are three stumps on the crest, and the Ridge will not say which of them is empty.

Some on the Ridge road tell it the other way about: that it was the other sister who let go, and Nell who held on.
I only tell it the way it was told to me.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Drawn from:** villages drowned under reservoirs and seen again in drought (Mardale Green, Capel Celyn, Vilarinho da
  Furna, the lone bell tower of Curon in Lake Resia). A lowland village of the Hide: stone and timber houses under
  thatch, a square with a well under a winding frame, a mill-race, a chapel with a rose window and a belfry, a
  lych-gate.
- **The water:** the Fen's own, clear, dry and flat: knee to waist deep in the upper streets, which the pilgrim wades;
  two to four yards over the lower ones, roofs and all. The square stands at the edge between them.
- **The waterline is a line of time**, as in the Fen: above it, a thousand years of weather (thatch fallen in, gables
  mossed, the Fen's dry reeds in the lanes); below it, the village on the day, under a thin skin of pale silt.
- **A life stopped in the middle of the day**, under the water: the silt worn into paths where the drowned walk their
  rounds; washing hung dry on a line; a broom against a step beside its heap of sweepings; a ladder at a roof with a
  bundle of thatching reed half laid; a cradle in a doorway; the loom.
- **The drowned:** figures under the water at their work, very still. When the pilgrim stands at the square's edge the
  nearest turn their faces up: a living layer, never a danger.
- **The bog side:** the clear water darkens into the bog's black mirror, and walls and a fallen arch stand in peat and
  black water (the bog's chamber of the village's outskirts); the two waters meet without mixing.
- **The ground stays alive:** fallen thatch, broken slates, reed litter and moss in the joints of the cobbles above
  the water; under it, the silt with its paths and small things lying sharp on it.

**Landmarks.**
1. **The Sunken Square.** *Shape:* the flags all tipped inward toward the well-head in the middle (the locked craggy
   floor, tipped toward one point), the water over its lower half. *Cause:* the curb cracked outward; the winding frame
   leaning, its drum still wound with the old rope, which goes down into the well taut, worn to a thread, and holds.
   *Material:* worn grey flags; the curb grooved a finger deep by ropes. *Light:* far down the well, the flicker of
   Pyre-Saints walking in the shaft, seen only on its inner wall. *Age:* the oldest made thing in the village. The way
   into the Well-Shaft is this well, by the iron rungs in its wall.
2. **The weaver's house.** *Shape:* a house at the drowned edge, its lower floor under the water and its upper window
   just above; through the door ajar below, the loom with the half-woven shroud. *Cause:* the shuttle in the shed; a
   figure at the loom. *Material:* dark oak, pale linen, limewash stained at the waterline. *Light:* the lamp in the
   upper window, the only warm light in the village, falling down through the water onto the loom; at dusk the
   Wick-Saints gather at the glass. (The game's lantern here, once "the Rotted Loom",
   now reads the Weaver's Lamp.)
3. **The Lych-Gate.** *Shape:* a roofed gate at the Fen-side entry, two oak posts and a roof of stone slates over a
   coffin-stone. *Cause:* the stone worn into two troughs by coffins; the road beyond it going down into the water, as
   it always went down to the water the village gave its dead to. *Material:* oak gone silver, mossed slates. *Light:*
   the zone's first lantern-stone hangs under its roof.
4. **The fallen belfry** lies across a drowned lane, its bell-frame broken and empty; where the bells went is told in
   `the-well-shaft.md`.

**Light and weather.** Overcast by day, moonlit at night, as in the bog. Rain darkens the stone and thatch above the
water and drips from the gables; on the dry water it beads, as in the Fen. No fog, no red light.

**The god showing through: a little.** The rope held taut from below, the square drawn in toward the well, the drowned
going on.

**Creatures.** The game's mix: Drowned Husks 62, Wick-Saints 26, Wick-Saints of the Canopy 9, Mire Gasps 7, Weepers 7,
Mire Vein-Borers 7, Gravebloats 6, Bellwethers 4, Bloatlings 4, Pyre-Saints 3.
- **Drowned Husks:** some of the drowned who climbed out, and those who went in after them, standing at doors that are
  not theirs until three gather.
- **Mire Gasps:** the breath the drowned drew in when the water came and never let out, coming up now as veils of
  small mouths.
- **Wick-Saints** gather at the weaver's window; those **of the Canopy** keep under the eaves. **Bellwethers**, by one
  telling, are the belfry's bells, got up and walking. **Mire Vein-Borers** rise under the silt and the peat, never
  under the cobbles above the water.
- **By material:** the old thatch above the water burns readily (in rain it only smoulders); stone walls stop needles
  and Bellwethers; wading slows everyone alike.

**Sound.** The near-silence of wading; the bells at dusk, coming up out of the well; now and then a shuttle's clack
under the water; wind in empty gables.

**Keep out.** The old lore's set table; crosses or other real church images; bloated or floating dead; fog; red
light.
