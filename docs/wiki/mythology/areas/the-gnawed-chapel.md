# The Gnawed Chapel

*Act I · zone `wolf_den_chapel`, shown in the game as the Gnawed Chapel · under the ground · creatures of levels 4–8 ·
borders: the Sighing Ridge · in the game: the old procedural crypt map (104 × 104), to be remade by the bog's method ·
the Tithed's ways with the dead: `../../in-game-texts/01-the-tithed.md` · the Pale Order's knights: `../../in-game-texts/02-the-pale-order.md`*

Under the Sighing Ridge, where the wind goes out over the crest like breath, the Ridge folk cut a chapel into the soft
rock and laid their dead in its walls. The Ridge has no depth of earth for barrows, so they cut a place for each of
their dead in the stone, closed it with plaster and painted a name and a face on the seal. Every seal in the chapel is
broken now, and every one broke outward. The door is gnawed through at its foot, also from the inside, and the dead are
gone. Nobody agrees on what gnawed it.

It is one of the first places under the ground a pilgrim finds: close and grieving, off the act's dry side. The god
shows only as a hint, in the lowest room, where a ring of teeth has come up through the floor.

**The one image:** a long gallery of opened niches, the painted seals lying face down on the floor before each one, and
at the far end, at the lantern's edge, the backs of the last niches broken through into the dark.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### The names under the Ridge

*Told by a woman of the Ridge, who came down to the lowlands in her old age, to a copyist who lodged next door to her.
He read it back to her the same night, and she made him strike out one word, which was a name.*

You can't dig on the Ridge. A spade goes down a hand and strikes stone, and the wind takes whatever earth you turn. So
we never had barrows like the lowland folk. When one of ours died, we carried them down the stair into the chapel under
the crest, and the men cut a place in the wall long enough to lie in. We laid the dead one in it with their belt knotted
at the back and a line of salt along the lip, and the plasterer closed it up flush with the wall. When the plaster was
dry, the nearest kin painted the face on it, and the name under the face. My mother painted my father. She was no
painter, and he looked surprised for forty years.

We closed them up flush because of the knights. Every spring the knights of the Pale Order came down the Ridge road in
their carved plate and asked us for our bones. A bone out of its place, they said, is a wound in the world. We told them
we had no dead. A people with no dead, one of them said to my grandmother, is a people that lies. She agreed with him
and gave him salt, and he went away with his chests empty, and the chapel walls looked like walls.

Up on the crest you never say a name aloud, because the wind takes it, but the wind can't reach under the rock. So on
the fast-day we went down and sang, and after the singing the eldest of us read the names off the plaster, every one, so
that the one whose name we never say could not have them either. The lowlanders keep their names behind their teeth
and say no name over a grave, and we always thought that a poor way to love anybody.

The winter I was married, she was reading the names and stopped in the middle, and then we all heard it. Behind the
plaster, in the wall, something was scraping, slowly, in more than one place. She started reading again, louder, and the
scraping came along the wall toward her voice. We left the chapel, all of us, and nobody went down the stair again until
the thaw.

At the thaw every place in the walls was open and empty. The seals lay on the floor in pieces, painted side down. The
door had a hole gnawed through its foot that a child could crawl through. And in the lowest room, where there had never
been anything but floor, a ring of great teeth had come up through it, with ash in the middle, the kind the pilgrims
step into.

The lowlanders say our own dead did it, gnawing their way out to come home. Let them say it. I have seen those teeth.
Something came up out of the Ridge from underneath, into the backs of our walls, and took our dead down with it. It came
up where we were saying their names, and I have not said a name aloud since.

The knights came that spring as they always did. We told them we had no dead, and for once it was true.

### A knight's day-book

*A leaf from the day-book of a knight of the Pale Order, written the spring after the chapel was opened. It was copied
for a lowland scholar by a brother who had been told to copy the book and not to read it.*

The Ridge folk say again that they have no dead. This year I went down into their chapel under the crest, which I have
passed nine springs without entering, because the old woman at the stair-head would not look at me.

Its walls are cut full of places for the dead, a hundred and more, each once closed with plaster and painted with a
face. Every seal broke outward: the pieces lie on the floor before each place, painted side down, and the plaster dust
is thrown out across the flags in fans. The lips of the places are worn in small curved grooves, two by two, the width
of a bite. I set my own teeth to one of them, Warden, and I ask your pardon for it. They fit.

The door is gnawed through at the foot, from the inside. The cloths are gnawed to lace, but that I think is the moths,
which are thick in there. The pews are whole. I found no bone in the whole of it, not a finger.

In the lowest room a ring of teeth has come up through the floor, of the kind the pilgrims walk into, and three of the
places nearest it are broken through at the back as well, into the rock, going down. I have written the ring in the
book. I have not written the three.

The woman told me at last that her people said their dead's names aloud over them every fast-day, so that the Unmaking
could not have them. We do the same when we lay a bone in the wall, and give it its number besides, and the number is
what holds it. A name with no number is a door left on the latch. I think these dead heard themselves called every
fast-day for a hundred years, and went to find out who was calling.

There was nothing to carry home.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Cut into soft rock under the Ridge.** Draw it from the rock-cut chapels of sandstone country: the Hermitage at
  Warkworth in Northumberland, the rock houses of Kinver Edge, and the cave churches of Cappadocia.
- **The rock:** a soft, tawny-to-rust sandstone (chapter 2), cross-bedded, every arris rounded, honeycombed at the wall
  bases where the damp rises, with pick marks in diagonal rows on every cut face.
- **True scale:** a stair of some thirty steps, each worn to a dish; the nave about 14 × 7 yards under a vault 5 yards
  up; the galleries of the dead 2.5 yards wide and 3 high, their niches in rows of four from floor to vault, each 2.2
  yards long.
- **The rooms by cause:** the stair; the nave; the galleries, the newest nearest the nave and the oldest farthest in;
  the plasterer's room; the lowest room, with the ring.
- **The floor is never bare:** seal plaster in shards and dust fanned outward from every niche; grit blown down the
  stair in tongues, with dry grass and heather twigs drifted at its foot; candle stubs, wax runs and dried flower stems;
  salt crusted along the niche lips; knotted belts where the dead lay; gnawed cloth; moth scales like grey dust. No bone
  anywhere.

**Landmarks.**
- **The galleries of the dead** (the zone's signature):
  - *Shape:* straight galleries lined from floor to vault with opened niches; from the game's camera, rows of black
    mouths, each with a pale fan of plaster on the floor before it.
  - *Story by cause:* every seal broke outward and lies before its niche; the lips are worn by paired grooves in arcs,
    the width of a bite; the oldest faces have faded to ghosts and the newest are bright; at the far end the backs of
    three niches are holed through into round bores going down.
  - *Material:* lime plaster over sandstone; faces in red ochre, soot-black and chalk; the bite-grooves paler than the
    plaster round them; the bores lined with something dark and glossy.
  - *Light:* none of its own. The lantern rakes along the rows, each niche lip shadowing its mouth.
  - *Age:* a lifetime since they opened, with dust over the shards and moths in the niches.
- **The door:** split timber strapped with iron, its foot gnawed through from inside into a ragged arch. The grey
  daylight down the stair behind it is the only daylight in the place, so from inside the door stands in silhouette, and
  the Ridge's wind moans through the hole.
- **The nave and the hymn-board:** the pews whole and polished by use, facing a plain altar cut from the living rock;
  everything else gnawed (the altar cloth to lace, the hangings, the covers of the song-books). The hymn-board still
  shows the numbers of the last songs sung, and they match no song in any book found there. A vent cut up through the
  Ridge drops a thin grey beam onto the altar, its source out of sight; rain comes down it too, and has worn a dark
  streak and a small hollow in the altar where it drips.
- **The ring in the lowest room** (the zone's waystone): eight fang-stones come up through the floor, the flags round
  them heaved, split and tilted outward (the locked craggy floor), ash in the pit, a dark stain seeping from each
  plinth. The stone round it is warm.

**Light and weather.** The lantern is the key light; the game's three lanterns stand where the Ridge folk kept lamps.
Grey daylight comes only down the stair and the vent, and the Ridge's wind comes with it, stirring the gnawed lace, the
plaster dust and the flames. Rain runs down the stair in a thin sheet and pools level at its foot, and the wet sandstone
darkens. No fog, no red light.

**The god showing through: a hint.** The ring of teeth, the warm stone round it and the glossy bores going down.
Elsewhere, only the wrongness of the seals.

**Creatures** (the game's mix):
- **Husks (31):** the Ridge's own dead come home, the lowlanders say, sitting in their families' pews; three together
  stand up at once. Take them one at a time in the door, the gallery mouths and the gaps between the pews.
- **Tithe-Hands (16):** they climb the niche rows and drop down behind. Keep a gallery wall at your back.
- **Weepers (13):** in the back pews and behind the altar. The rock piers stop their bone needles, and the pews are too
  low to.
- **Wick-Saints (7) and Wick-Saints of the Canopy (7):** drawn to the lamps; under the low vaults their swoops are short
  and they cannot climb away.
- **Stalker Crones (7):** the long straight galleries are theirs, seen at the edge of the eye down a row of niches.
- **Vein-Borers (4):** only where grit lies deep, at the stair foot and round the ring; never under cut stone.
- **Bloatlings (3) and Pyre-Saints (2):** strays from the Heath side. The gnawed cloth and the dry pews burn fast; the
  puddle at the stair foot puts a Pyre-Saint out.
- **Objects:** one waystone (the ring), three lanterns, four chests. Sandstone and plaster stop shots and break them;
  the stair is one long doorway.

**Sound.** The Ridge's wind down the stair, rising and falling like breath; plaster ticking underfoot; moths at the
lamps; the vent's drip; and a slow scraping somewhere behind the walls, never placed, which stops when the pilgrim
stops. No singing, where once there was singing every week.

**Keep out.**
- Any sign of animals: no claw marks, dens, fur, howling or animal bones. The marks are a person's bite.
- The game's old lines about "the hungry", a "kill-floor" and "meals": no food framing.
- Fog; red light.
