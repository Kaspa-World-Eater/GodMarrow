# The Ash Shore

*Act I · zone `ash_shore` · outdoors, 152 × 152 · creatures of levels 3–5 · borders: the Burnt Heath · in the game: the
old procedural map (`ash_shore_s1001` to `s1020`), to be remade by the bog's method · the Red Water, which this sea may
be: `the-red-shore.md`, `../../in-game-texts/08-the-shore-folk.md`*

Below the Heath the land goes down in grey dunes and ends at a grey sea that runs to the edge of sight and does not move
like water. No wave breaks on it; it heaves in long, low swells like a sleeper's back under a blanket. Everything the
god's last breath has carried off the Moor and the Heath comes down here at last and settles on it, so the sea lies
under a skin of ash.

**By our proposal, what lies under the skin is blood: the same blood as the Red Water** far along the coast, gone grey
under ash the way coals go grey when a fire is banked. Nobody on the shore knows it, and only a few believe it. Where
the grey wets the warm ash of the strand, the ash hardens into black glass, as it does on the Moor where a Husk bleeds.

The shore is the end of a pilgrim road. Its own Broken Kneeler, a halved prayer-bench, stands by the water, and the
Kneelers who came the last league on their knees face the sea along the strand. Salt-boilers work above the tide. It is
the Hide's edge, where the skin stops, and it should feel like the end of the world gone quiet: flat, pale, listening.

**The one image:** a flat grey sea heaving without breaking, a strand of black glass where it meets the ash, a row of
kneeling figures facing it, and the salt-boilers' smoke laid flat over the water by the wind.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### The grey

*Told by a salt-boiler of the Ash Shore to a carter who had come down for salt and asked why nobody ever put a boat on
the water. The carter's daughter, who was with him, wrote it down on the walk home.*

Nobody puts a boat on the grey because nobody knows where a boat would go. My grandfather said that as if it were a
joke, and nobody laughed.

Look at it. It doesn't break and it doesn't splash. Throw a stone on it and the stone sits on top for a breath, then
goes down slowly, the way a foot goes into a bog, and the grey closes over the hole without a ring. Twice a day it comes
up the strand and twice a day it goes back, and the tide brings in nothing. Not a stick, not a body. Nothing
washes up here but the grey's own skin, in plates, and those we turn face down and leave.

The wind always blows out over it, never in. Every bit of ash that ever lifted off the Moor or the Heath comes down here
in the end, and the wind lets it fall on the water, and the water takes it, and has been taking it longer than anyone
has counted. That's the grey: ash on top. What's under the ash, nobody here can say. The pilgrims who come down for
the Kneeler call it the god's eye, filmed over and still looking up, and they won't look at it for long. They don't
have to work beside it.

We dig the ash at the tide line where the grey has soaked it, wash it in the pits, and boil the wash in the pans, and
the pans give salt. Good salt; the Moor puts it on its doorsteps and its sills. My father said that settles it. The god
never had any salt of its own, everybody knows that, so whatever is under the ash is water, whatever the old women say.

My aunt said otherwise. A trader came along the coast once, in her girlhood, from far along, where he said the sea is
red. The people there live up in the trees, he said, and burn their dead, and give the water nothing, because whatever
goes into it comes back. My aunt said ours is the same sea. She said grey is the colour red goes under ash, the way
coals go grey when you bank a fire. She said the glass along the strand was the proof, because ash goes to glass only
where blood runs into it, and look at the strand. My father said the trader was a liar and the glass is just the shore.

My uncle, when he was a boy, threw a big stone off the end of the glass as far as he could, and it tore the skin. He
said that for the space of a breath, before the grey closed over, he saw what was under it, and it was dark red, the
colour of the inside of a cheek. My father said his brother saw the sky in it. There is no red sky here, and there never
was.

We used to give our dead to the grey, laid on a board at low water, and it took them down slowly, the way it takes a
stone. The old women stopped that in the year the grey came up the strand higher than anyone had ever seen, and stood
there a day and a night before it went back. Since then we carry our dead up to the Heath fire, and the keeper lays them
by the saint.

I boil salt. I don't throw stones.

### The last league

*Painted on a board at the head of the last league of the shore road, where pilgrims who mean to go the rest of the way
on their knees take off their boots. The board has been painted over many times, and the old letters show through the
new.*

Pilgrim, this is the last league. From here the road runs down through the dunes to the water, and at the water is the
Broken Kneeler, the true one, whatever you were told on the Moor.

It is a bench of grey stone, long enough for nine to kneel at it side by side, broken in two in the middle. On the night
the god died something came down out of the dark over the grey, struck the bench in two, and went on into the water,
and the water closed over it. That is why the halves lean apart toward the sea, and why the strand between them is
glass. The people of the Moor have a stone man up on their rise who kneels with his head off, and they call him the
Kneeler, and they are welcome to him. A kneeler is a thing you kneel at. Kneel at ours.

Go the last league on your knees if you have come to do that. Many do. Leave your boots here. They will be here when you
come back, and if you do not come back, someone will have the use of them. Whatever you have come to ask, ask it at the
bench. Do not ask it of the water.

Along the strand, facing the grey, you will pass the ones who came the last league on their knees and did not get up
again. Some say they are pilgrims who prayed too long. The old women of the shore say they are some of those who never
came out of the god at all, still on their knees where they knelt, and that the sea only drew them nearer. Do not kneel
near them, and pray where they cannot hear you, because they are listening for how. If one of them is nearer the bench
in the morning than it was at dusk, leave the shore that day.

At dusk the Husks come down out of the dunes to the water's edge and kneel in a row, and put their faces down to the
grey. Do not go near them while they do it. In the morning they are gone, and there is a new line of glass along the
strand where they knelt.

## For building it

**The land, from the Heath down.** A real coast after an ashfall, and a sea under a raft of pumice: Iceland's
grey-black south-coast sands, and the pumice raft of 2021 from Fukutoku-Okanoba, which choked Okinawa's harbours until
the sea looked like land. The salt-works follow the medieval salt-makers of the Frisian coast, who burned sea-soaked
peat and washed its ash into brine.
1. **The heath's edge** thins into dunes of drifted ash held by lyme grass and sea holly, every tuft trailing a tail of
   ash toward the sea.
2. **The salt-works:** boiling-houses of stone and turf with wide iron pans, clay-lined brine pits, raked ash beds above
   the tide, and mounds of spent ash taller than a house. One or two are still worked.
3. **The glass strand** at the tide line, and **the grey sea.**

**The floor stays alive:** ash in a dozen greys, rippled across the wind; glass chips, black and brown-green at their
thin edges; rills where rain runs to the sea; dune grass; dried plates of the skin cracked into polygons; white salt
crust at the pits; pilgrims' dropped things.

**The grey sea** (built as the Red Water is, `the-red-shore.md`):
- **Colour:** ash grey, matte, faintly warm, darker and browner in the troughs where the skin stretches thinnest. Never
  red at the surface, and nothing glows.
- **Movement:** long, low swells passing under the skin as a slow heave, never breaking; at the edge the skin
  wrinkles as it climbs the glass and smooths as it draws back.
- **The skin:** ash fine as flour, cracked in great plates like old paint. Where anything breaks it (a stone, a
  creature, a pilgrim who falls) the dark beneath shows for a breath, deep red-brown and glossy, and the grey closes
  over without a ring.
- **Stranded plates** at the tide lines, grey on top, dark red-brown and tacky beneath, which the salt-boilers turn
  face down: the only place what lies under the grey can be seen. Keep them small.
- **Never walkable:** the zone's edge.

**Landmarks, by the pit method.**
- **The Shore's Broken Kneeler** (the altar). A kneeling-bench of grey stone about seven yards long, broken in the
  middle, its halves leaning apart toward the water like a door opened onto the sea; the strand between them glassed in
  a fan; knee-hollows worn into each step; salt, cloth strips and glass heaped on the landward ends. Its hidden light: a
  lantern behind the landward half, out of sight, so its light reaches the step first. Older than the Tithed.
- **The glass strand:** black glass 3 to 10 yards wide, in low ridges, one for each high tide, like the rings of a
  felled tree; and rows of glass running straight down to the water, a stride apart, where the Husks knelt.
- **The boiling-houses:** pans scaled grey-white, one with a dark ring in its bottom that no scraping takes out; the
  worked house's hearth seen first as warm light under its own smoke.
- **The three statues:** wayside figures along the last league, each turned to face the water.

**Light and weather.** Overcast; the shore pale, the brightest values the glass and the salt crust. The wind always
blows out to sea: smoke lies flat over the water, and ash lifts off the dunes in low skeins and settles on the grey.
Rain darkens the ash and beads on the glass; the sea's skin takes it without a mark. No fog, and no red light: what
shows when the skin breaks is a colour, never a glow. The lantern is the key light.

**The god showing through: more than on the Heath,** and still only shown: the sea itself, kept grey; the plates'
undersides; the glass; the warm ash.

**Creatures.** Husk 36, Tithe-Hand 21, Wick-Saint 12, Kneeler 12, Vein-Borer 11, Weeper 6.
- **Husks** come down to the water at dusk and kneel in rows, faces to the grey; disturbed, they rise in threes.
- **Tithe-Hands** come up out of the grey at low water on their fingers, shedding ash, as they come up out of the surf
  at the Red Shore.
- **Kneelers,** the most in the act, face the sea along the strand, a little nearer the bench each morning.
- **Vein-Borers** come up through soft ash, never through the glass strand, which is the safe road.
- **Wick-Saints** gather at dusk over the boiling-house fires; **Weepers** keep behind the mounds and walls.
- **In a fight:** mounds and walls are cover; the glass strand is safe from Borers but bright, so Weepers find you on
  it; brine pits douse fire; dune grass burns, ash does not; the pilgrim cannot go into the sea.

**Sound.** No surf: a long soft hiss as the skin slides up over the glass and drags back; glass ticking underfoot; the
wind going out over the water; a pan seething; the Kneelers' scourges. No shore life at all.

**Keep out.** Breaking waves, foam, splashes and rings; sea life of any kind; a red surface; fog; boats; crows; the old
generator's shared landmarks.

**Open questions for Derek.**
- **The proposal itself.** If the grey is the Red Water's blood under ash, the shore's salt comes out of the god's
  blood, against the Tithed belief that the god never had any salt of its own. The salt-boilers argue it both ways,
  unreconciled. Does that contradiction stand?
- **The dead.** The salt-boilers once gave their dead to the grey, and at the Red Shore the dead of "somewhere far along
  the water" came up in pilgrims' cloaks. They may be the same dead, if the coasts are to be tied that far.
- **The two Broken Kneelers** (the Moor's a kneeling figure, the Shore's a halved bench): we keep both, each claiming
  to be the true one.
