# The Old Barrow

*Act I · zone `barrow`, the Old Barrow · under the ground · creatures of levels 7–10 · borders: the Ashen Moor, the
Fallen Watchtower · in the game: the old procedural barrow map (87 × 87), to be remade by the bog's method · the
diggers' own rules: the Codex's "The Digger's Tally, Widow's Lane" · the Tithed's ways with the dead:
`../../in-game-texts/01-the-tithed.md`*

The Barrows are where the Tithed bury their own: nine barrows to a ring, and nine of a family to each barrow. The Old
Barrow is their oldest end, at the foot of Widow's Lane, where the first ring was raised round a standing stone that was
there before it. Every lid in the Barrows sinks from the middle, deeper at dawn than at dusk, and every hollow leans the
same way: east, toward the Barrow ring, a ring of tooth-stones beyond the Lane. The old rings have sunk so long that
their chambers have run together under the turf, and the zone is the inside of them: passages and chambers of grey slab
and chalk under roofs that sag into the room like a cheek sucked hollow.

It is the Tithed's own place of the dead, kept by frightened rules: salt along every shelf, no name said over a grave,
the risen put back face down with a flat stone on the neck. It should feel like grief held very still. The god is in the
ground's breath, which went out once, the diggers say, and never came back in.

**The one image:** a chamber's roof sagging down to a ragged hole of grey sky, rain falling through it in a thread, and
the dead on their shelves under lines of salt.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### Low Day

*Written by a pilgrim who wintered with the families who bury at the Barrows and kept Low Day with them. The leaf was
found folded into the back of his road-book when the book was sold.*

Low Day falls in the dark of the year, and nobody in the barrow families works on it. At dusk every household comes out
with a lantern, the old ones carried and the children holding the belt of whoever is in front, and they all walk
down to the Barrows without a word. They set a lantern at the foot of every barrow, nine to a ring, ring after ring,
until the whole field is pricked out with lights like a town seen from a hill.

When the last lantern is down, they stand among the rings and say one line together, low, nine times over: *lie down,
lie still, lie low, lie long, till the Kneeler finishes.* Afterward I asked who the Kneeler was. The woman whose house I
lodged in said it was the one who is still praying. When I asked who that was, she said it was the one in the song, and
went inside.

Nobody says a name. That is the hardest rule of the Barrows. The diggers say the dead follow their names, so no name may
be said over a grave, not even on Low Day, not even to the one you put there yourself. You stand at the foot of your own
family's barrow with your mouth shut and your lantern burning, and that is all you are allowed.

So when the line has been said, the widows go to the Grieving Stone. It stands in the middle of the first ring, and the
families say it was standing there before the first barrow was raised, and that their people built the ring round it
because it was already the place where people came to grieve. There is a hollow worn in its face at the height of a
woman's forehead. The widows go to it one at a time, put their foreheads into the hollow and say the names into the
stone, quietly, all the names they have not said all year. Then they go home without looking back. The stone keeps the
names, they say, so that the dead do not hear them.

I went to it last, when they had all gone. The hollow was warm from their foreheads. I had nobody of my own to name
there, and I stood with my head against it anyway. After a while I heard, from inside the stone or from under it,
something saying names back, too low to make out. I told the woman of my house in the morning. She said that was the
stone keeping them, and that I should not have stayed.

### The Turned Mound

*Told by a woman of the barrow families to a pilgrim at her door, and written down by him there. She would not give her
mother's name, or her own.*

Every lid in the Barrows points east, to the ring of teeth. Stand at the foot of the Lane at dawn and look along them,
and all the hollows lean the one way, like grass in a wind. All but my mother's.

She should have gone inside, under the Moor, with the mothers before her, but that was shut long before her time, so she
went into our family's barrow at the foot of the Lane. We laid her face up, with the salt along her and her belt knotted
at the back. Nine days after, my brother went out at first grey and found her standing at the foot of the Lane, facing
up it. She was clean, cleaner than when we laid her, cleaner than she had been in her life.

The digger came with his spade and the flat stone, and he and my brother led her back and laid her face down, and put
the stone on her neck, and said no name. That is how it is done. She came up again at midsummer and got as far as the
well. The third time she got to the corner, where you can see our roof, and she was standing there looking at it when
the digger found her.

After the third time she stayed down. But in the year after, her lid turned. It sank from the middle like all the
others, and its hollow leans west, up the Lane, toward where we live. It is the only one in the Barrows that points
home. The diggers call it the Turned Mound. They have laid it flat again more than once, and inside a year it goes back
the same way.

The knights came that spring and asked for her, as they ask for all of them, and my brother let them have her, because
he could not sleep. The knight lifted her out in his two hands as if she were asleep and carried her up the Lane. She
went with him quietly. He said she was the easiest he had lifted in the Barrows.

Her lid still points home, and there is nothing under it now. I go and stand in the hollow sometimes, because it is the
only place in the Barrows that faces the right way.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Under the turf of a chalk down.** Draw it from the barrow cemeteries of Wessex (Normanton Down, Winterbourne Stoke)
  and the tombs inside such mounds: West Kennet Long Barrow's passage of upright sarsens with side chambers, Newgrange's
  corbelled chamber with stone basins in its recesses. Real barrows often have a sunken centre where the chamber fell
  in; here every one has, and they all lean one way.
- **What was made:** passages walled with upright slabs of grey sarsen-like stone, chalk rubble between, roofed with
  capstones; round chambers roofed by corbelling, each course of slabs stepping in until one stone closes the top;
  shelves of slab round each chamber, three high, three dead to a shelf; a stone basin in each side chamber for salt.
- **True scale:** passages 1.2 to 1.6 yards wide and 2 high, so the pilgrim stoops under some capstones; chambers 5 to 7
  yards across, their roofs 4 to 5 yards up before they sagged.
- **The floor is never bare:** chalk rubble and grave-earth in many tones, white through grey to brown; turf clods
  fallen through the lids; roots; salt crusted along every shelf lip; Low Day's candle stubs and soot at every chamber's
  foot; the diggers' sacks of leftover grave-earth, which stays warm, stacked in the passages; knotted belts, a child's
  shoe, small carved things laid with the dead.

**Landmarks.**
- **The broken dishes** (the zone's signature):
  - *Shape:* a chamber's corbelled roof sagging down in a funnel and, in the oldest, a ragged hole at its lowest point
    through to the sky, turf and roots hanging from the lip.
  - *Story by cause:* the roof slabs have slipped inward and down, each course tilted toward the centre; every funnel's
    low point is pulled east, and a line drawn from one to the next runs to the ring of teeth (the ground log's one true
    detail); the dead under each hole lie wet and dark with the drip, and those in the dry stay pale.
  - *Material:* chalk going grey and green where the rain comes in; sarsen; turf.
  - *Light:* the overcast sky through the hole, a hard-edged pool on the floor, the hole itself out of frame above.
  - *Age:* the oldest rings on the act's near side; moss and fern grow only in the pools of light.
- **The Grieving Stone:** a lone standing stone twice a man's height, rough and lichened, in the sunken open middle of
  the first ring. A hollow is worn in its face at forehead height, polished by foreheads, and the ground round it is
  trodden bare. This court is the brightest place in the zone, so the stone stands dark against its own daylight. No
  tool has touched it.
- **The Turned Mound:** the one chamber whose roof sags west, toward the Lane. Its shelf is bare but for the flat stone
  lying where the neck was, and its hole lets the grey light fall on the west wall.
- **The Empty Ninth:** the ninth barrow of the digger's ring, its corbelled roof whole and level, the only one with no
  sag and no hole. Its shelves are swept, and longer than any body; in its floor is the place the digger cut for
  himself, his blanket and spade beside it. It is the darkest chamber in the zone, and the only one where the lantern
  flame leans out toward the door, the way breath should go.
- **The ring of teeth** (the Barrow ring, one of the zone's two waystones): eight fang-stones in a second sunken court
  east of the first ring, a dark stain seeping from each plinth. Its crust lifts once in thirty-one heartbeats, a
  measurer counted.

**Light and weather.** Overcast daylight comes only through the broken dishes and into the two courts; elsewhere the
lantern is the key light. The game's one lantern stands where the Lane comes down into the first ring. Rain comes
through the holes in threads, darkens the chalk to a wet grey, runs down the sagging slabs, drips from their lowest
edges and pools level beneath. No fog, no red light.

**The god showing through: more than on the Ridge, less than in the crypts.** The diggers say the ground breathed out
once and never in, and the sags deepen at dawn; yet near the mounds breath goes the wrong way, in, and every lantern
flame leans east, except in the Empty Ninth. Where a chamber's chalk floor has slumped away, a hand's breadth of warm
grey hide shows.

**Creatures** (the game's mix):
- **Husks (34):** the Barrows' dead come up clean and stand facing home, so set them in chamber mouths facing the Lane.
  Take them in the passages.
- **Tithe-Hands (19):** the slab walls give a wall for your back.
- **Wick-Saints (18) and Wick-Saints of the Canopy (2):** they come down through the broken dishes at dusk and swoop in
  the high chambers; in the low passages they cannot climb away.
- **Vein-Borers (14):** under the soft grave-earth and fallen turf; never under slab floors or the diggers' boards.
- **Weepers (5):** behind the Grieving Stone; go round it and close.
- **Bloatlings (5), the Stalker Crone (2), Pyre-Saints (2):** dry turf and grave-goods burn; the rain pools put a
  Pyre-Saint out.
- **Objects:** two waystones, one lantern, four chests. Slab and chalk stop shots and break them.

**Sound.** The ground drawing in; chalk grit trickling; the rain's threads and drips; and far off, from the watchtower's
side, a single muffled stroke under the turf when the wind is right. No voices at all.

**Keep out.**
- Any voice here saying a dead person's name.
- The old lines' "Ossuary Lords" (now the Pale Order's knights) and "pauldrons on backwards" (the dead come up clean).
- Heaped bones: the dead lie in order on their shelves.
- Fog; red light.
