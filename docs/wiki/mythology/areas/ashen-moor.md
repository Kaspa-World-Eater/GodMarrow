# The Ashen Moor

*Act I · zone `moor` · outdoors · creatures of levels 2–6 (the old moor's; the camp has none) · borders: the Hollow
Crypt, the Drowned Fen, the Old Barrow, the Sighing Ridge, the Hollow Wood · in the game: **a bare camp**
(`moor_s9001`, 48 × 48, `tools/worldgen/camp_export.py`), the stand-in start since 2026-10-08 · **its future is open** ·
the Tithed's own telling of how they came out onto the Cheek: `../../in-game-texts/01-the-tithed.md`*

**Wiped, and waiting.** On 2026-10-08 Derek had the Moor cleared ("we can nuke the entire first area anyways, its
fucking trash"; "wipe it, keep a bare camp ... make it very basic so we can finish the bog"). What stands in the game
is a small clearing: the camp's people round the camp lantern, the stash, the waystone and the five roads out. The new
start is the Red Shore (`the-red-shore.md`). Whether the Moor comes back as a zone, gives its pieces to its neighbours,
or stays a camp is Derek's to decide, and **nothing on this page is a plan to build it.** The page keeps its lore, as
lore and as inspiration.

**What it was.** The god's cheek, where the face struck first when the god came down. The ash lies to the ankle and is
warm in every season, because the flesh under it is still cooling, and black glass forms in it wherever a Husk has
bled. The Tithed came out of the god's mouth and fell here. The god's last breath goes out across it as a steady wind
at the back, over the Sighing Ridge and away. Its landmarks are Maren's camp, whose lantern was the first lit after the
fall; the Broken Kneeler on the long rise called the Jaw, with the ring of teeth below it; the milestones of the old
road; the Sighing Lantern, which three tellers put in three places; and, where the Moor lifts into the Ridge,
the Widow's Stumps (`sighing-ridge.md`). Its crows, which Derek allowed here and nowhere else, never leave it.

**The one image:** ash to the ankle, grey and steaming faintly where the rain lands on it, one lantern in the middle of
it that has never been put out, and crows stepping across ground too warm to stand still on.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### What the ash-pickers know

*Told by an ash-picker to a copyist who wanted to know where the black edge on his knife had come from. He wrote it on
the back of a bill for salt, and kept the bill.*

You want to know about the glass. Everyone who buys a knife wants to know about the glass, and nobody who picks it
wants to say, so I'll tell you once and you can stop asking the others.

The ash on the Moor is warm. It is warm at noon and warmer at night, and in a hard winter it is the only warm thing
there is, which is why the camps sleep on it with their hands flat. My mother said the god's cheek is under it, still
cooling, and that it will go on cooling long after there is anyone left to feel it.

Where a Husk bleeds into the ash, the ash drinks the blood and goes hard, and what it goes hard into is the glass. It is
black, smooth on the face that was up and rippled on the face that was down, and it stays warm in the hand for a day
after you lift it. A Husk that falls quickly leaves a splash, and a splash is chips. A Husk that stands a long while
bleeding, the way they do, leaves a run as long as a man, and a run is knife-edges for a whole camp. We go out in the
morning after a bad night and walk the ash in a line, and you can tell from the camp where the glass is, because that
is where the crows are standing.

The crows were here before us. My grandmother said that when the people first came out, the crows were already
standing on the ash in rows, as if they had come to watch. Nobody has ever seen one off the Moor. They walk behind the
pickers, lifting one foot and then the other because the ash is warm, and they stand round a run of glass and look at
it, and they never set a foot on the glass itself. When every crow on the Moor gets up at once, the old women stop
talking and look round at the children, because it means somebody has said a name out loud that should have been kept
behind the teeth.

The glass lies thickest in the hollow past the Broken Kneeler, where a ring of stones like teeth stands round a pit of
ash that lifts and settles as if something under it is breathing. No picker crosses that hollow after dark. My husband
did, once, for a run of glass he had seen from the rise. He came back with it after dark and would not sell it, though
it was the longest run anyone at our fire had ever seen. It had set in the ash in the shape of somebody kneeling. It
hangs over our door now. In winter it is the warmest thing in the house, and nobody sits under it.

### The Kneeler that kneels

*Said by an old woman of the Moor camps to a copyist at the ninth milestone of the old road, as her answer to a
salt-boiler who had told her that the true Broken Kneeler stood on his shore. The copyist kept his copy; whether the
answer ever went down to the shore, he does not say.*

Write this down, and give it to the next carter going down to the grey water, and let him read it to that salt-boiler
with the whole strand listening. The Broken Kneeler is ours. It kneels on the rise above the hollow, a robed figure of
stone that is taller kneeling than two men standing, with its hands held to its breast, facing down into the hollow
toward the ring of teeth. Its knees have sunk into the ash to the shin. One arm is gone at the elbow. Its head is off at
the neck and lies in the ash before it, face up, worn so smooth that nobody living can say whose face it was.

It was there when the people came out. Nobody remembers carving it, and nobody knows whom it was carved to be. It was
already kneeling, and already broken, because something fell on it the night the god died and struck its head off. The
grandmothers say what fell was the god's last tear. The knights who come down off the white mountains for bone say it
was a piece of the sky, and the year I was married two of them dug a pit behind it looking for the piece, and found only
more ash, warmer the deeper they went, and filled the pit in again quickly. The children say nothing fell at all: the
stone man was praying so hard that his head came off, and that is why you must never pray where a Kneeler can hear you.

Now the salt-boiler says the true one is down on his shore, and that ours is a statue someone carved and someone else
broke. I have never been to his shore, but I know what his Kneeler is from those who have. It is a bench, a long stone
bench of the kind you kneel on in a chapel, broken in two in the middle. Tell him this from me. His is a thing you kneel
on. Ours is a thing that kneels. Let him explain to his pilgrims which of the two is the Kneeler.

And tell him one thing more. Put your hand under the ash at the foot of ours, where its knees go in, and the ash is
warmer there than anywhere on the Moor, warm the way a lap is warm when somebody has only just stood up. I have done it
every year of my life. I would like to know what he finds under his.

### The chalk on the milestones

*The last leaves of a day-book kept by a copyist in a stone room built against the ninth milestone of the old road.
It came back to the copyists with his other papers when he died.*

The milestones on this road were set in the two hundred and sixth year after the god died, by people who found the road
already lying here and wanted it counted. The figures go down the farther you walk, and nobody can say what
they are counting down to. I have sat beside the ninth for thirty-one years, and I can tell you only that it has grown.
It came to my hip when I first leaned on it. It is at my shoulder now.

Every spring, for as long as there have been copyists on this road, the hooded penitents came up two by two from their
chapter at the great wound and chalked the figures fresh on every stone. They said nothing to us and took nothing, and
went home the way they came. The chalk lasted eleven months, never twelve, and we used to say the year had not turned
until the hoods had been.

They have not come for three springs. The chalk is gone from every stone; the figures are still cut, and anyone who
stoops can read them, but nobody stoops. The four of us on this road argued all one winter over whether to chalk the
stones ourselves. The eldest would not have it. She said a figure chalked by the wrong hand counts wrong, and since we
did not know what the stones were counting, we could not know what a wrong count would do. We left them bare.

This morning there was chalk on the ninth again. None of us put it there. The figure is right. The chalk is the colour
of old brown ink.

## For building it

**Nothing is to be built until Derek decides the Moor's future.** These notes keep what was learned, for whichever way
he chooses.

**The land, if it returns.** A real upland moor under a fall of ash: the heather and moor grass of Dartmoor and the
North York Moors, buried and growing grey up through it, as the farms under Eyjafjallajökull were in 2010
(`tools/art_study/library/environments/ash-moor.md`, `ecosystems/moor_and_heath.md`, chapter 2).
- **Ash** to the ankle, rippled across the one wind, with a windward pile and a downwind tail behind every object.
- **Plants and stone:** heather cushions on the drier rises, moor grass and peat pools in the hollows, bracken on the
  slopes, lone trees only in shelter and all leaning one way, outcrops and cairns.
- **The floor stays alive:** ash in many greys, cinders, black glass chips, heather stems, moss in the wet, bone grit,
  and crow tracks, the Moor's alone.

**Landmarks, by the pit method.**
- **The Broken Kneeler and the ring of teeth below it,** already studied in the Jaw (`tools/art_study/moor_scene.py`,
  `tools/landkit/kneeler.py`; ten passes, no grade from Derek yet). A robed stone figure five yards high as it kneels,
  facing the pit, its head fallen face up before it; knees sunk to the shin, ash banked on every ledge, the knights'
  filled pit a softer patch behind it; worn grey stone with paler, rougher breaks. Its hidden source is heat: the ash
  at its knees is the warmest on the Moor and steams in rain. Older than the Tithed.
- **The black glass runs,** each in the shape of what bled: a smooth face up, conchoidal ripples beneath, glints held
  back as on the pit's obsidian.
- **The milestones of the old road:** the ninth grown to a man's shoulder, bare of chalk for three years, one figure
  chalked again in brown.

**What travels if the Moor does not come back:** the Sighing Lantern and the Widow's Stumps to the Sighing Ridge, whose
folk claim both; the Broken Kneeler's rival stays on the Ash Shore (`ash-shore.md`); the camp's people go where the Red
Shore's open question sends them.

**Light, weather and the god.** Overcast. Rain darkens the ash by a third, crusts it and cuts branching rills, and
steams in threads where it lands on the warmest ash, never in a layer. The lantern is the key light. The god shows only
as a hint: the warmth, the glass, the ground drawing in with the cold and out with the warm, hide where the ash blows
thin.

**Creatures (the old mix).** Husk 178, Tithe-Hand 59, Wick-Saint 45, Weeper 42, Gravebloat 35, Gasp 34, Wick-Saint of
the Canopy 31, Elder Vein-Borer 17, Pyre-Saint 17, Bloatling 16, Vein-Borer 16, the Trunk-Thing 5, Kneeler 3,
Bellwether 2. The Husks were the Moor's own ("the pilgrims fell mid-hymn ... some are standing up"), and the Gasps
belonged to its night wind. If the zone returns, the mix is rethought with its land.

**Sound.** The wind at the back, never turning; crows; the Sighing Lantern breathing far off; the ash ticking as it
cools at dusk; the ring below the Kneeler.

**Keep out.** Crows anywhere but here; fog; red light; the old generator's landmarks that turn up in every zone (the
Sword None Drew, She of the Six Gifts, the Unshod Waymark).

**Open questions for Derek.**
- The Moor's future: a zone again, its pieces given away, or a camp.
- Where the Sighing Lantern stands in the game: the tellers say the Moor's crossroad, the end of the round, or the
  Ridge. `sighing-ridge.md` suggests the Ridge.
- Whether the crows are ever explained, or only watched.
