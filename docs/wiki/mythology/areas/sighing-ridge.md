# The Sighing Ridge

*Act I · zone `sighing_ridge` · outdoors, 172 × 124 · creatures of levels 2–4 · borders: the Ashen Moor, the Burnt
Heath, the Gnawed Chapel · in the game: the old procedural map (`sighing_ridge_s1001` to `s1020`), to be remade by the
bog's method · the breath the wind is said to be: `../../in-game-texts/06-the-myriad.md`*

Where the Moor lifts into a long bare ridge, the god's last breath goes over it. Every lowlander feels that breath as a
steady wind at the back, always pulling toward the same quarter; on the Ridge it is strong enough to hear. It comes in
long, slow surges like breath going out, stops, and comes again, and it has never once blown the other way. In the
stillness between surges, the people of the Ridge stand where they are and say nothing.

A broken colonnade runs along the crest, its columns worn down on the side the wind comes from. The ash the wind lifts
off the Moor streams from the lee of every column in long grey scarves, down onto the Burnt Heath and at last to the Ash
Shore. A line of lanterns along the crest is the far end of the lantern round, and at its end stand the Widow's Stumps
and, the Ridge says, the Sighing Lantern. The Ridge is the first bone a pilgrim walks on (the ridges of the Hide are the
god's bones): by the copyists' reading, the cheekbone, rising out of the Moor's cheek. It should feel high, bare and
exposed, a place where the wind is the main thing in the world and its silences are what frighten.

**The one image:** a line of broken columns on a bare crest under a grey sky, ash streaming off their lee sides like
smoke off snuffed candles, and at the end of the line one small lantern breathing in and out.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### When the wind stops

*Told to pilgrims by the woman who wiped the lanterns at the Ridge end of the lantern round, and written down by one of
them at the next fire. She would not give her name; she said that on the Ridge a name said aloud is a name the wind
takes.*

You've felt it at your back since the Moor. Up here you'll hear it too. That wind is the god's last breath, still going
out of it, and it has been going out longer than anyone has counted. In my mother's life and her mother's it never once
blew the other way. Everything light enough goes with it: ash, smoke, a hat, a name said too loud. Nothing comes back
up the Ridge on it.

You'll feel that it comes in long breaths. One starts at your back, leans on you, pushes, and eases off; that
is the sigh the Ridge is named for, and you'll hear it in the columns. Between one breath and the next there is a
stillness. Mostly it lasts as long as it takes to count three. Sometimes it lasts longer.

The rule of the Ridge is this: when the wind stops, you stop. Stand where you are. Don't speak, don't lift a foot, don't
set anything down, until it starts again. My mother said something listens in the stillness for whoever moves. The
copyists say there is nothing there at all, only the quiet between one breath and the next. I have seen what
the copyists haven't.

When I was a girl a carter came over the crest in a long stillness, pulling his cart in its harness the way carts are
pulled. He was late, and he could see the next lantern, and he didn't stop. A dozen of us watched him from the
columns, standing like posts. He walked on in the quiet with the cart behind him. When the wind came back he wasn't
there. The cart stood in the road with its straps hanging empty, and the ash where he had been walking lay smooth, as
if nobody had walked on it that day.

The longest stillness I ever stood in began at dusk and ended in the middle of the night. Every soul on the Ridge stood
in it, pilgrims and carters and me, and the Husks stood too, out among the columns, and in the dark you couldn't tell
which of the still shapes were which. When the wind came back, all of us walked on together, and none of us looked at
the others.

Nobody knows who raised the columns. The grandmothers on the Moor say our people raised them in the first years, as a
walk out of the wind for pilgrims, with a roof on it. The copyists say the columns were standing when the people came
out, the way the road was lying there. The wind is wearing them down from the side it comes from, a finger's width in a
lifetime. On the sheltered side you can still see the carving: people walking, every one of them going the way the wind
goes.

When you're past the last lantern, the wind will be at your back all the way down onto the Heath. Let it be. Don't turn
round to see where it's going. It's going where everything goes.

### The Widow's Stumps

*Told on the Ridge by the lantern folk, and on the Moor by the grandmothers of the camps. A copyist heard
both tellings in one autumn and wrote them on one leaf, the Ridge's first.*

In a year nobody can now name, a caravan was crossing the Ridge, and its foreman walked between the carts with a
daughter at each hand. Something came on them on the crest. Some say it was Husks, three standing among the columns who
lifted their heads all at once. Some say the wind stopped and the foreman would not stand still in the stillness,
because his girls were cold and the lantern was near. Whatever it was, he did not let go of either hand. When the next
carts came over they found no foreman and no daughters, only three stumps on the crest where the three had stood, a tall
one between two smaller, leaning together. They were warm to the touch, and they are warm still. Their tops have grown
into one another like hands that have held on too long to come apart.

Nobody can say what they are made of. The Ridge folk call them stone. Woodcutters who came up from the Wood to look
called them wood. A knight of the Pale Order cut a chip from the tallest, wrote a word in his book, crossed it out, and
wrote a number under it instead.

They are the widow's because of the foreman's wife. When the caravan did not come down onto the Moor, she walked up
looking, found the three of them standing, and sat down at their feet. She would not come away. The lantern folk sat
with her by turns, and she sat there until she died, and they buried her where she sat. That is how the Ridge tells it,
and the Ridge will not hear it told another way on the crest.

The grandmothers of the Moor tell one thing more, and they tell it quietly. One of the daughters let go. She let go of
her father's hand when whatever it was came on them, and she ran, and she lived. Her name was Nell, and the drowned kept
her a long while afterward in the village under the Fen, and nobody could ever say what they wanted with her. So there
are three stumps on the crest, the grandmothers say, and only two of them were girls who held on. Put your hand on each
in turn, and one is a little less warm than the others.

The lantern folk say all three are exactly as warm as each other, and always were, and that the grandmothers' hands are
cold.

## For building it

**The land.** A long ridge bare to the wind, drawn from real wind-scoured uplands: the quartzite crest of the
Stiperstones, with its tors and frost-sorted stone stripes; the wind-striped heath on the summits of the northern isles
(Ronas Hill in Shetland, Ward Hill on Hoy), where heather and turf lie in stripes and crescents combed by one wind; and
the holed Blowing Stone below the Ridgeway, which booms when air is driven through it.
- **Everything leans one way,** read off one wind map: heather, bilberry and crowberry in low stripes along the wind;
  windward faces scoured; ash deep in the lee of every stone and column, with a long tail behind it; lone thorn trees
  only below the crest, flagged, every branch on the downwind side.
- **The floor stays alive:** heather and crowberry mats, mat-grass tufts, lichen on every stone (grey-white, orange,
  black), ash rippled across the wind, grit, frost-shattered stone in stripes downslope, cinders blown up from the Moor,
  and dropped things a lee has kept (a glove, a strip of cloth, straw).
- **The rock:** a hard cap along the crest over softer stone in the flanks, as real ridges stand (the Ridge folk cut
  their chapel into the soft, `the-gnawed-chapel.md`). In a few small patches where the wind has taken the turf off the
  crest, the cap is pale, close-grained and warm, with a fine, close grain like old bone polished by hands. The Ridge folk call it bone; the
  copyists, a fine white stone.

**Landmarks, by the pit method.**
- **The colonnade.** One long line of columns along the crest, broken into runs where drums have fallen: loose and
  gappy, never a corridor of walls. Eleven hundred years of ash on one wind have sanded every column from one side:
  the windward face planed smooth and pale, the lee face keeping its flutes and a frieze of walking figures, all going
  downwind. Fallen drums lie in lines along the fall, half buried as at Palmyra (chapter 2); the blast has worn holes
  through some, and the wind sounds in them. A pale fine stone, bleached where the ash strikes, the flute bottoms pale
  with ash and the ribs dark. Its hidden light: the Sighing Lantern stands behind the last column, so its breathing
  glow is seen on the carved lee faces before the lantern itself. Drums 1 to 1.5 yards across; whole columns stood 6
  to 8 yards, most now 1 to 4. Older than any telling.
- **The Widow's Stumps.** Three stumps at the crest's highest point, the tallest about two and a half yards, leaning
  in, their tops fused in a twist like clasped hands. They are warm, so ash never settles on them: they stand dark and
  clean among ash-heaped columns, and rain steams off them. At their feet, the widow's grave, a low mound of stones and
  cloth strips. Their material is found nowhere else: grey and close-grained like wood, heavy and cold-polished like
  stone, so both tellings can stand.
- **The line of lanterns** (the zone's four), the far end of the lantern round, each on a stone notched with the
  tenders' tallies. The last, past the Stumps, is the Sighing Lantern: bronze, a cracked bell in its cap humming in the
  wind, its flame swelling and sinking slowly as breath does, "Aveth. She was kind about the road." scratched at the
  foot of its glass.
- **The statues and the altar** (three and one): figures of the frieze, fallen and stood up again in the lee; the altar
  at the colonnade's end, swept by the wind, the one place on the crest where nothing drifts.

**Light and weather.** Overcast, the light flat and grey. The wind is the weather: grass, heather, cloth and ash move
in long surges with dead-still pauses, on the one gust (`core/gust.gd`). Rain comes sideways and wets only the windward
faces. The scarves are ash lifted off real drifts, never fog. The lantern is the key light.

**The god showing through: a hint.** The warm stumps, the pale warm rock, and the wind itself.

**Creatures.** Husk 37, Tithe-Hand 15, Wick-Saint 9, Bloatling 6, Kneeler 4, Weeper 3, Vein-Borer 1.
- **Husks** stand among the column stumps like more stumps; at dusk the pilgrim cannot tell them apart until three
  move. In the wind's pauses they stand with everyone else.
- **Tithe-Hands** come along the crest in threes and fives; the columns give the pilgrim a wall for the back.
- **Wick-Saints** ride the wind at dusk; **Kneelers** wait on the road among the lanterns; **Weepers** keep behind
  fallen drums; **Bloatlings** drift in the lee; the one **Vein-Borer** keeps to the softer ground below the crest.
- **In a fight:** columns and drums are stone cover, and shots shatter on them; the lee slope's heather is dry and burns
  fast downwind, toward the Heath; there is no water on the crest.

**Sound.** The wind above all, rising and falling, with a low hollow note in the holed drums; the hum of the Sighing
Lantern's cracked bell; ash hissing over stone. Then the pauses: true silence, held a beat too long. Nothing alive
calls.

**Keep out.** Crows (the old maps scatter them here; they belong to the Moor); fog; red light; walls in place of
columns; any wind that turns; the old generator's landmarks shared by every zone.
