# The Lower Catacombs

*Act I · zone `cata2` (the game's Bone Catacombs II) · under the ground · creatures of levels 15–18 · borders: the
Upper Catacombs, the Smugglers' Hold · in the game: the old procedural zone, to be remade by the bog's method; the
Ossuary Matron, the act's last · the Pale Order's Count: `../../in-game-texts/02-the-pale-order.md`*

Below the kept bone the walls forget what patterns were for. Monks nobody can name dug these galleries under the
pilgrim ossuary to make room for more dead, and the lower they went the less sure the laying became, until the laid
bone and the grain of the god's own bone cannot be told apart. A ceiling has sagged into a face. In a round chamber of
skulls the count never comes out the same twice. In the last gallery a pick-head is set in the end wall with its shaft
snapped, where the diggers struck something warm, and there the Ossuary Matron stands the dead up and counts them. It
should feel deep, warm in the wrong way, and patient. The god shows plainly: below the first stairs the walls are its
bone, and the warmth is its marrow, never named in the world's voice.

**The one image:** a snapped pick-head in a wall of bone, old amber run down beneath it in hard glossy tongues and lit
from behind, and rows of the stood-up dead waiting before it in the dark.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### Two counts of one room

*A tallier of the Pale Order wrote this to the elder brother who had sent him down. A carter found it in a dead
knight's pack on the white road, and sold it.*

Brother, I have done as you asked and counted the round chamber in the lowest galleries, the one the pilgrims call
the Miscount.

It is a round room at the end of a short passage. Its wall is skulls, laid in rings from the floor to the top of a
dome, each ring a little smaller than the one beneath, so that the room closes over you like a hand. There is no dust
on its floor.

I counted it as we are taught, ring by ring from the floor, tying a knot at every ninth skull and reducing each ring
as I went. Four thousand and thirteen. Its root is eight, the root of a man: an open count, one short.

I counted it again to close it, as we are taught. Four thousand and fourteen. Its root is nine.

I did not miscount. I counted a third time with my eyes shut and my hand on each skull, and found where the two counts
parted. It is in the lowest ring, beside the door, at the height of my knee: a skull I am sure I touched the second
time and sure I did not the first. It is no different from the others, except that it is warm, and set a little
crooked, turned toward the door, as if it had looked round when I came in.

Then I went out into the passage and counted the room from the doorway, without setting foot inside. Four thousand and
thirteen.

I stood in the doorway a long while, and did not go back in. The pilgrims say that whoever counts that room twice
finds one more, and that the one is the counter. Lowland people say a great many things. I write it down because you
told me to bring back every number, and I have brought you both.

Six thousand one hundred and ten, tallier.

### Where the monks stopped

*A lowland telling of the monks who dug the lower galleries. A carter's daughter wrote it down as her grandmother told
it, who had it from hers.*

Nobody can tell you which monks they were. They wore grey, my grandmother said, or brown, and kept no name that
anyone remembers. What everyone agrees on is that the pilgrim ossuary was full.

There were more dead on the road than there were walls to lay them in, so the monks went down. They cut a stair under
the last gallery and dug a new gallery below it, and laid the dead in it the way the walls above were laid, a sermon to
a wall. When that one was full they dug another below it, and another below that. Each was a little warmer than the
one above, and in winter they were glad of it.

You can read how it went with them in the walls, my grandmother said. Their highest galleries are laid as carefully as
the ossuary's own. Lower down, a wall of hands turns halfway along into something else, as if the monk laying it had
forgotten what he began with. Lower still the bones are laid in shapes nobody has a word for, branching and joining
like the grain inside a split bone, until you cannot tell where the laid bone stops and the wall begins.

In the lowest gallery they struck something warm. The pick went into the end wall and stuck, and when the monk leaned
on it the shaft broke in his hands. What ran out round the pick-head was thick and slow and the colour of old resin,
and warm, and it set as it ran. The monks put down their tools where they stood and went up the stair, and none of them
was seen again. The pick-head is in the wall still.

The roof of the gallery before the last came down the next year, slowly, over a whole winter, and when it stopped it
had settled into a face. My grandmother never saw it. Her grandmother did, and said only that she could not tell whose
it was, and did not want to.

The bones down there are warm, and if you lean on them the warmth goes into you. My grandmother told me not to lean on
them. She never said why.

### End the count

*A lowland woman's telling at a wake, when the talk turned to the ossuary. The reeve's clerk, who was sitting up with
the dead that night too, wrote it down.*

My grandfather went down to the ossuary on a board, the way our dead always went, and my mother walked
beside him as far as the stair. She told me about the matron there.

There was always a matron at the head of the stair; when one grew too old, another took her beads. She met every
company that came down with a body and counted it in, one bead off her string for each of the dead, so that none would
be laid twice and none forgotten where no sermon reached. She said the number aloud over each one, and that was all
the name it got. The matron who counted my grandfather was so old she counted with her eyes shut.

She was the last. Nobody came to take her beads, and when the monks went down to dig, she went down after them to
count what they laid, because it was hers to count. My mother believed she is down there still, standing the dead up
in rows the better to count them, and beginning again whenever she reaches the end, because the number is never the
same twice.

Our old women tell it differently. They say so many people asked the matron to keep their dead for
them, and went on asking so long, that when she died she could not lie down. Too many were still asking. A thing that
enough people ask for gets up in the end and answers, they say, whether it wants to or not.

The knights from the white mountains say there was never any such woman, and that whoever counts the dead standing up
has it all backward: the dead are counted lying down, in their walls, by brothers, once a year.

Whoever she is, we say the same words for our dead who went down there, at every wake. My mother said them for my
grandfather, and I will say them for her. Those who went down and came back have cut them into the steps of the last
stair, over and over in every kind of hand, so that she will see them whenever she comes up.

End the count. Let them lie.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Drawn from:** the deepest quarry galleries under Paris, where bone heaps lose their facing; and the inside of a
  sawn bone, its spongy lattice of struts and plates, at the size of a hall.
- **The cutting:** hand-dug galleries 4 to 5 yards wide and about 3 high, wandering, joined by short stairs. Going
  down, the cut faces change from pale stone to a dense smooth band (the god's hard outer bone), then to the lattice.
- **The walls:** careful courses at the top, then patterns that change halfway along, then branching shapes that
  follow the lattice until the laid bone and the god's cannot be told apart.
- **The floor is never bare:** bone grit and a fine warm dust; snapped struts lying like dry twigs; tools the monks
  dropped where they stood (a shovel, a basket half full of bone, a lamp); paths worn between the Matron's rows.

**Landmarks.**
1. **The Miscount.** *Shape:* a round chamber of skulls in narrowing rings closing into a dome, down a short passage.
   *Cause:* no dust on the floor; one skull by the door set crooked, turned toward it; counts scratched up the passage
   by those who came to count, each run ending one higher. *Material:* skull bone, the lowest rings rubbed smooth by
   counting hands. *Light:* a thread of warm light under the lowest ring, as if the room were set down on something
   lit. *Age:* the oldest counts nearly worn away.
2. **The Face.** *Shape:* in the gallery before the last, the roof slumped into a mass that meets the floor, its front a
   face too large to take in close: brow, closed eyes, a mouth a little open. *Cause:* the old roof's laid bones
   dragged down and stretched into its skin; the monks' props bowed under its chin; a crack across one cheek.
   *Material:* bone gone soft and set again, glossy where it stretched. *Light:* the last gallery's warmth spills in
   along the floor and catches the mouth and chin; the brow stays dark. The camera sees only walls, so the face is the
   slump's front, where it meets the floor.
3. **The Snapped Pick.** *Shape:* the last and widest gallery, ending in a blank wall of dense bone with the pick-head
   set in it at chest height, the broken shaft below, and the Matron's stood-up dead in rows facing the stair. *Cause:*
   the amber that ran out round the pick set in tongues down the wall and pooled in a hard sheet; the monks' tools where
   they fell; numbers scratched on the brows of the stood dead; END THE COUNT cut over and over on the last stair's
   treads. *Material:* dense bone; the set marrow glossy and translucent as old resin. *Light:* a dull amber glow from
   the crack round the pick-head, shining through the set tongues from behind and putting the front rows in silhouette;
   it breathes, slowly. Never red, held dark near the lantern. *Age:* the oldest tongues dark and crazed. The gallery is
   open, with ways round it, never sealed.

**Light and weather.** No weather. The lantern is the key light; the zone's lantern-stone stands at the Warm Wall by
the stair in. Elsewhere, black. No red light, no fog.

**The god showing through: plainly.** Its bone in the walls, the lattice, the warmth, the set marrow, the Face; no
flesh, which belongs deeper.

**Creatures.** The game's mix: Ossuary Weepers 48, Marrow Duelists 32, Gravebloats 16, Husks 10, Mire Gasps 8, and the
Ossuary Matron.
- **The Duelists and Weepers** are the Matron's dead, standing in her rows until she calls them (in the game, at half
  her life). **Gravebloats and Husks** came down from above; **Mire Gasps** strayed down from the wet country.
- **The Ossuary Matron:** the tellings disagree on whose she was. She still wears the crypt master's figure tinted
  bone-white and needs her own. A proposal: a tall, stooped keeper in the ossuary's old coif and a long apron gone
  brown, an empty counting-string wound round both wrists, counting under her breath.
- **By material:** the lattice is cover whose struts snap under force; bone shatters shots; the Miscount has one way
  in, a trap for whoever is chased into it; the Matron's rows are cover until she calls them.

**Sound.** The Matron counting, a whisper of numbers carried along the galleries that stops when the pilgrim stops;
bone ticking as it settles; blades somewhere ahead; in the last gallery, a slow pulse felt more than heard.

**Keep out.** Red light; glowing eye-holes; flesh; a boss room that closes; any "way opens behind her"; skeletons
played for laughs; bones of anything but people.

**Open questions.**

- Her rows as a set piece that wakes when she calls?
