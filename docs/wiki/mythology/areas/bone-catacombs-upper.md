# The Upper Catacombs

*Act I · zone `cata1` (the game's Bone Catacombs I) · under the ground · creatures of levels 14–16 · borders: the
Drowned Fen, the Lower Catacombs, the Plague Hospice, the Root Deep · in the game: the old procedural zone, to be
remade by the bog's method · how the Pale Order lays its own dead, which is nothing like this:
`../../in-game-texts/02-the-pale-order.md`*

From the reeds at the edge of the Drowned Fen the ossuary stair goes down into the pilgrim ossuary. For longer than
the lowlands remember, pilgrims who died on the road were carried here by those who walked on and laid into the walls
without their names, and the walls were laid as sermons: a gallery of thigh-bones, a gallery of jaws, and the Cup
Wall, where skull-caps lie cup-down like roof tiles and one touched cap sets ten thousand ringing. Nobody keeps it
now. Bone knights walk the galleries and salute each other as they pass, fencers of bone practise their forms in the
dark, and in a bay off the long gallery a book lies open, written in the hand of whoever reads it. It should feel
kept: grave, ordered and very quiet, the order beginning to slip near the stair down. Part of the god (a proposal):
the bone of its neck, behind the Fen's throat; it shows only as a grain in the stone and a warmth up the stair.

**The one image:** a curved wall of skull-caps rising like a tiled roof stood on end, a band polished pale at hand
height where pilgrims touched it, and a thin grey shaft of daylight falling on it from somewhere far above.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### The walls, read aloud

*How the old keepers of the ossuary preached the walls to pilgrims, walking ahead of them with a lamp. A pilgrim
wrote it down from memory that night at the head of the stair, and a lowland scriptorium kept her leaf.*

Keep your lamps low, and keep close. The galleries are long, and it is easy to stop at a wall and find your company
gone on without you.

The dead in these walls were pilgrims, like you. They died on the road, of cold and fever and the things that come
up out of the ground, and the ones who walked on carried them here and laid them in. They were laid without their
names. On the road a name is the first thing you lose, and the walls have no need of one. A wall says what it has to
say whoever is in it.

This first wall is thigh-bones, laid with their ends toward you, the whole gallery long. Put your hand flat on it.
Every bone in it walked. Each one carried somebody down a road like yours until it could carry nothing, and then
somebody carried it. That is the first sermon: you were carried here, and you will be carried.

This next wall is jaws, and it is the quiet one. They have said everything they had to say. Pilgrims do not speak in
this gallery, and I will not either, until we are through it.

Now the Cup Wall. These are the crowns of the dead, laid cup-down like tiles on a roof, from the floor to the top of
the vault. Each of them held a mind once. Now each is turned down, so that nothing more can be poured in and nothing
taken out. Lay two fingers on one and say the name of one of your dead, quietly, and listen. Every cap touches its
neighbours, and the name goes round all of them, ten thousand and more, faint as a bell rung in another valley, so
that everyone laid here will know your dead when they come. Do not say your own name to it. Keep that behind your
teeth, here as everywhere.

If you hear singing from the stair down, it is none of ours, and you are not to join in. You know why. They know the
tune.

The last wall is not finished. My teacher never finished it, nor hers, and I will not. A sermon has to stop
somewhere. This one stops here, and you finish it yourselves on the road home.

You will want to know who laid all this. I cannot tell you. My teacher said the first pilgrims laid their own dead,
company by company, learning the pattern from the walls already there. The knights from the white mountains came down
once and counted a gallery, and went up again without saying the number, because nothing here was laid with its name.
The old women of the Fen say the walls were here before the first pilgrim came down, and the pilgrims only learned to
read them. Believe whichever you like. The walls do not mind.

Now we go up the way we came, and nobody looks back down the stair.

### The page one line ahead

*A copyist's letter to the master of the scriptorium that sent him to copy the book in the Reader's Bay. It was found
at the head of the ossuary stair with his copy folded inside it, which his master has never unfolded.*

You asked me for a copy of the book in the Reader's Bay. I have made one, and first I will tell you how.

The bay opens off the long gallery past the Cup Wall: a small room with a lectern at the back. A lantern hangs low
beside the lectern, shaded on the side toward the door, so that the only lit thing in the room is the page. The book
lies open near its beginning. One leaf has been turned, and no more.

I set down my lamp and leaned over it, and the hand on the page was mine: the hand you taught me, with the long tail I
give my letters at the end of a line, which you have scolded me for these nine years. It told how a copyist leaned
over the book in a bay off the long gallery, and found that the hand on the page was his own.

I read on, and it was always one line ahead of me. When I took out my pen, it said I took out my pen. When I began to
copy, what I copied was what it said. I thought I would get ahead of it. I wrote a thing no book could know, the name
of the girl at the mill at home, and when I looked up the page had her name already, in my hand, with the long tail.

Then I understood that I was only copying, and I put down the pen, and the page said I put down the pen. I did not
read what it said after that. I laid my coat over the book and went out backward, the way you leave a room where
someone is asleep.

The pilgrims tell of a reading-stand somewhere in the old ruins of the world, with a book on it that holds the life of
whoever reads it, a page ahead. I cannot say whether this is that book. I know that it has a second leaf, and that I
did not turn it.

At the head of the stair, in the daylight, I read over my sheets. They go on further than I remember copying. The
last lines tell how a copyist sat at the head of the stair and wrote to the master who had sent him, and folded his
copy inside the letter, and did not look at what came after.

I am folding it in now. I have not looked.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Drawn from:** the Capuchin Crypt in Rome, each room laid from one kind of bone; the Paris Catacombs, long bones
  faced ends-out with bands of skulls and loose bone heaped behind, in old quarry galleries; the ossuary at Sedlec.
  Take their forms, never their crosses or crests.
- **The cutting:** straight galleries 5 to 7 yards wide and 3 to 4 high in a soft chalky stone the colour of old
  bone, pick marks in rows, bays off them, stairs going down in flights.
- **The walls:** each gallery one sermon in one kind of bone (thigh-bones ends-out banded with skulls; jaws in arcs;
  the Cup Wall), a face about 2.5 yards high with loose bone heaped to the roof behind it. A skull is a fifth of a yard
  (4 px), so skull bands and caps are real form; a thigh-bone's end is a pixel or two, so the courses between are
  fine grain (MASTER_RULES 0.3).
- **The floor is never bare:** bone grit and dust drifted against the walls, fallen finger-bones and teeth, runs of
  old wax, chalked marks, knotted cords, pilgrims' badges, a copper in a crack, a trodden path down each gallery.
- **Dry under a fen:** the Fen's water does not wet, so where it seeps through the roof it creeps down the bone as a
  clear film and leaves the galleries dry as chalk.

**Landmarks.**
1. **The Cup Wall.** *Shape:* a round chamber where three galleries meet, rising into a low dome of skull-caps laid
   cup-down, each course lapping the one below like tiles. *Cause:* every cap touches its neighbours, which is why one
   rings them all; a band at hand height is polished pale by fingers, with names scratched faint in it; a few caps are
   prised out. *Material:* thin bone, pale cream on the crowns, dark in the cups, the skull's zigzag seams fine dark lines
   across each cap. *Light:* a light-well at the crown, its top out of sight, lets down a thin grey shaft (the moon's
   at night) that catches the rims in rings. *Age:* the low courses yellow-brown and worn.
2. **The Reader's Bay.** *Shape:* a small bay off the long gallery, the book open on a lectern. *Cause:* the step worn
   into two hollows; a coat slid off the book onto the floor; dust everywhere but on the lectern and the book.
   *Material:* dark old wood worn pale at the edges; rag paper that never yellows. *Light:* the zone's lantern-stone
   hangs beside the lectern, shaded toward the door, so only the page is lit. *Age:* the room old, the book new.
3. **The Unfinished Wall.** *Shape:* the last gallery's face stops partway, its top course ragged, the pilgrims' altar
   (the game's one altar) before it and the stair down beside it. *Cause:* each company laid a course of its own dead,
   so the courses do not line up; on the altar, what pilgrims left. *Light:* round the stair's first turn, out of
   sight, the Lower Catacombs' lantern-stone at the Warm Wall lights the steps and the last course from beneath, and
   warm air comes up with it. *Age:* even the newest course is long old.

**Light and weather.** No weather reaches here. The lantern is the key light, raking along the courses so every skull
has a lit lip and a shadow; beyond its pool, black. No fog, no red light.

**The god showing through: a little.** Near the stair down the stone shows a grain of pores and growth lines, like
sawn bone, and is faintly warm. The bones in the walls are people's.

**Creatures.** The game's mix: Ossuary Weepers 45, Husks 41, Gravebloats 29, Marrow Duelists 28, Ossuary Wardens 7,
Mire Gasps 2.
- **Ossuary Weepers:** by one telling, mourners who came down with their dead and never went up, faces worn smooth
  against the walls. They keep behind the ends of the walls; bone stops their needles, so break their line of sight.
- **Husks** stand among the walls until three gather (fight them at a gallery's mouth); a **Gravebloat**'s burst fills
  a gallery's width.
- **Marrow Duelists:** fencers of bone, heard before they are seen, practising their forms for no one.
- **Ossuary Wardens:** bone knights behind skull-faced tower-shields (the breath-house next door takes them for the
  Pale Order's standing dead), walking in pairs and lowering their shields a hand's breadth to each other as they
  pass. The same motion comes before a blow: a Warden that lowers its shield to the pilgrim is giving no salute.
- **Mire Gasps** are strays from the wet country. **Choristers** belong here by the lore (the singing from the stair
  down), but the game's mix has none.
- **By material:** the bone faces shatter shots; the loose heaps behind a slumped face give way under force; almost
  nothing burns.

**Sound.** The Cup Wall ringing whenever anything touches it; dust trickling from a face; bone plate creaking
at a turn; blades ticking out of sight. No drip, though a fen lies overhead: that silence is the strangest thing here.

**Keep out.** Crosses or any real church image; bones that glow; skeletons played for laughs; bones of anything but
people; named niches in the Pale Order's manner (these dead are nameless); red light; fog.

**Open questions.**
