# The Fallen Watchtower

*Act I · zone `fallen_watchtower` · outdoors · creatures of levels 6–9 · borders: the Old Barrow · in the game: the old
procedural moor map (124 × 124), to be remade by the bog's method · the sky-readers' charcoal on its stair is the
Codex's "The Sky over the Hide, Forty Years Read"*

On a granite rise at the edge of the Ashen Moor, where the ash runs down to the barrow-field, stands the stump of a
round stone tower. The families who bury at the Barrows built it to keep watch, because the dead there come up and walk
home, and hung a bell in its top to wake the families when they did. Most of the tower fell, in one long tongue of stone
down the slope. Its bell went into the ground, and it still rings under the turf when the wind is right, though nobody
strikes it. Later two sky-readers, a master and then his pupil, roofed the stump, read the sky from it for seventy-one
years and wrote what they saw on the inside wall, climbing with the stair. Nobody lives there now, and the writing has
gone on climbing.

It is the barrow country seen from above: open high ground over the Old Barrow, all wind and heather and ash. The god
shows only in the warmth of the ash and in the hollow where the bell lies.

**The one image:** the broken tower against a grey sky, a red thread on a pole on its roof pointing away past the Ridge,
and below it the barrow-field, every hollow leaning toward the ring of teeth.

### The watch bell

*Told by an old man of the families who bury at the Barrows to a pilgrim who asked why they all go quiet when the wind
comes from the tower. The pilgrim wrote it on the back of his road-pass.*

Before there was a tower, we took turns sitting up. Every night one of the men sat on the rise with a lantern and
watched the Barrows, and when one of ours came up out of a lid and started home, he ran down shouting, and everybody got
up with the spades and the flat stones. But men fall asleep, and too often the dead got to the doors first. So we
built the tower, high enough to see every ring from the top, and hung a bell in it, and a man sat up there every night
with the rope in his hand. One stroke meant one of them was up, and two meant more than one. At three strokes every door
was barred until morning.

The bell had words cast round its rim, as bells do. I won't say them. It is still out there, and it might answer.

My grandmother saw the tower fall. She said the bell rang on its own one night for a long time, more strokes than
anybody could count, and every family sat behind its doors and listened. In the morning the tower had fallen down the
slope toward the Barrows like a man asleep on his feet, and there was no bell anywhere in it. The diggers tell it
otherwise. They say the ground under the tower sank from the middle, as the lids do, and the tower leaned into the
hollow and went over, and the bell went down into it and the turf closed over it. The man who sits at every fire says
the bell moved and the tower stayed where it was. Nobody has ever got him to say what he meant.

It still rings under the turf when the wind comes from the right quarter, never more than once at a time. When we hear
it we go out with the lanterns and walk the rings, as we did when there was a man in the tower, and in all my life we
have not once found anybody up. My grandmother said that was because it no longer rings for the Barrows. I asked her who
it rang for, then, and she said for whoever is listening, of course.

After the fall nobody would sit up in the broken stump at night, so our people cut watchmen out of the moor-stone and
stood them on the slope below, facing the Barrows, each with a hollow in its hands to hold a lantern. Somebody always
sits up, the grandmothers say. Now it is them.

Over the tower door a line is cut in old letters that says the watch saw the god fall. My grandmother laughed at that.
If anybody had been standing outside to watch it fall, she said, where would we have come out of?

### Two shrines at the tower's foot

*A leaf from the day-book of a purifier of the Shrine Keepers, written on her way to the Fen, and copied for a pilgrim
at the Well-Shrine.*

At the foot of the broken tower on the barrow rise I found two shrines of the small gods, forgotten: a stone with a cup
worn in it where the wind comes round the tower, and two posts and a lintel by the old door, with nothing left of the
paper. Whoever set them set them where the wind snags, which was right. Both guardian stones had turned from the road. I
knelt and turned them back, and breathed on their faces until the dust moved, and the air was easier.

In the morning both had turned again, down the slope, toward the low hump in the turf where the barrow families say
their bell is buried. A forgotten stone turns to the wall; these had turned to the hump, both the same distance, as if
they had heard the same thing at the same moment.

I turned them back a second time and sat up with them. Some time before the grey the wind came round off the Moor, and
under the ground there was one stroke, so low that I felt it in my knees before I heard it. I watched the nearer stone,
and it did not move while I watched. I closed my eyes to listen for a second stroke, and when I opened them it was
facing the hump.

My elders teach that a stone turns away in grief at being forgotten. Some of the old ones say it turns to listen to
something on the far side. I have never been sure which I believe. These two are not grieving. I have left them facing
the bell. It did not seem mine to turn them from it.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Open upland moor,** falling to the barrow-field. Draw it from Dartmoor: granite tors with aprons of clitter, heather
  and whortleberry on the dry slopes, purple moor-grass and bracken, cotton-grass and peat pools in the hollows, lone
  thorns bent in the lee of the rocks. Over it lies the Moor's warm grey ash, with the heather growing up grey through
  it.
- **The tower:** from the round towers of Ireland (Glendalough, Clonmacnoise, Kilmacduagh): tall, slender bell-houses of
  coursed stone, with the door set high off the ground and small slit windows, many of them stumps now.
- **True scale:** the tower 5 yards across at the base and once about 30 high, its walls a yard thick; the stump about
  11 yards high under the readers' roof of timber and slate; the door 3 yards up, reached by a stone stair the readers
  built against the wall; the fallen tongue some 40 yards long.
- **The floor is never bare:** ash drifts with a tail behind every stone; heather cushions and moor-grass tussocks
  greyed with ash; rust-brown dead bracken in drifts; lichen on every block; fallen blocks half sunk, with ash in their
  joints; slate shards; candle ends and charcoal stubs at the stair foot.

**Landmarks.**
- **The tower stump** (the zone's signature):
  - *Shape:* a broken round tower on the crest, the only tall thing for leagues; from the game's camera, a dark drum
    with a low roof and a pole.
  - *Story by cause:*
    - it fell toward the Barrows and lies downhill in a long tongue, the dressed blocks farthest out in rows that keep
      their coursing, the core rubble in a ridge at the stump's foot (chapter 2's fallen wall);
    - the readers' roof sits low on the broken top, and the red thread on its pole points away past the Ridge;
    - over the door, the line in old letters;
    - inside, the charcoal line climbing with the stair, in the master's hand, then the pupil's, and above the pupil's
      last line the same hand going on;
    - on the lintel inside the door, two columns of the count of the cold lights in the night sky, each figure lower
      than the one above it.
  - *Material:* grey moor-stone ashlar, its arrises rounded, gold and grey with lichen on the weather side; soot-black
    inside.
  - *Light:* inside, grey light from the slits falls in bars across the charcoal line, its source unseen.
  - *Age:* the tower centuries old; the fall some grandmothers ago; the roof two lifetimes old.
- **The Buried Bell:** a low hump at the foot of the fallen tongue, where the turf has sunk in a ring the width of a
  bell's mouth. One curve of the rim shows above the turf, bronze gone green and black, its cast letters filled with
  ash, polished where pilgrims have touched it. Beside it a rent in the turf looks down into a hollow where the bell
  hangs mouth-down over a patch of pale, warm hide, lit only by a faint cold light from the hide itself. When the wind
  is right it goes through the rent and the bell gives one stroke, and the ash on the turf round it jumps.
- **The stone watchmen** (the game's five statues): rough moor-stone figures a little taller than a man, in a line down
  the slope, facing the Barrows, each holding a sooted hollow at its chest. The wind has worn their faces blank; ash
  drifts against their backs and tails away in front. One has fallen on its face and been left there.
- **The two shrines:** a cup-stone and two posts and a lintel, both guardian stones facing the Buried Bell.
- **The altar:** the game places one. Make it the squat block below the tower door, its top crusted white where the
  barrow families laid salt for the watchman each dusk.

**Light and weather.**
- Overcast by day, moonlit by night. The lantern is the key light; the game's two lanterns stand on the road up the rise
  and at the tower's foot.
- The wind is constant, from the Moor out past the Sighing Ridge, and everything leans with it: heather, grass, thorns,
  ash tails, and the red thread, which lifts and falls and never turns.
- Rain acts by material: the granite darkens and streams down the tower's weather face, the readers' roof leaks onto the
  stair in one place, the ash crusts and runs in rills, and the peat pools ring.
- No fog. No red light: the thread's red is only its colour.

**The god showing through: a hint, and once more than a hint.** The ash is warm in every season, and from the crest the
whole barrow-field reads as one breathing thing, every hollow leaning one way. Under the bell, once, the hide itself.

**Creatures** (the game's mix):
- **Husks (40):** the Barrows' risen, walking homeward up the slope or standing on the open ground. Open ground
  has few doorways: take them at the tower door, in the gaps of the fallen tongue and between two watchmen.
- **Weepers (15):** behind the fallen blocks and the watchmen, which stop their needles. Go round and close.
- **Bloatlings (10).**
- **Wick-Saints of the Canopy (9) and Wick-Saints (4):** under open sky they swoop long and climb high; at dusk they
  come off the barrow-field and circle the tower.
- **Gasps (8):** the wind brings them over the rise; they shy from the lantern.
- **Vein-Borers (4):** under the soft ash and the peat; never under the road or on the granite.
- **Pyre-Saints (4):** dry heather and dead bracken catch from their footprints and the fire runs downwind; the peat
  pools and the wet ground put them out.
- **Objects:** two lanterns, one waystone, five chests, two shrines, five statues (the watchmen), one altar. Granite
  stops shots and gives cover.

**Sound.** The wind over the heather and through the tower's slits, rising and falling; the red thread snapping on its
pole; slates ticking; and once in a long while, under the turf, a single low stroke felt in the feet before it is heard.
Below, the barrow-field is silent.

**Keep out.**
- A garrison: it was the barrow families' watch, with no banners or weapon racks.
- The old whisper's "watchman's horn".
- Crows: they are allowed on the Moor itself; keep them off the rise unless Derek says otherwise.
- Fog; red light.
