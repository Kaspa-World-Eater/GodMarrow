# Fern Gully

*Act I · zone `fern_gully` · outdoors, 132 × 172 · creatures of levels 3–6 · borders: the Burnt Heath, the Hunter's
Cache · in the game: the old procedural map (`fern_gully_s1001` to `s1020`, on the Hollow Wood's ground), to be remade
by the bog's method · the small gods of springs and fords: `../../in-game-texts/06-the-myriad.md`*

Between the Burnt Heath and the hunters' heather, the ground folds into a deep, narrow cut, and in the bottom of it
everything is wet and green. The god's breath passes over the top without coming down, so the air in the cut is still
and damp. A stream runs along its floor over pale stones, and its walls are hung from the stream to the lip in ferns.
The Heath's fire has come to its lip again and again and never once gone down into it. It is the only green place on the
dry side of the act, and it should feel wrong for that: the water tastes of iron, the fern fronds uncurl as the pilgrim
passes and curl shut behind, and something stands among the red berries at the ford and watches who picks them.

The gully is a fold in the Hide, wet as folds of skin are wet. The god shows here a little more than on the Heath: in
the spring, in the fronds, and in the ring of ferns on the floor below the ford.

**The one image:** a slot of grey sky high overhead, walls of fern on both sides from the stream to the lip, and fronds
curling shut along the path behind the pilgrim like fingers closing.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### Why the fire stops at the lip

*Told by a hunter's mother to the children at the hunters' fires beyond the gully, and written down long after by one
of those children, who had gone to the copyists to learn her letters.*

When the Heath burned, the year after the god died, the fire went everywhere the wind sent it. It went over the low
ground and the high, and round the pools, and it came to the lip of the gully, all along it, the whole length, and
looked down, and did not go in. That is why the heather on our side has never burned.

My mother said that was because the gully is wet, and that is true as far as it goes. The air down there is always wet,
even when the Heath is so dry the heather snaps if you look at it. The wind goes over the top of the gully and never
comes down into it, so nothing down there ever dries. Water comes out of the walls. You'll see it running down the
ferns in threads.

But my mother's mother said the gully is wet for the same reason the crease of your arm is wet on a hot day, and the
inside of your hand when you have held it shut. The ground folded there when the god lay down, she said, and a fold
keeps its wet. She said the ferns are the god's small fingers, growing up through the fold where the skin is soft, and
that is why they curl and uncurl when you pass: they are trying to remember what they used to hold.

The stream that runs along the bottom is real water, and cold, and it comes up out of a spring at the head of the gully,
under a slab of stone. It tastes of iron, the way your mouth does when you have bitten your tongue. The Shrine Keepers
have set a stone at the spring with white paper tied round it, and they bow to it when they pass and never ask what it
is called. We drink the water anyway. There is no other.

Below the ford there is a ring of ferns on the floor of the gully, as round as if it had been drawn with a cord and a
peg. The fronds all lean in toward the middle, and the middle is bare, and nothing stands in it for long. A Hand that
runs into it closes, the way a fist closes, and stays closed. A man who steps into it finds he would like to kneel, and
then he would like to lie down, and if somebody does not pull him out by the back of his belt, he does. My mother's
mother said the ring is where the fold is deepest. The hunters say it is where a great Hand went down into the floor of
the gully when the god fell, and closed there, and that the ring is its fingertips, and a closed hand keeps whatever is
put in it.

So keep out of the ring, leave the berries at the ford alone, and when you drink at the spring, bow to the stone.

### The Berry-Warden

*Told among the women of the heath's edge, and set down by a road-warden who wanted to know why they spoke aloud to
nobody when they went down to the ford. He kept it with his notices.*

Below the spring, where the gully widens and the stream runs shallow over pale stones, there is a ford, and on the far
bank a thicket of small trees with berries as red as a cut. The berries never fall and never wither, winter or summer.
Our grandmothers went down to the ford every autumn and picked a handful each, and strung them on thread, and tied a
string round the wrist of every child at the heath's edge, against the things that come up. A child wearing the ford's
berries, they said, would be passed over.

In our grandmothers' day a woman kept the ford. She lived in a hut on the near bank, and she showed the mothers which
branches to take from and how many, and would not let anyone take more than they needed. The mothers called her the
Berry-Warden, and they meant it kindly. When she was old she stopped coming up out of the gully, and nobody went down
to see why for a whole winter, because the Heath was burning near the lip that year. In the spring her hut was empty,
and the thicket had grown over its door.

The mothers went on picking, and that is when it began to be seen. Something stands in the thicket while you pick. It
is tall and thin and bent, a woman by the shape, and it is only ever at the edge of your eye; look straight at it and
there is nothing but branches. It stops nobody. It watches how much you take. The mothers said it was the Warden, still
keeping the ford, and they took a handful each and no more.

Then one autumn a woman took two handfuls, because one of her children was sickly and she meant to give that one two
strings. She felt it come out of the thicket behind her as she turned to go. She did not look round. She stood still
with her back to it and said out loud what the second handful was for, and why. Then she walked up out of the gully
with it behind her the whole way, near enough to touch, and at the lip it was gone. Her sickly child lived to be old.

Since then the mothers say aloud, at the ford, what every handful is for. The hunters laugh at them. The hunters say
the thing in the thicket is the same one the woodcutters see in the tall grass at the Wood's edge, and that it was never
the Warden, and does not care about berries or reasons; it only waits for you to turn your back. The mothers say the
hunters have never once been let off, and that this is because they never say what anything is for.

## For building it

**The land.** A ravine cut into dry heath country, drawn from the real thing:
- the ghylls of the High Weald, steep wet sandstone ravines in the heaths of Ashdown Forest, where Atlantic ferns and
  mosses live on in the still, damp air;
- the wooded gills cut into the northern moors;
- for the walls, Fern Canyon on the Pacific coast (Prairie Creek), hung from stream to lip in five-finger ferns.

The trees are the wet coast's: red alder along the water, bigleaf maple hung with moss and licorice fern, and at the
ford, red elder, whose real folklore has a keeper who must be asked before anything is taken.
- **The cut, by cause.** The gully runs across the one wind, so the wind passes over it. Its walls are 8 to 15 yards
  high, steep and seeping; the floor is 6 to 20 yards wide, with a stream over pale stones and gravel bars, flood logs
  jammed across it, and a spring at its head. On the Heath's side the heather is charred to the very lip and stops dead,
  the first fern hanging just below the burn; on the far lip the heather is old and has never burned.
- **Plants:** five-finger and lady ferns curtaining the walls; hard fern and polypody on the ledges; mosses and
  liverworts on every stone and log; fiddleheads everywhere, some always curled.
- **The floor stays alive:** fallen fronds, moss, wet gravel, alder leaves, twigs, flood-wrack, a few bones in the
  gravel. Never a plain green.

**Landmarks, by the pit method.**
- **The fern walls.** Both walls sheer and hung with ferns, the sky a slot above. Seeps run out of the walls in threads
  and feed the ferns; where a seep runs rust-dark with the iron, the ferns grow larger and darker. Where a curtain has
  slipped, the wall beneath is pale, warm and creased like the skin over a knuckle. The light comes down the slot and
  bounces green off the wet walls; the lantern lights the fronds from beneath. As old as the fall.
- **The spring and its stone** (one of the three shrines): a pool at the gully's head under an overhanging slab, water
  welling up through pale stones stained rust where it rises, and along the pool's floor a pale cord lying like a root,
  beating very slowly. A standing stone beside it, wrapped in white paper strips, some new.
- **The ford and the Berry-Warden's thicket.** The stream widens over pale stones; on the far bank, a dense thicket of
  red elder, its berries red as a cut all year; under it on the near bank, the Warden's hut, turf and wattle, the
  thicket grown over its door. A worn path to the berries, thread ends, dropped berries. The ford's lantern leans away
  from the thicket, which is the darkest place in the gully. The Stalker Crone of the mix belongs here, seen only at the
  edge of the eye.
- **The ring of ferns.** A perfect ring 6 yards across on the floor below the ford, every frond leaning in, the middle
  bare, soft, dark and faintly warm; a peg and a cord left at its edge by some hunter. Its hidden light: at night a
  faint pale foxfire in the bare middle, seen only on the inner faces of the fronds.

**Light and weather.** Darker than the Heath, green-grey, lit through the slot. Rain does not fall straight in: it comes
as drip off the lip and the fronds, as stemflow down the alders, and as rings on the pools. The wet is in the surfaces,
never in the air, so no fog. The lantern is the key light; the four lanterns stand along the stream.

**The god showing through: a little more than on the Heath.** The iron in the water, the pale cord in the spring, the
creased wall behind the ferns, the fronds closing like fingers, the ring.

**Creatures.** Weeper 27, Wick-Saint 15, Tithe-Hand 14, Vein-Borer 12, Husk 9, Pyre-Saint 3, Stalker Crone 1,
Wick-Saint of the Canopy 1.
- **Weepers,** the most, stand on the ledges of the walls and behind fern curtains with long sight down the cut; the
  pilgrim breaks their line at the bends and under the overhangs.
- **Tithe-Hands** hide among the ferns: fronds curl, and the pilgrim cannot tell which are fingers until they run.
- **Vein-Borers** come up through the soft wet floor, never through the stream's stones or the ford, which are the
  safe ground.
- **Wick-Saints** come out of the damp at dusk; **Husks** wait at the ford; the three **Pyre-Saints** come down off
  the burning Heath, and in the gully their burning hisses.
- **The Stalker Crone,** one, at the ford: the gully's people call her the Berry-Warden.
- **In a fight:** the walls block shots; the stream douses a Pyre-Saint; wet ferns will not burn, though the dry fronds
  on the lip will; flood logs are low cover.

**Sound.** Water everywhere: the stream over stones, drip off the fronds, seeps trickling. The wind goes over the lip
high above. Ferns rustle as they curl; insects hum in the damp. At the ring, nothing.

**Keep out.** Any berry eaten, or any word of food; fog; red light; tree ferns of the tropics; crows; the old
generator's shared landmarks (Aumbrel Beneath the Bark and the Door Above the Wood among them).

**Open questions for Derek.**
- The gully's trees: the wet coast's alder and maple, or the Hollow Wood's pale vein-trees (the game now puts the gully
  on the Wood's ground).
- Whether the ring of ferns makes what stands in it kneel, as a thing of play: creatures and pilgrim alike.
- Whether the Berry-Warden is the Stalker Crone. The tellings disagree; we keep them so.
