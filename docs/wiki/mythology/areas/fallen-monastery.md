# The Fallen Monastery

*Act I · zone `fallen_monastery` · under the ground, half its great room open to the sky · creatures of levels 7–9 ·
borders: the Hollow Crypt, the Bog-Witch's Shack · in the game: the old procedural crypt map (120 × 120), to be remade
by the bog's method · the Pale Order: `../../in-game-texts/02-the-pale-order.md`*

Below the Hollow Crypt, where the low road turns down toward the wet country, the Pale Order cut a house into the rock,
and the lowlanders call it the monastery. The knights coming down from the white mountains each spring rested their dead
and their chests there, and a few brothers lived in it all year, keeping the house, its count and its night office. Its
great room was cut under a vault of living rock, and one night the vault came down on one side: half the nave is under
rock and half under the grey sky, and the rain falls into the font. The altar faces away from the pews, toward the rock
wall and the crypts beyond it. The brothers turned it, the lowlanders say, the night of the fall.

It was a small house, far from the Gilded Peak, which fell elsewhere and in another year; nothing of the Peak belongs
here. It should feel stern and broken open, a dry, sooted half beside a wet, green half. It sits over the same dark as
the crypt, and its floor is thin.

**The one image:** a nave cut in the rock, half its vault gone to grey sky, rain falling into the font, and the pews
facing the plain back of an altar that has turned to the wall.

## Found in the world

*In-game writing: texts a pilgrim can find here, in the world's own voices.*

### The night office

*Told by a woman who as a girl lived above the house and did the brothers' washing, and heard it fall. Her grandson, a
copyist, wrote it down.*

Every night of my girlhood the brothers sang. They sang after dark and on into the night, deep voices all together, nine
lines and a rest, nine lines and a rest, and it came up out of the ground under our feet like a cellar full of bells.
My mother hated it. On the Moor you do not sing at night; the things out there know the tune. She went down to the house
more than once to tell them so, and the brothers thanked her, and counted her, I think, and went on singing. They said
they had sung at night since the night of the fall. By the fall they meant the night their Mother fell, when the god
came down, and they said their house had been standing then. My mother said that was the Order's way: they were there
first, whatever it was.

The winter I was twelve, the singing changed. I was lying awake, and I heard the brothers stop for their rest, and in
the rest something else went on singing. It was further down, under them, slower, with more voices than there were
brothers in the house, and it was singing their tune. When the brothers came in on their next line, it stopped. The next
night it sang through their rest again, and the night after, and by the end of that winter it no longer stopped when
they came in. It sang along with them. My mother said it was learning.

In the spring my mother came back from the house with the washing, white in the face, and said the brothers had turned
their altar round. They had got it up off its seat with bars and turned it to face the back wall, the wall toward the
crypts, so that the brothers in their stalls were singing to the back of it. She had asked the eldest why. He said they
were singing to whoever was answering.

The roof came down on a still night the next winter, in the middle of their singing. I heard the singing, then the roof,
a long noise and then a short one, and then the singing again, only it was no longer theirs, and it went on until it was
light. We did not go down. When the knights came in the spring there were four brothers left to meet them, and those
went up the mountain with the knights and did not come back.

The brothers' tune still comes up out of the ground on some nights. There are no brothers.

### A knight's report from the low road

*Written at the fallen house by a knight of the Pale Order to his Warden, the spring after it fell. A copy was left
pinned inside the door for the knights who would come after him, and a pilgrim who sheltered there copied that.*

Warden,

I came down the low road at the thaw with twelve of our dead and the chests, and found the house fallen. The vault of
the great room has come down along a line from the east wall to the font. The south half of the room lies under the fall
and open to the sky; the north half stands. It is raining into the font as I write.

The house held nineteen brothers. I have counted four living, whom I will bring up the mountain with the season's bones.
I have counted nine under the fall and laid them in the sleeping-niches until they can be carried, which is not yet. Six
I cannot find. They are not in the house, and they are not under the fall, unless the fall is deeper than its stone.

The four living say the altar was turned in the winter by the brothers' own hands, to face the east wall. They say it
was turned the night of the fall, by which they mean the Falling, as the brothers here have always said that this house
stood when she fell. I do not argue it. I note that the seat of the altar shows the marks of bars, and the marks are
fresh.

The tallier's count on the wall stops at fifty-four of a hundred and eight. His last mark is cut half its length.

The floor of the north half lifts under the foot a little, and settles, as the floor of the crypt above does. It did not
when I lodged here as a novice.

I have set eleven of our dead to keep the house until I am answered: four at the door, four at the stair to the crypts,
three at the altar. They will stand where they are told. I ask whether the house is to be cut again or closed, and
whether I am to carry the altar home, which is white stone of the Ossa and the Order's, or leave it facing where it
faces.

## For building it

**The land.**
- **Under the ground at the Moor's foot,** below the crypts. Draw it from rock-hewn houses of prayer: the churches of
  Lalibela, cut down into the rock from above and standing in trenches; Vardzia, whose cliff face fell away in an
  earthquake and left its rooms open to the sky; the Kyiv Monastery of the Caves, its brothers laid in niches along
  narrow passages.
- **The rock:** a soft, pale tuff (chapter 2), cut smooth and plain; the Order's own things are white stone and bone.
- **The house by cause:** the court, a trench cut down from the moor above, its walls fringed with fern; the nave, cut
  into the trench wall, its choir and stalls under the vault that held; the sleeping-niches, cut like the niches of the
  laid but a hand shorter; the bone-room, where the season's chests waited; the stair up toward the crypts; the passage
  toward the wet country, where bog water seeps in and the floor turns to peat and reed.
- **True scale:** the nave 22 yards long and 9 wide, its vault 8 yards up; the court trench 6 yards deep; the altar 2
  yards long and a yard high.
- **The floor is never bare:** the fall's slabs, large to small, with silt, puddles, moss, liverwort and fern wherever
  the rain reaches; on the dry side, lamp soot, shards and dust; splintered stalls; song-book leaves gone to pulp;
  knotted cords; heather and bracken blown into the court's corners; reed and peat creeping in from the bog passage.

**Landmarks.**
- **The half-fallen nave** (the zone's signature):
  - *Shape:* one long cut room, its vault whole on the north side and gone on the south, the break running from the east
    wall to the font. From the game's camera, a room split into a black half and a grey half.
  - *Story by cause:* the slabs fell inward in a ramp across the south pews, crushing the front ones; the font stands
    under the sky, full of rainwater and silt; south of the break the tuff is bleached, wet and green, and north of it
    dry and black with centuries of lamp soot.
  - *Material:* pale tuff; soot; moss and fern on the wet side only.
  - *Light:* the grey sky through the break, a hard-edged pool across the south half. The north half is black.
  - *Age:* a lifetime since the fall: fern rooted in the slab joints, silt in the font.
- **The turned altar:**
  - *Shape:* a long block of white stone on a low seat, its carved front to the east wall at arm's length and its plain
    back to the pews, so the carving can be seen only by squeezing in behind it.
  - *Story by cause:* two arcs scored in the seat where its corners ground round, and bar-marks under its lip.
  - *Material:* white stone of the Ossa, polished by hands and stained amber with chrism; along its back edge, a ridge
    that no mason cut.
  - *Light:* between the altar's front and the wall a slow lamp of chrism still burns, kept by the dead, so the altar
    stands black against a warm glow on the wall.
- **The stalls:** two rows facing across the choir, dark wood worn pale on the seats, splintered on the fall side.
- **The tallier's wall:** tallies cut in groups of a hundred and eight, as on the Counting Wall in the crypt above (the
  brothers took that way of counting from it), stopping at fifty-four, the last mark half its length.

**Light and weather.** The lantern is the key light under the rock; the sky lights the open half, grey by day and
moonlit by night; the chrism lamp glows behind the altar; the game's three lanterns stand in the court, at the nave door
and in the bone-room. Rain falls straight into the open half, darkens the slabs, runs down the fall in sheets, rings in
the font and drips in a fringe from the broken vault, while the north half stays dry. A damp breath comes up the bog
passage. No fog, no red light.

**The god showing through: more than a hint.** The floor of the north half lifts and settles a finger's width on the
breath, as the crypt's does. A crack runs along the line of the fall from the east wall toward the font, and warm air
breathes out of it. The altar faces the way the crack runs.

**Creatures** (the game's mix):
- **Husks (55):** travellers of the low road who sheltered in the ruin and stayed. The court trench, the nave door and
  the gaps in the fall are the doorways.
- **Choristers (25):** the singing in the stalls, a dirge that drags at the feet; fewest under the open sky.
- **Gravebloats (14) and Gasps (14):** the Gasps come through the rock as if it were mist, and shy from the light.
- **Ossuary Wardens (11):** the eleven dead the last knight set to keep the house, at the door, the stair to the crypts
  and the altar. They block from the front behind shields of skulls; go round them where the room is wide.
- **Weepers (8):** behind the stalls and the fallen slabs.
- **Kneelers (7):** penitents of the low road on their knees in the nave, facing the altar's back.
- **Objects:** two waystones, three lanterns, four chests in the bone-room, one altar (the turned one). The fallen slabs
  stop shots; the dry stalls burn and the wet ones smoulder; the font and puddles douse fire.

**Sound.** Rain into the nave and ringing in the font; the drip fringe at the broken vault; the Choristers' dirge in the
stalls, nine lines and a rest, and in the rest something lower going on; the floor's slow lift; the Wardens' tread.

**Keep out.**
- The Gilded Peak: no gold leaf, black glass, gilded figures, begging bowls or black flame.
- Anything Tibetan; any mask; anything that glows of itself; iron as a theme.
- A refectory.
- The old whisper about chanting and the wind (a banned form).
- Fog; red light.
