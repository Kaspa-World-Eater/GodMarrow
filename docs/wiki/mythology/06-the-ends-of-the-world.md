# The ends of the world

Every people foretells an end. Each is one people's view of the next turning of the wheel, and all of them have
happened before (`00-the-deep-lore.md`). None of them mentions the pilgrim. Every one of them is read in the cards.

## The Pale Order: Beyond the Pale

The Pale is named for its fence. When the first brothers came up onto the ridges to lay the Mother's bones, they drove a
pale round the first bone-house: thigh-bones set upright in the ground, end to end, each with its number cut in it.
Inside the pale, everything was counted. Outside it, nothing was. Uncounted, the brothers teach, a thing is not yet
anything; it is only waiting. Beyond the pale is where the waiting things are.

Every year, at the counting of the walls, the pale is moved out by the length of one bone. Since the first morning it
has gone out across the ridges and down the valleys and out over the lowlands, a line of numbered thigh-bones walking
across the world one bone a year, and everything it passes is counted, and is something at last. The brothers call the
country inside it the Pale, and the rest of the world is beyond it.

**The Setting Down.** One year the pale will meet itself. It will have gone all the way round, and closed, and there
will be nothing beyond it any more: everything counted, every bone laid, the Count closed at the Last Number. Then the
Mother will set the world down. Everything finished will stay with her: the laid bone, the closed counts, the dust.
Everything unfinished will be given to the wind: the flesh still growing, the breath still leaving, every name. The sky,
which is the Order's dust, will come down at last and lie over her like a cloth over a face, and she will rest. The
brothers use the word *rest* of nothing but dust and her.

**The heresy of the inward pale.** A few brothers, who are walled up when they are found, read the same count the other
way. The pale is not going out, they say. It is coming in. Every year the Count comes out higher than the year's dead,
so the Count is counting something that is not dying; and what is counted is laid; and what is laid is inside. One day
the pale will close round the last living thing on the Hide, standing alone in a ring of numbered bones, and a brother
will count it, and the Count will close on that. Then everything will be beyond the pale except the Mother, and the
Mother will be alone.

**The cards.** The brothers lay the cards at the counting of the walls, and one card is laid every year by law, face
down, on the last bone of the pale, and not turned. More cards lie face down along the pale now than any brother has
counted, one a bone's length apart, which in the Pale Order is a kind of blasphemy. The Order holds that when the pale closes, the cards will turn over by themselves, all at once, and spell
the Last Number.

At the end of every counting the brothers stand and name every other end of the world any faith has ever told, one by
one, and condemn each of them aloud. It takes until dark.

*Behind the myth.* The pun is meant, and the world never makes it. The Count rises every year by more than the year's
dead, and nobody in the Order will say what else it is counting.

## The Gilded Peak: the Pause Completed

The monks of the Peak taught that the world is a held breath, and that the Silence is the pause it is held in. A held
breath cannot be held forever. Their end is the letting go.

Their whole teaching was the hourglass of two sands: a glass with two sands in it, one bright and one dark, running
against each other through the one waist, so that whichever way the glass is turned, both are always falling. The bright
sand is **Radiance**, the open hand: the light that shows everything, and in showing it, takes it, because a thing seen
completely has nothing left to keep. The dark sand is **Absence**, the closed hand: what is let go, the dark that keeps
nothing and so loses nothing. Every monk carried a small glass at the belt and turned it at every bell, and the life of
the Peak was the turning.

**The Dissolution.** The end begins, the monks taught, when the two sands stop running against each other and run the
same way. Radiance will come first. The light will grow even, as it was in the Held Space, coming from nowhere and
falling on everything alike, until nothing on the Hide casts a shadow. Without shadows nothing can be told from
anything else: the edge goes out of every stone, the face goes out of every face, the line goes out of every letter,
and things will stand in the light quite still, and quite plain, and nobody will be able to say where one stops. Then
Absence: the light will close like a hand, all at once, not into darkness, which is still something, but into the
nothing that the dark is cut out of. The two sands will lie in the bottom of the glass together, one grey sand, and
nobody will turn it. That is the Pause Completed. Nothing will have been destroyed. The sentence will have been
finished, and a finished sentence has been said, and does not need saying again.

**The gilded saints.** The Peak's holiest dead were sealed in gold leaf, layer on layer, so that nothing of them could
be seen, only the shape. A thing looked at and never seen, the monks taught, is neither told nor untold, and cannot end;
the saints are waiting in the gold for the Dissolution, so that they can be seen once, completely, and go. When the
Peak fell, the gold on the saints in the fallen sanctum began to flake, a little each year, from the inside.

**The Empty Hand.** The monks who were below the mountain when it fell keep the last office for those who were above.
When the end comes, they will be the empty space outside it, the hand that holds nothing so that nothing is held
wrongly, and they will turn the last glass.

**The cards.** The Peak laid the cards blind: the reader never looked at a card, only at the shadow it threw on the
cloth by the one lamp, and read the shadow. In the Peak's last winter, the monks' letters say, the cards had begun to
throw no shadow at all.

## The Veiled Crone: the Unweaving

The circles teach that the world is a cloth, and the Crone is weaving it, and that she is blind. She weaves behind her
veil, by feel, a thread for every life, warp and weft, and she has never once seen what she has made. The grey over the
Hide is her veil, drawn across the whole sky; the two moons behind it are her eyes, one open and one shut, and when the
dark moon crosses the pale one she is blinking.

**The lifting of the veil.** One night she will lift it. The circles do not agree why. Some say she will finish the
cloth and want to see it; some say a thread will cut her finger; some say the Silence, which got in once already, will
get in under the veil and lift it from inside. However it begins, the grey will draw back off the sky for the first time
since the first morning, and whoever is left on the Hide will stand out of doors and see the sky bare: the sun for the
hole it is, and the two moons for what they are, and the Crone looking down through all three.

And she will see what she has woven, and it will be wrong. It has been wrong since the old one died, the circles say,
because the old one was the loom and she has been weaving without one ever since, and the cloth has puckered and run and
gone to knots, and every war and plague and lost child is a knot in it. She will see it all at once. And she will begin
to unpick.

**The Unweaving.** She will take out the newest threads first, because they are on top. The newborn will forget their
names, and then their mothers, and then that they were born. Then the threads under those: the young, the old, the
dead in the barrows, each life pulled loose from the cloth and drawn out through the eye of the needle the way it went
in, and every place a thread is pulled, the cloth around it will gape. Rivers will lose their beds, because a river is
a thread too. Roads will forget where they went. Faces will come loose from the front of heads. The grey will come down
in rags. At the last she will pull the oldest thread, the first one, the one the whole cloth hangs from, and the cloth
will fall off the loom into her lap, a heap of loose thread in the dark, and nothing will be anything.

**The two circles.** The circles of **the Shroud** keep the veil on her. They veil their own faces from birth, cover every mirror and
every still water at a death, and sing at midwinter to keep her weaving, never looking; a cloth with knots in it, they
say, is still a cloth. The circles of **the Mirror** want her to see. They go bare-faced and keep mirrors uncovered on purpose, and
they say an unpicked cloth is only thread, and thread can be woven again, and better; that the Unweaving is the only
way anything here will ever be made right. At midwinter both circles keep the same rite, for opposite reasons: the
year's cloth, woven through the year a row for every death, is unpicked thread by thread on the longest night, and
woven again before dawn. The Shroud does it to show her she need not. The Mirror does it to practise.

**The cards.** The circles read with a deck they never shuffle; they cut it once, with a thread, at the place the
thread falls. In the circles' oldest decks the last card is a blank one, worn soft, called the Loose Thread. It is
said to turn up face up on the night the veil lifts, in every deck on the Hide at once.

## The Bleeding Maiden: the Filling

The flesh will fill every hollow: every wound and every mouth, every grave and every room, and the gap between one word
and the next. When nothing is hollow anywhere, there will be no *then*. That will be the Mother made whole.

The processions keep the name of every knight of the Pale Order in their litanies, so that she will know whom to take
first. The few lowland children, who know nothing of the litanies, end their counting game with the rhyme's last verse:

> *Who will open the Tall Red Hat?*
> *Mother will.*

*Behind the myth.* The gap between one word and the next is where the Silence is. The Filling is the one end that would
close the Silence too, and the processions say it carefully.

## The Myriad: the Breath Drawn In

The keepers teach that the world is the dead one's out-breath, and that every breath that goes out comes back in.

The one wind is still going out across the Hide, thick with the kami of everything the great one ever thought, and
of everything everyone since has said aloud. They quarrel in it. That is the only thing keeping the world as it is:
**discord**, belief against belief, kami against kami, each small one holding every other small one down. The keepers tend
the quarrel. They whisper contradictions into their shrines so that the caught kami never settle. They breathe a lie
into a room where a truth has been told too often. They set one house's grudge loose in the next house's well. They
are hated for it, and they would say they are hated by people who do not understand what they are holding back.

**The Concord.** But the miasma is thickening. Every year the breath is fouler, and the kami in it are sicker and
quarrel less, and here and there, the keepers have seen it, they have begun to agree. Two grudges find they are the same
grudge and run together. A whole valley's fear of the dark becomes one dread, and wakes. The keepers say the end
comes when the agreeing spreads faster than they can sow quarrel: when, on one still day, every kami in the breath
believes the same thing at the same moment, and they run together, all of them, into one god as large as the dead one.
It will not be a new god. It will be the old one, made again out of its own last thoughts, and it will do what a thing
that has breathed out does next. **It will breathe in.**

The wind will turn, for the first time since the first morning. Everything that went out on it will come back: the ash,
the voices, the thoughts, the kami, and every breath anyone has ever let go of, drawn back across the Hide toward
wherever the great one lies. Everyone breathing will have their breath taken with it, out of their mouths, all at once,
in one long pull. The world will be inhaled, and held.

**The held breath.** The keepers alone mean to live through it, and they say how: by holding. Every keeper learns to hold
a breath longer than anyone living, a night, then a season of nights, and the eldest say they have not truly let one go
in years. On the day the wind turns, they will hold, with everything they have caught still in their lungs, while the
world is drawn in around them. And when the great one has held its breath as long as it can, it will have to let go
again, and the keepers will be the only things left with breath of their own, and the kami they carry will be the
only ones left in the world. That, the eldest say, is what the shrines were always for.

**The cards.** The keepers read the cards by breathing on them: each card is held to the lips, breathed on once, and laid
down, and they read which ones the breath fogs and which stay clear. In this age more of the cards stay clear.

The bells of the wet country have been heard to ring a new way in this age, all in tune. The keepers say they ring *in
is closer than it was*.

## The Second Mouth: the Swallowing

The cult that calls itself the Second Mouth teaches that the old one had two. The first mouth breathed. It breathed in once,
at the beginning, and everything that is was in that breath, and since the old one died the first mouth has been breathing
out: the long exhale, the one wind, the world going out of the old one a little more every year. The second mouth eats.

The cult is the second mouth's teeth. They do not pray; they eat. At their tables, at night, they eat the flesh of the
world: the warm earth of Shog-Mire, the meat of the things that come up, the red of the wet places, and, at the great
feasts, the dead of their own, so that nothing of the faithful is ever lost to the outside. What is eaten is carried.
What is carried is kept. The processions of the Bleeding Maiden, who give their blood back to the world a cup at a time,
burn the cult wherever they find it, and the cult forgives them, because the processions are only the first mouth's
people, giving things away.

**The Swallowing.** When enough of the faithful carry enough of the world inside them, the cult says, the second mouth
will open. Every member of the Second Mouth, in every place, will wake at the same moment in the same night, and get up
without speaking, and walk: out of their doors and down the roads, all toward one place, a hollow in Shog-Mire that is a
little wider every year. They will walk into it singing with their mouths full, and the hollow will close over them,
and the world they have eaten will go with them, down, into the only belly left that is not empty. What is left behind
will be the rind: the bone and the dust and the wind, an empty Hide with nobody hiding on it. The processions call that
the end of the world. The cult calls it grace, and says it before every meal: *we are the end of the meal*.

**The cards.** The cult does not read the cards. It eats them, one a feast, chewed and swallowed by the eldest at the
table, and holds that whatever card is eaten has been taken out of the world's future. They have been eating the deck
for longer than the processions have been burning them. They say there are fewer cards in the world than there were, and that the ones left are the
ones nobody could keep down.

Deep in Shog-Mire it is whispered that something in the red water is learning the words.

## The Tithed: the Reckoning

The Tithed who are left have no great end of their own, and the faiths' ends are not asked of them. What they have is
the debt (`02-the-creation-myths.md`).

They have been paying it since the first morning: the tenth row left standing, the tenth loaf on the sill, the tenth
night kept awake, the tenth word unsaid. Nobody knows to whom. And the grandmothers say a debt paid by the tenth is
never paid. It is only kept from coming due. One day it will come due.

**The collector.** On that day, they say, whoever is owed will come to collect the rest. Not the tenth. The other nine.
It will come at night, on foot, down the roads, from wherever the morning came from, and it will knock, and it will be
very polite. It will not be angry. It will only have come for what was always its. And the people will lie down where
they stand, as they were lying on the first morning, cold, on the ash, with nothing in their mouths, and the collector
will go on to the next door. And in the morning, the grandmothers say, the morning will come for someone else.

The grandmothers will not say what the collector looks like. The children say it carries a lantern. The old men say
it carries a deck of cards, and that it has been seen already, here and there, sitting by a road, laying them out for
nobody.

**The signs, as the last hearths tell them:** the tenth rows left standing in the fields have begun to lean, at night,
all one way, as if something were walking past them; the tenth loaf is taken earlier in the night than it used to be;
and on the nights kept awake, more of the few watchers say they heard footsteps on the road that did not stop at any
door. Yet.

## The shore folk: When the Grandmother Cuts Her Hair

The shore folk never call it water. It is **the Red**, and it is not the sea's colour but the sea's name, and it is
alive.

At the bottom of the Red, they say, far out and far down, an old woman sits with her back to the shore, and the Red is
her hair. In the beginning, when the Fallen One fell and the land lay down, she knelt at its side and wrung her hair out
over it, and what ran out of her hair was the Red, and she has been wringing it out ever since. She holds the end of her
braid in her fist, and when she draws it in, the Red goes out and bares the black sand and the benches of rock and the
dead; and when she lets it out, the Red comes in. That is the tide. Twice a day she draws it in and lets it out, slowly,
because she is old, and because her hands are tired, and because the braid is heavy with everything the Red has taken.
The shore folk call her **the Grandmother of the Red**, and the keepers on the stair call her **She Who Holds the Line**.

They give her back what they owe her. At every dark of the moon the shore folk set small boats of bark on the Red at the
tide's turn, each with a stub of candle and a hair from the head of everyone in the house, and push them out, so that
she will know who is grateful and who is sorry; for every life on the shore is lived on what she lets the Red bring in,
and every death on the shore is given back to her. Whatever goes into the Red comes back, they say. She sends it back.
She is only lending.

**The cutting.** Every grandmother on the shore cut her hair when she was widowed, and laid it on the Red. So, the
keepers say, will the Grandmother of the Red, when the Fallen One finishes dying. She will take a knife to her braid
and cut it at the nape, and let it go.

Then the tide will stop. The Red will run out once and not come in again: down past the rocks and the stacks, farther
out than anyone has seen the sand go, and on, down the slope of the bottom of the world, until the black sand goes on
forever under the grey. Everything the Red ever held will be lying on the bottom of it, bared: every boat and every
body, the drowned city and its lights, and the shell. And everything the Red ever held will rise, and turn round, and
walk home up the dry bottom toward the shore, all of them, all night, toward the houses they were taken from.

**The holes.** And the keepers who have studied with the old men of the highest stair add the last of it, and the
others do not like it told. When the Red has gone out, the grey will thin over the bare bottom, and the sun will open
wider, because the sun is a hole, and then there will be another hole beside it, and another, until seven stand open in
the sky, one after the other, each a little brighter, and the light of whatever is on the other side will pour through
all of them. The black sand will run like wax and set as glass, and the forest will burn standing, and the walkers from
the Red will be glass where they stand, a whole people of red glass, on the shore, facing the houses. That, the old men
say, is why the oldest roads on the Hide are black glass in places. It has happened before.

**The cards.** The shore folk read the cards on the wet sand at low tide, laid face up in a line from the dry sand down
toward the Red, and read them by which ones the Red takes when it comes back in. They noticed, in their last years, that
the Red had begun to leave the cards alone.

## The signs

Whoever is left lays the cards, and in this age they have all been seeing the same things. These are the signs that any
of them can read, now:
- **The Pilgrim keeps turning up.** In every spread laid on the Hide these last years, whatever the deck and whoever the
  reader, the card called the Pilgrim has turned up, and nobody can remember when it last came up reversed.
- **New cards are found.** More cards turn up every year that nobody has seen before, in the coats of the dead and the
  bottoms of chests and the decks of people who swear they never had them. One was found with nothing on it but a
  gap the width of a thumb.
- **The Unwritten is drawn.** The blank card at the bottom of the old decks, which a reader may go a life without
  drawing, has been drawn three times in a year at one fire.
- **The Count rises.** The Pale Order's Count rises every year, by more than the year's dead.
- **The lanterns are fewer.** There are fewer lanterns on the roads every year.
- **The rings move.** The rings with teeth are coming nearer to each other, and someone is still cutting them.
- **The wind goes out** and never comes back.
- **The Red still beats,** but it has begun to leave the cards on the sand alone.
- **The dark moon** crosses the pale one more often than it did, and covers more of it each time.
- **The gold flakes.** The gold on the saints of the fallen Peak is flaking from the inside.
- **The stair is not swept.** The stair in the shore folk's temple has not been swept.
- **A pilgrim is walking** who does not know where it came from.

*Behind the myth.* Every end in this page has happened before, in some turn of the world, and every one will happen
again. At the end of this turn the pilgrim takes up the mantle of what it came to end, and becomes the next one
(`00-the-deep-lore.md`). Whichever end this turn's people see coming, it will look to them like their own prophecy, come
true. The cards have been showing it to everyone, and everyone has read it as their own.
