# The Age of the Last Breath

From the old one's death to now, longer than anyone remembers. The world is a corpse that has not finished dying. Its last
breath is still going out, and the few who are left live in that breath. Its bone still stands, its flesh still grows,
its threads still pull, and things still come up out of it. Knowledge is the rarest thing there is. What is left of the
faiths keeps the dark back with a soul in a jar for a lamp.

Nothing here is dated. The events stand in the order the tellings give them, and where the canon never fixed an order
it is set here so we share one shape; `01-the-ages.md` has the whole chronicle.

## The waking

**The first mourning.** The people woke on the ash of the place now called the Ashen Moor, scattered and cold, with no
names and nothing before (`02-the-creation-myths.md`). The light was grey and came from one side, and it has come that
way ever since, the same length every day to the width of a thread.

**The first night.** The things that come up out of the ground came to them on the first night, and stood round them
in the dark, and breathed. The people had nothing to give them, so they gave them a tenth of what little they had: a
tenth of the bread, laid on a flat stone at the edge of the firelight. In the morning the bread was gone and the people
were not. Ever since, the Tithed set the tenth loaf on the sill at night and hang a lantern at the cradle's head. What
the things that come up want, nobody knows. The grandmothers say they are only the people's own fears come back up
with teeth on them, and that a fear is fed like anything else.

**The first lantern.** In that first winter a soul chose to be kept rather than go down with the last breath, and
somebody found how to keep it, in glass, as a flame. That was the first lantern. A kept soul chooses whom it follows; it
carries them back to the lantern-stones when they fall, and keeps a little of them each time, and the little it keeps is
what it burns: a way of whistling, a sister's name, the second verse of a song. The makers of lanterns were always few,
and were later hunted out by the faiths, but every lantern on every road is theirs still. Every lantern carries the same
words: *Dust on the glass. Wipe it. It is someone.*

**The burning.** In the first years after the death, the heath below the cheek caught and burned season on season, and
nobody lit it. Some say hot ash came over the ridge on one long breath of the wind. The few fire-keepers left on the
heath say it came up from underneath, the heather only the wick. The Burnt Heath has never quite stopped burning since,
and every burn on it runs one way: the way the wind goes.

**The wind.** That wind is the old one's last breath going out. It blows one way across the whole Hide, out past the
Sighing Ridge toward the dark and out over the Red Water, and it has never once blown back. Ash that goes out on it
never comes back.

**The first Laying.** Somewhere in the pale ridges, men who would become the Pale Order began laying bones, each in
its place, each with a name or a number. They say they stood beside the Mother as she fell, and laid her bones where
they lay.

## The first years

**The spreading.** The Tithed spread out over the Hide in companies, walking close in the dark, each with a hand on
the shoulder of the one in front. They learned the rules of the
world the hard way:
- the dead do not stay down;
- a name said aloud can be called;
- a song sung at night on the open ash is answered.

So they kept their true names behind their teeth, and stopped singing at night, and buried their dead in rings of
barrows, nine to a ring and nine of a family to each, face down, and said no names over them. The barrow lids sank
inward, all pointing to a ring of standing teeth. The mothers they carried instead into the crypts dug between the
old one's ribs, because the crypts were warm, and the mothers had always been cold.

**The hunting.** The old powers that had outlived the Wasting still walked, and the new people were small and warm
and full of belief. Things waited at the edge of every lantern's light, because a lantern has
a soul in it. Whole companies went down roads and did not come to the end of them.

**The first cup.** At a great wound in the ground that did not close, people began to pour a measured cup of their own
blood once a year, and to say words over it as it poured. The processions of the Bleeding Maiden say their faith began
with the first cup filled. The wound asked a little more every year, and every year the line in the cup was raised a
thumbnail.

**The numbered stones.** People who found an old road already lying through the Hide set numbered stones along
it, the Pilgrim Road. The figures count down as you go out along it, and nobody has ever been able to say what they
count toward. The road was older than the people who numbered it, older than the faiths, hard and close-fitted with no
tool mark anywhere, and in places it is black glass.

**The pit fed again.** Some of the Tithed went down into the low wet country, and there they found the long dead thing
lying through it, its stone spine the only dry road, and the pit of black stone at its throat still glowing red from
somewhere below. They were afraid, as every people before them had been afraid, and a people that is afraid feeds what
it fears, even a corpse. They fed it as the old peoples had: once a year, at the pit, the blood of the chosen, down the
gutters into the dark. In return, they believed, the water kept its place and the road stayed above it.

**The refusal.** A keeper of that people had a daughter, and the daughter was chosen, and the
keeper would not give her. On the night of the giving she took the girl, and everyone who would follow her, and walked
out along the spine away from the pit and up out of the wet country, and did not stop until she was among the giant
trees above the Red Water. There her people built a house for the keepers on a platform, and up to its door a stair,
and she carved the serpent on the stair's rails, its body running down and its heads rearing at the foot, so that if
the thing in the bog was only sleeping, it would see itself already at their door and believe itself still kept, and
not come looking. She set the rule that the stair be swept every morning before anything else is done.

Down in the wet country nobody fed the pit after that, and nothing came up out of it, and the red down there glows on.
The bog folk who walk the spine forgot whose it is, and say their own people laid it as a road. Pilgrims made a saint
of it, and wore its name down like a step until only *Uss* is left.

## The long middle

**The lantern-makers hunted.** Every faith came to keep its dead its own way, and each
called its own craft holy and the others' theft. They hunted the lantern-makers out between them. Nobody has kept a new
soul in glass since, or nobody says so. The old lanterns are enough; they last, and they keep a little more of everyone,
and there are a few less of them on the roads each year.

**The dead come back up the black sand.** The shore folk in the trees above the Red Water gave
their dead to the Red for generations, laid in canoes with their faces to the sky and pushed out on the falling tide.
One winter the dead came back. They climbed out of the Red at night, red to the eyes, up the black sand, and stood at the
edge of the trees looking up at the village, and the next night there were more. From then on the shore folk burned
every dead body on the night it died, and gave the Red nothing: no dead, no blood, no word spoken over it. Whatever goes
into the Red comes back. They sent seven canoes out once to find the Red's far side; one came back at the end of a
summer with three still living in it, red to the shoulders, saying there was nothing out there but more of it, and that
the farther they went, the warmer it grew.

**The Gilded Peak.** A bell-caster struck a new bell to hear its note, and in the moment after
the note died he heard what was behind it. He put down his hammer, climbed a mountain of bone that some say is the old one's
knee, sat down at the top, and did not speak for years. The monastery of the Gilded Peak grew up around him, its
stair gilded on every seventh step. Its monks chanted one syllable and then a silence as long, *the syllable a fence and
the silence the field it marks*. They gilded what they honoured, so that it would be looked at and not seen. At dusk they
came down to the lowlands in fours with empty bowls and said at every door, *Put a name in it*, and people whispered
into the bowls the names of what they wanted gone, and forgot them. The forgetting had no edge.

**The circles of the Veiled Crone.** Readers of threads sat down in rings from dusk to the grey
hour and said the Crone's names, mouth to mouth round the ring, until they could not tell the names from themselves.
They learned the one law every circle keeps: a soul cannot pass its own reflection. A reader's son died young, and his
soul stopped in the yard at a pail of rainwater, looking into it, and stood there through the winter, until she covered
the pail with grey felt and a stone on each corner, and he went on. Since then, at every death, the circles cover every
mirror and every still water until the dead have gone through. The eldest of every circle wears the coat of glass, iron
sewn with hollowed mirrors by every eldest before, until it weighs what a second body weighs.

**The bone-houses of the Ossa.** The Pale Order filled the pale ridges with its bone-houses, nine great and a hundred
lesser, each with its stair going down, its walls of laid bone counted to the last knuckle, its warden called the
Ossuarch, an office and no one man. Its brothers drew amber marrow from the wells under the houses, and down the fifth
well something kneels that they call the Marrow Pontiff, which went down before the stair was cut and has never come
up. They taught that the stars are pieces of the Mother's bone not yet fallen. Wherever an Ossuarch was sent out to
gather bone, he set white stones in a line across the lowlands, and the line moved outward year by year; beyond it,
things were past keeping.

*Behind the myth.* The bone-houses were never cut by the Order. They are the upper floors of the Angles' halls
(`04-the-held-space.md`), and the Order hides what lies beneath.

**The bridge over the vein.** Beyond the Root Deep the Hide lies split open, and along the bottom of the rift runs a
clear, slow, thick water that the few who have seen it call a vein. Masons came to bridge it, a crew on each bank,
singing across the water to keep time as they built toward each other. One evening the far crew stopped singing in the
middle of a line, and was never heard from again, and the near crew built out as far as the stone would hold and
stopped. The Broken Bridge's two halves still reach for each other. The last name cut on the near parapet is half a
name.

**The Well-Shrine.** The Myriad's keepers set their first shrine at the Drowned Fen's one clean pool and began the
Cutting-Away: once a year every keeper breathed out into the Fen every stolen breath she could not use, and the Fen took
it, and the year went with it. It took it for generations.

**The cards, all along.** Through all of it, every people laid cards. The brothers laid them by number, the circles
read them like threads, the processions turned them at the cup, the shrine-keepers asked them which way the breath
would go, the shore folk drew them on the black sand, and the grandmothers dealt them by the banked fire. Every people
named the cards its own way, and every so often a card turned up that nobody had seen before. Nobody has ever known all
of them.

## The late dark

**The one cult.** Inside the processions, in the dark, a woman cut a second
mouth under her own breastbone, or had it cut, and said the Words into it backward. From her the Second Mouth spread:
a mouth cut under the breastbone at the first grade, three at the second, and at the third a body that stops growing
older. She has outlived her name, and sits folded nearly double in a chair she has not left in a lifetime, covered in
mouths, and the mouths have begun to answer one another.

**The rising count.** Every year at the counting of the walls, the Pale Order's count came out higher than the year
before, by more than the year's dead. The brothers called the difference the Mother's gift and wrote it on a second leaf
in a hand no counter ever saw at work.

**The grey year.** After generations of taking the world's wrong breath, the Fen gave it back. The
clean pool went grey from the bottom up in a single night, and the grey came up out of the water and lay over the reed
camps for days on end. Seven keepers went down the drowned stair under the pool to find what had turned it. One counted
thirty-three steps, one thirty-four, and the last one up counted thirty-six and would not say what was on the extra
steps. Six came up horned. The seventh carried up a girl nobody had sent down, and the girl was dry. There has been no
Cutting-Away since, only the Small Cutting, breath into paper boats burned on a dry bank. In the day-book under that
year someone wrote: *Where does the year go now? It goes into us.*

**The east wall.** A circle of the Veiled Crone sat down in its reading-room to say the names,
and something was standing by the east wall that had come in without the door opening. It showed nothing in the glass.
The candle's flame leaned toward it, and the ink went white at the edge of the page nearest it. In the morning the next
circle found the seven chairs in their ring with no bodies in them, no bones and no dust, and the floor along that
wall has stayed dry in every season since.

## The breaking

**The wound that hatched.** A chapter of the processions was built down both lips of a great
wound in the ground, as long as a morning's walk. In its last year the wound would not take the blood. It crusted over
from end to end, warm underfoot, and the hoods broke the crust with picks to pour the cup, and it closed over the
blood again before the cup was empty. In the night after the fast-day the crust split along its whole length. What came
out walked in the hoods' robes, which had hung on their pegs in the crypt: the shapes of children with nothing behind
their eyes, in the order of the procession. At the front walked something tall, sewn together out of more than one
thing, carrying the Maiden from the niche on its back, and the Maiden had a face now. The Rule in the crypt gained new
clauses in a hand that leans the other way. The last of them says *She hears.* The procession went out, and has never
come home.

**The grave too many.** At the Hollow Wood's edge, every door stood open one morning, the beds
still holding the shapes of the sleepers, and there was nothing left of anyone: no bone, no tooth, no smell. The one man
left dug a grave for every hearth and laid a shawl or a pair of boots in each, and dug one more than he had names for.
Something that leaves nothing had passed.

**The fall of the Gilded Peak.** In the spring the monks began to die in their rooms at night, one or two a night, and
in the morning there was nothing in the room but a little black glass and a little dust. Night after night the abbot
counted them, and at last he sent the youngest down the mountain with a letter and told him not to look back. The next
night the great bell over the courtyard rang, long, though it had never rung where it hung. In the morning the courtyard
was black glass from wall to wall, warm to the hand, and it is warm still. The sanctum's doors stood open. The gilded
saints were in their niches, a hundred and seven of them, and the last niche on the left was empty as it had always been
kept, except that the dust before it held footprints going out. Over the door someone had cut in the night *THEY WERE
NOT WEEPING FOR US*, or, as others read it, *UNDER THE GOLD THEY WERE STILL LOOKING*. Since then the few monks who were
below the mountain walk the roads carrying nothing, and are called the Empty Hands, and what they strike leaves glass,
dust, or nothing.

**The loose winter.** The dead did not go through. The mirrors were covered and the felt laid right, and still,
night after night, the dead came back up the valleys to the doors of the houses they had died in, and asked whoever
opened the door the same thing, quietly: *Which way?* An eldest of the circles stood up in the coat of glass, which she
had not done in years, and said, *There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes*, and sat down, and died,
and did not go through either. After that some circles took the felt off and let the dead stop at their own faces in
the glass, and kept them there, and carried them. The circles split: the Shroud, who send the dead on faster and
fiercer than ever and burn uncovered glass, and the Mirror, who keep the dead in glass and walk the roads with them
circling like small cold lights. The coat of glass stood up on its own, full of the kept, and has not fallen down.

**The siege at the Red Water.** In the wet season after the loose winter the dead began to come up out of the Red Water
again at the shore folk's village. They were not the shore folk's dead. They came from somewhere far along the water,
wearing pilgrims' cloaks and carrying staffs, and stood at the edge of the trees looking up at the fire. Then one
night they did not go back down. After them came the things that were never alive in the way people are: things that
crawl up out of the surf on their fingers, things with no faces, things that come up through the sand. Winter after
winter the shore folk held their wall:
- they split cedar for it, and pulled down their own huts and canoes to mend it;
- each fighter danced the slow dance a dead teacher had taught them before going up on the wall, so that if they fell,
  the teacher would know them and come for them before the Red could;
- they played the drum and the pipe all night, so that the fighters would not hear the dead calling up to them by name;
- they burned their dead every night, and in time never let the fire go out.

In the end the wall broke at its wet end, where the posts had gone soft, and the last eleven died on their temple's
platform, at the top of the swept stair.

## Now

The fire in the ring of huts above the black sand still burns the dead, and nobody is seen to feed it. A merchant picks
the village clean, and laughs at everything a stranger does not know. In the temple a candle burns that no one living
could have lit. The stair has not been swept.

Across the Hide the lanterns are fewer every year, and so are the living. The Count rises. The rings with teeth are said
to be coming nearer to each other, and someone is still cutting them. The wind goes out and never comes back.

And under the black cliffs, in a pool of blood, something is rising that does not know where it came from: out of the
Red, out of a ring, out of a dream, or out of nothing. On the top step above it, a stranger is shuffling the cards
(`00-the-deep-lore.md`).
