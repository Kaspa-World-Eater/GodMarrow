# The Age of the Last Breath

From the god's death to now: eleven hundred and fourteen years of the common count. The world is a corpse that has
not finished dying. Its last breath is still going out, and people live in that breath. Its bone still stands, its
flesh still grows, its threads still pull, and things still come up out of it. Knowledge is the rarest thing there is.
The faiths keep the dark back with a soul in a jar for a lamp.

The dates are the common count. Those marked *(proposed)* were never fixed by the canon and are set here so we share
one shape; `01-the-ages.md` has the whole chronicle.

## The first year

**The coming out.** The congregation came out of the thing's mouth onto the ash of its cheek, the place now called the
Ashen Moor, and kissed the ash because it was ground. The light was grey and came from one side, and it has come that
way ever since, the same length every day to the width of a thread.

**The first night.** The things that come up out of the god came to them on the first night, and stood round them in
the dark, and breathed. The people had nothing but the salt they had carried out. They laid it in a line across the
mouth of their shelter and in a ring round the first child born in the open, and the things stood outside the line all
night and did not cross it. Ever since, the Tithed put salt on every sill and round every cradle, and hang a lantern at
the cradle's head. The things that come up are made of the god. The people are the only things that are not, and the
things want them back: some to take inside, some to put on like a coat and kneel and be them. They do not hate the
people. They are homesick.

**The first lantern.** In that first winter a soul chose to be kept rather than go down with the last breath, and
somebody found how to keep it, in glass, as a flame. That was the first lantern. A kept soul chooses whom it follows; it
carries them back to the lantern-stones when they fall, and keeps a little of them each time, and the little it keeps
is what it burns: a way of whistling, a sister's name, the second verse of a song. The makers of lanterns were a guild,
later hunted out by the faiths, but every lantern on every road is theirs still. Every lantern carries the same words:
*Dust on the glass. Wipe it. It is someone.*

**The burning.** In the first year after the death, the heath below the cheek caught and burned for a year, and
nobody lit it. The carters say hot ash came over the ridge on one long breath of the wind. The heath's fire-keepers say
it came up from underneath, the heather only the wick. The Burnt Heath has never quite stopped burning since, and every
burn on it runs one way: the way the wind goes.

**The wind.** That wind is the god's last breath going out. It blows one way across the whole Hide, out past the
Sighing Ridge toward the dark and out over the Red Water, and it has never once blown back. Ash that goes out on it
never comes back.

**The first Laying.** Somewhere in the white ridges, men who would become the Pale Order began laying bones, each in
its place, each with a name or a number. They say they stood beside the Mother as she fell, and laid her bones where
they lay. The common count is theirs, and it starts here.

## The first centuries (years 1 to about 300)

**The spreading.** The Tithed spread out over the Hide in companies, holding each other's belts in the dark, and
knotting their belts at the back so that whoever walked behind them had something to hold. They learned the rules of the
world the hard way:
- the dead do not stay down;
- a name said aloud can be called;
- a song sung at night on the open ash is answered.

So they kept their true names behind their teeth, and stopped singing at night, and buried their dead in rings of
barrows, nine to a ring and nine of a family to each, face down, and said no names over them. The barrow lids sank
inward, all pointing to a ring of standing teeth. The mothers they carried instead into the crypts dug between the
god's ribs, because the mothers had carried the children out, and wanted to go back in.

**The hunting.** The old powers that had outlived the Starving still walked, and the new people were small and warm
and full of belief. Things waited at the edge of every lantern's light. The mouths at the wick came to the lanterns,
because a lantern has a soul in it. Whole companies went down roads and did not come to the end of them.

**The first cup, about 80.** At a great wound in the ground that did not close, people began to pour a measured cup of
their own blood once a year, and to say words over it as it poured. The processions of the Bleeding Maiden count their
years from the first cup filled. The wound asked a little more every year, and every year the line in the cup was raised
a thumbnail.

**The numbered stones, 206.** People who found an old road already lying through the Hide set numbered stones along
it, the Pilgrim Road. The figures count down as you go out along it, and nobody has ever been able to say what they
count toward. The road was older than the people who numbered it, older than the faiths, hard and close-fitted with no
tool mark anywhere, and in places it is black glass.

**The serpent fed again.** Some of the Tithed went down into the low wet country, and there they found the serpent,
long past its age and still alive, its back above the black water and the pit of black stone at its coil. They were
afraid, as every people before them had been afraid, and a people that is afraid feeds what it fears. They fed it as
the old peoples had: once a year, at the pit, the blood of the chosen, down the gutters into the dark. In return the
water kept its place and the road on its back stayed above it.

**The refusal, about 300** *(proposed)*. A keeper of that people had a daughter, and the daughter was chosen, and the
keeper would not give her. On the night of the giving she took the girl, and everyone who would follow her, and walked
out along the serpent's back away from the pit and up out of the wet country, and did not stop until she was among the
giant trees above the Red Water. There her people built a house for the keepers on a platform, and up to its door a
stair, and she carved the serpent on the stair's rails, its body running down and its heads rearing at the foot, so
that it would see itself already at their door and believe itself still kept, and not come looking. She set the rule
that the stair be swept every morning before anything else is done.

Down in the wet country, with nobody left who would feed it, the serpent starved. A starved god breaks, and what it
breaks into is worse than what it was. Its back is the road through the Sunken Bog now, mostly overgrown, its vertebrae
breaking through the moss. The bog folk who walk it forgot whose back it is, and say their own people laid it as a road.
Pilgrims made a saint of it, and worn its name down like a step until only *Uss* is left.

## The middle centuries (about 300 to 900)

**The lantern-makers hunted** *(proposed, about 300 to 400)*. Every faith came to keep its dead its own way, and each
called its own craft holy and the others' theft. They hunted the lantern-makers out between them. Nobody has kept a new
soul in glass since, or nobody says so. The old lanterns are enough; they last, and they keep a little more of everyone,
and there are a few less of them on the roads each year.

**The dead come back up the black sand** *(proposed, about 450)*. The shore folk in the trees above the Red Water gave
their dead to the Red for generations, laid in canoes with their faces to the sky and pushed out on the falling tide.
One winter the dead came back. They climbed out of the Red at night, red to the eyes, up the black sand, and stood at the
edge of the trees looking up at the village, and the next night there were more. From then on the shore folk burned
every dead body on the night it died, and gave the Red nothing: no dead, no blood, no word spoken over it. Whatever goes
into the Red comes back. They sent seven canoes out once to find the Red's far side; one came back at the end of a
summer with three still living in it, red to the shoulders, saying there was nothing out there but more of it, and that
the farther they went, the warmer it grew.

**The Gilded Peak** *(proposed, about 550)*. A bell-caster struck a new bell to hear its note, and in the moment after
the note died he heard what was behind it. He put down his hammer, climbed a mountain of bone that some say is the god's
knee, sat down at the top, and did not speak for nine years. The monastery of the Gilded Peak grew up around him, its
stair gilded on every seventh step. Its monks chanted one syllable and then a silence as long, *the syllable a fence and
the silence the field it marks*. They gilded what they honoured, so that it would be looked at and not seen. At dusk they
came down to the lowlands in fours with empty bowls and said at every door, *Put a name in it*, and people whispered
into the bowls the names of what they wanted gone, and forgot them. The forgetting had no edge.

**The circles of the Veiled Crone** *(proposed, about 650)*. Readers of threads sat down in rings from dusk to the grey
hour and said the Crone's names, mouth to mouth round the ring, until they could not tell the names from themselves.
They learned the one law every circle keeps: a soul cannot pass its own reflection. A reader's son died young, and his
soul stopped in the yard at a pail of rainwater, looking into it, and stood there through the winter, until she covered
the pail with grey felt and a stone on each corner, and he went on. Since then, at every death, the circles cover every
mirror and every still water until the dead have gone through. The eldest of every circle wears the coat of glass, iron
sewn with hollowed mirrors by every eldest before, until it weighs what a second body weighs.

**The bone-houses of the Ossa.** The Pale Order filled the white ridges with its bone-houses, nine great and a hundred
lesser, each with its stair going down, its walls of laid bone counted to the last knuckle, its warden called the
Ossuarch, an office and no one man. Its brothers drew amber marrow from the wells under the houses, and down the fifth
well something kneels that they call the Marrow Pontiff, which went down before the stair was cut and has never come
up. They taught that the stars are pieces of the Mother's bone not yet fallen. Wherever an Ossuarch was sent out to
gather bone, he set white stones in a line across the lowlands, and the line moved outward year by year; beyond it,
things were past keeping.

*Behind the myth.* The bone-houses were never cut by the Order. They are the upper floors of the builders' halls
(`04-the-held-space.md`), and the Order hides what lies beneath.

**The bridge over the vein** *(proposed, about 750)*. Beyond the Root Deep the Hide lies split open, and along the
bottom of the rift runs a clear, slow, thick water that every boatman calls a vein. Masons came to bridge it, a crew on
each bank, singing across the water to keep time as they built toward each other. One evening the far crew stopped
singing in the middle of a line, and was never heard from again, and the near crew built out as far as the stone would
hold and stopped. The Broken Bridge's two halves still reach for each other. The last name cut on the near parapet is
half a name.

**The Well-Shrine, about 867.** The Myriad's purifiers set their first shrine at the Drowned Fen's one clean pool and
began the Cutting-Away: once a year every purifier breathed the year's wrong breath into the Fen, and the Fen took it,
and the year went with it. It took it for two hundred years.

**The cards, all along.** Through all of it, every people laid cards. The brothers laid them by number, the circles
read them like threads, the processions turned them at the cup, the shrine-keepers asked them which way the breath
would go, the shore folk drew them on the black sand, and the grandmothers dealt them by the banked fire. Every people
named the cards its own way, and every so often a card turned up that nobody had seen before. Nobody has ever known all
of them.

## The late centuries (about 900 to 1090)

**The one cult** *(proposed, some centuries before now)*. Inside the processions, in the dark, a woman cut a second
mouth under her own breastbone, or had it cut, and said the Words into it backward. From her the Second Mouth spread:
a mouth cut under the breastbone at the first grade, three at the second, and at the third a body that stops growing
older. She has outlived her name, and sits folded nearly double in a chair she has not left in a lifetime, covered in
mouths, and the mouths have begun to answer one another.

**The rising count.** Every year at the counting of the walls, the Pale Order's count came out higher than the year
before, by more than the year's dead. The brothers called the difference the Mother's gift and wrote it on a second leaf
in a hand no counter ever saw at work.

**The grey year, about 1071.** After two hundred years of taking the world's wrong breath, the Fen gave it back. The
clean pool went grey from the bottom up in a single night, and the grey came up out of the water and lay over the reed
camps for a month. Seven purifiers went down the drowned stair under the pool to find what had turned it. One counted
thirty-three steps, one thirty-four, and the last one up counted thirty-six and would not say what was on the extra
steps. Six came up horned. The seventh carried up a girl nobody had sent down, and the girl was dry. There has been no
Cutting-Away since, only the Small Cutting, breath into paper boats burned on a dry bank. In the day-book under that
year someone wrote: *Where does the year go now? It goes into us.*

**The east wall** *(proposed, about 1080)*. A circle of the Veiled Crone sat down in its reading-room to say the names,
and something was standing by the east wall that had come in without the door opening. It showed nothing in the glass.
The candle's flame leaned toward it, and the ink went white at the edge of the page nearest it. In the morning the next
circle found the seven chairs in their ring with no bodies in them, no bones and no dust, and the floor along that
wall has stayed dry in every season since.

## The breaking years (about 1090 to 1114)

**The wound that hatched, about 1093** *(proposed)*. A chapter of the processions was built down both lips of a great
wound in the ground, as long as a morning's walk. In its last year the wound would not take the blood. It crusted over
from end to end, warm underfoot, and the hoods broke the crust with picks to pour the cup, and it closed over the
blood again before the cup was empty. In the night after the fast-day the crust split along its whole length. What came
out walked in the hoods' robes, which had hung on their pegs in the crypt: the shapes of children with nothing behind
their eyes, in the order of the procession. At the front walked something tall, sewn together out of more than one
thing, carrying the Maiden from the niche on its back, and the Maiden had a face now. The Rule in the crypt gained new
clauses in a hand that leans the other way. The last of them says *She hears.* The procession went out, and has never
come home.

**The grave too many** *(proposed, about 1095)*. At the Hollow Wood's edge, every door stood open one morning, the beds
still holding the shapes of the sleepers, and there was nothing left of anyone: no bone, no tooth, no smell. The one man
left dug a grave for every hearth and laid a shawl or a pair of boots in each, and dug one more than he had names for.
Something that leaves nothing had passed.

**The fall of the Gilded Peak, 1098.** In the spring the monks began to die in their rooms at night, one or two a
night, and in the morning there was nothing in the room but a little black glass and a little dust. For nine days the
abbot counted them, and on the ninth he sent the youngest down the mountain with a letter and told him not to look
back. On the tenth night the great bell over the courtyard rang, long, though it had never rung where it hung. In the
morning the courtyard was black glass from wall to wall, warm to the hand, and it is warm still. The sanctum's doors
stood open. The gilded saints were in their niches, a hundred and seven of them, and the last niche on the left was
empty as it had always been kept, except that the dust before it held footprints going out. Over the door someone had cut
in the night *THEY WERE NOT WEEPING FOR US*, or, as others read it, *UNDER THE GOLD THEY WERE STILL LOOKING*. Since then
the monks who were below the mountain walk the roads carrying nothing, and are called the Empty Hands, and what they
strike leaves glass, dust, or nothing.

**The loose winter, 1106.** The dead did not go through. The mirrors were covered and the felt laid right, and still,
for forty nights, the dead came back up the valleys to the doors of the houses they had died in, and asked whoever
opened the door the same thing, quietly: *Which way?* An eldest of the circles stood up in the coat of glass, which she
had not done in years, and said, *There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes*, and sat down, and died,
and did not go through either. After that some circles took the felt off and let the dead stop at their own faces in
the glass, and kept them there, and carried them. The circles split: the Covered, who send the dead on faster and
fiercer than ever and burn uncovered glass, and the Unveiled, who keep the dead in glass and walk the roads with them
circling like small cold lights. The coat of glass stood up on its own, full of the kept, and has not fallen down.

**The siege at the Red Water, 1107 to 1114.** In the wet season of 1107 the dead began to come up out of the Red Water
again at the shore folk's village. They were not the shore folk's dead. They came from somewhere far along the water,
wearing pilgrims' cloaks and carrying staffs, and stood at the edge of the trees looking up at the fire. On the ninth
night they did not go back down. After them came the things that were never alive in the way people are: things that
crawl up out of the surf on their fingers, things with no faces, things that come up through the sand. For seven
winters the shore folk held their wall:
- they split cedar for it, and pulled down their own huts and canoes to mend it;
- each fighter danced the slow dance a dead teacher had taught them before going up on the wall, so that if they fell,
  the teacher would know them and come for them before the Red could;
- they played the drum and the pipe all night, so that the fighters would not hear the dead calling up to them by name;
- they burned their dead every night, and after the second winter never let the fire go out.

In 1114 the wall broke at its wet end, where the posts had gone soft, and the last eleven died on their temple's
platform, at the top of the swept stair.

## Now, 1114

The fire in the ring of huts above the black sand still burns the dead, and nobody is seen to feed it. A merchant picks
the village clean, and laughs at everything a stranger does not know. In the temple a candle burns that no one living
could have lit. The stair has not been swept.

Across the Hide the lanterns are fewer every year. The Count rises. The rings with teeth are said to be coming nearer to
each other, and someone is still cutting them. The wind goes out and never comes back.

And somewhere a pilgrim is walking who does not know where it came from: out of a ring, out of a dream, or out of
nothing, given a fate by a reading of the cards (`00-the-deep-lore.md`).
