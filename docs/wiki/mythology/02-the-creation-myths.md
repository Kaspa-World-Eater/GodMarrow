# The creation myths

Nobody was there. Every people that has lived on the Hide tells the beginning, or told it, and every telling is a face
put on something that has none. What the world is made of was too large and too strange for any of them to see whole, so
each people took the part it could bear to look at and gave that part a face. The faces are masks. The myths are what
the masks say.

They agree on almost nothing. They agree on this much:
- the ground is warm;
- the dead do not stay down;
- things still come up out of the body;
- the world is a body, or was.

A child in the lowlands counts it on her fingers:

> *Soul for the thread and Bone for the frame,*
> *Flesh for the filling, and none for the name.*

## The Tithed: the First Morning

*What they look at:* nothing they can name. The Tithed have no beginning of their own. They have a morning.

Every memory the Tithed have starts in the same place. A grey morning; ash underfoot and in the mouth; the sky low and
the light coming from one side; and everyone lying down, scattered over the ash in the cold, as if they had been set
there in the night. Nobody remembers lying down. Nobody remembers anything before it. The grandmothers say "before the
morning" the way other people say "before you were born", and they mean the same thing by it: a place nobody can get
back to, and nobody is to try.

That much every telling agrees on. How the people came to be lying there, no two tellings agree, and the tellers have
stopped expecting to. The tellings heard most often:
- **Dreamed.** The grandmothers of the lowlands say the old one was dying under the ash that night, and the dying dream,
  and what the dying dream of most is company. It dreamed of people around it, and dreamed so hard that in the morning
  they were there. That is why, some nights, you dream a dream that is not your own, of warm dark and a sound like
  breath: it is the old one's dream, still running in you, and you are in it.
- **Grown.** The diggers of the lowlands say people came up out of the Hide the way everything comes
  up out of a body left lying: the old one died, and the warm ash bred, and what it bred was them. They say it without
  shame. Everything that lives here grew out of something dead, they say. Why should we be the only ones ashamed of it?
- **Given back.** The shore folk say the first people came up out of the Red on the first morning, red to the hair,
  crawling up the black sand on their hands, because the Red gives back whatever goes into it, and that morning it gave
  back a people. Nobody asks them what had gone in.
- **Counted.** The Pale Order says people were always here, laid in the Mother like the bones of a hand, and the morning
  is only when the first of them was counted. Before the count, nothing is anything.
- **Left.** Some old women say the people were here before the morning, a long time before, living under the old powers
  in the old age, and that whatever took the old one's names that night took everyone's names with it, and left the bodies
  lying on the ash to wake up with nobody in them. They say: we are not new. We are what was left.

None of the tellings says who the people were before, or whether there was a before. The Tithed have learned to leave
it there.

**The debt.** Every telling agrees on one thing more: the people owe. Whoever or whatever gave them the morning gave it
on credit, and a tenth of everything is still owed. That is the name. The Tithed who are left leave a tenth of whatever
they grow standing in the ground, the tenth loaf on the sill overnight, the tenth word of a long telling unsaid. Every
tenth night they keep awake, so that whatever comes to collect finds someone up to pay it. Who they owe, they do not
say; they do not know. What they say is: *we are behind on it*, and they work the way people work who are behind on
something, from dark to dark, and do not rest well.

**The Hide.** They called the land they woke on the Hide. It is the skin of the thing that died beneath it, the
grandmothers say, and anyone who lies on it at night can feel that it is warm. And it is where the people hid, ever
after, from whatever had taken the names and from whatever came up out of the ground, keeping their heads down and
their true names behind their teeth. Both meanings are meant. A child who asks which is told: *yes*.

Everyone left on the Hide has a saying for what it hides, and no two agree. *All flesh is hide*, the common saying goes,
*and it hides.* What does it hide? The brothers of the Pale Order say: the bones. The processions of the Bleeding
Maiden say it hides nothing; it only holds in the flesh, which is bursting at every seam. The circles of the Veiled Crone
say it hides the name, and the person under the name, and that the true self is hidden from the one who wears it most
of all. The keepers of the Myriad say it hides the breath. The grandmothers say only: *it hides*, and that a child who
asks what is old enough to be told.

*Behind the myth.* Nobody knows where the people came from (Derek's ruling, 2026-10-09), and the bible never picks.
Each telling is a little true, as everything told here is (`00-the-deep-lore.md`). The pilgrim's waking in the opening
cave, up out of the Red with no before, rhymes with all of them and answers none.

## The Pale Order: the Falling

*What they look at:* the bone. The frame of the world that will not lie down. The brothers gave it a mother's face,
**the Bearing Mother**, because a frame that carries everything had to be someone who carries.

Before the Count there was no count. That is all a brother may say of it, and in the Order's oldest books that time is
written as a gap, a space left on the page.

Then the Mother fell. She came down out of that time nobody speaks of, the frame that the whole world hangs on, and
where her bones came to rest, there the world was. The ridges are her bones. The pale ground of the Ossa is bone
ground fine. The lowlands are her far edges, where the flesh still lies over her and hides what she is.

The first brothers were there. They stood beside her as she fell, and they did what had to be done: they laid her
bones. Every bone went to its place, and every bone was given its name, and none was dropped and none was broken. When
a bone came to them whose name they did not know, they did not lay it nameless. They named it for what it was and where
it lay. And as they laid them, they counted them, because a name can be taken from a thing and a number cannot. That
was the first Laying, and the first Count, and the Order has been laying and counting ever since.

The world was set down, the brothers say, as a load is set down at the end of a long road, and it is waiting to be
set down once more. Over the deepest stair of every bone-house it is cut: *WE WERE HERE WHEN SHE FELL. WE
WILL BE HERE WHEN SHE RESTS.*

**The keep.** The brothers have one word for everything they do, and it is *keep*. A bone-house is a keep: walled, gated,
and the last thing standing on its ridge. They keep the Count, which is to say they keep it whole. They keep the dead,
which is to say they keep a body together after the flesh has let its bones go, every bone in its place and none
dropped. And they keep a secret, which is the meaning the others are built on and the one no brother says aloud. The
Rule cut on the deepest stair of every bone-house uses the word twice: *Keep her where she is. Keep it.*

*Behind the myth.* The bone is not a mother. It bears nothing, chose nothing, and is not lying still. **It is
growing.** Every year the Count comes out higher than the year's dead can account for, and rooms appear that nobody cut.
The dust weighs more than the bone it came from, and the pale sky over the Ossa thickens and hangs lower. The
bone-houses were never the Order's work: they are the upper floors of the Angles' halls, which the Order found and
keeps hidden. The Rule on the deepest stair says only *Keep her where she is.* None of this is ever said in the game; it is
only let show.

## The Gilded Peak: the Held Breath

*What they look at:* nothing, on purpose. The monks would not give what they looked at a face, a name or a will, and
they would not call it a god; a god is a thing that wants, and what they looked at wants nothing. They called it That
Which Cannot Be Named, and That Which Cannot Be Held, and when they had to be brief they left a pause.

Before anything, there was the Silence, and it lacked nothing. Everything that could ever be meant was already in it,
the way everything a sentence is for is already in the pause before it is spoken.

Then something held. Not someone: a holding, the way a breath is held, the way a pause is held a moment too long before
the word. Everything that has ever been is inside that one held breath: the light, the ground, the dead, the mountain,
whoever is reading this. Nothing did the holding. It held. The world, the monks taught, is what a held breath looks
like from the inside, and that is the whole of their beginning, and they taught it as a warning: whatever is held, will
be let go.

**The two hands.** That Which Cannot Be Held has two hands, and both are empty, and both destroy.
- **Radiance** is the open hand. It is light with no shadow, which shows a thing so wholly that nothing is left of it
  but the showing. The monks wrote the word for looking with a gap in it, *be hold*, because to behold a thing
  completely is to stop holding it. A man who stares into Radiance long enough is not blinded. He is seen through,
  and then he is not there. Radiance is not mercy and it is not wrath. It is destruction by light, and it is pure.
- **Absence** is the closed hand. It is not the dark, which is still something. It is the closing itself: the letting
  go, the turning away, what is given up and not taken back. It destroys by keeping nothing. The dark is only what
  Absence leaves behind.

Between the two hands the monks walked, and their whole discipline was to hold nothing: not a name, not a wish, not a
breath longer than it was needed. Hold nothing, and neither hand has anything to take from you. That is why the ones
who came down the mountain afterward call themselves the Empty Hand.

They gilded what they honoured, because a thing covered in gold shines so that it is looked at and never seen: held
between beholding and letting go, where neither hand can finish it.

They said one more thing, always on the same stair: *Everything on the Hide is a wound that has not yet been allowed to
close. We are wounds too. We have tried to be clean ones.*

The few in the lowlands who remember the Peak at all say the monks climbed so high to peek at what cannot be named, and
that it peeked back.

*Behind the myth.* No god in it: That Which Cannot Be Named is the Silence, unknowable, the Tao read as a void
(`00-the-deep-lore.md`). Radiance and Absence are the Empty Hand's two sands. The gilded masters in the Peak's sanctum
were not dead under the gold; the gold kept them from finishing. Whether the Silence is a power, a place, or simply what
is there when nothing is being said, is unknown, and stays unknown.

## The Veiled Crone: the First Thread

*What they look at:* fate. The way lives are pulled toward each other and apart, and the sense that something, somewhere,
is weaving what happens. The circles gave it an old woman's face, veiled, because what cannot be seen must be covered.

Before her, everything was like a dry wall in a shut room. It was dry in every season, it did not move, and nothing
held it.

Then she came, and she drew the first thread out of nothing, and she tied the first knot, and the knot was the world.
Every life is a thread. She spins it out of the knot, and weaves it into her cloth, and the pattern the threads make
together is fate, which nobody inside the cloth can see, because it can only be seen from the far side of the loom.

**What the weaving makes.** Where two threads are woven together, the circles teach, they make something neither of them
was. Lovers are one thing woven of two. So are a mother and her child, and a killer and the one he killed, which is why
the dead and their killers are never free of each other. A family is a creature with many threads and one pattern; so is
a feud; so is a haunting, which is two threads that were never cut apart. The Crone weaves souls into each other as
easily as wool, and what she weaves lives, in its own way, and wants, and is not the sum of the threads that made it.
The circles say that some of the old powers were only that: so many lives woven so tight for so long that the cloth
itself woke, and wanted.

**The needle's eye.** Every thread goes through the eye of her needle before it can be woven, and through it again when
a life is done and its thread is drawn into the cloth for good. The eye is a hole. It is the one place in all her
weaving that is not cloth and not thread, and it is the one thing she does not make. Novices are taught that the eye is
where a thread goes in. The old weavers know it is where a thread goes out, and that whatever is on the other side of
it was there before her. They do not name it. They hum it, with the mouth shut.

She has four faces. She is the Spinner, who draws the thread out. She is the Weaver, who sets it in the cloth. She is
the Unraveller, who takes back what was woven wrong. And she is the Eye, the hole every thread passes through, whose name
is only hummed.

When the old one beneath died, the threads of the world went slack, the way a weaver's hand tires at the treadle. The
circles were set to tighten them again, and that is what remembering is for: a thread that is remembered stays taut. So
they sit in rings from dusk to the grey hour saying her names, until the names and the selves saying them are woven
into one thread.

The keepers do not say the last part in front of novices, and the novices always learn it anyway. Someone had tied a
knot before she began.

*Behind the myth.* There is no hand at any loom. Whatever pulls the threads finishes nothing; the heresy of the loose
winter, "There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes," comes nearer the truth than the faith can bear.
The eye of the needle is the Silence, the void every telling passes through (`00-the-deep-lore.md`). One thing the
circles know is real: a soul cannot pass its own reflection.

## The Bleeding Maiden: the Wound

*What they look at:* the flesh, the warm, growing, mindless stuff of the world. The processions gave it two faces:
**the Maiden**, the opening that gives, and **the Mother**, the growing.

In the beginning there was a wound. It was a wound in nothing, for there was nothing yet to be wounded. It opened, and
it did not close.

What came out of it was flesh. The flesh grew because nothing told it to stop, into the hills and the mires and the
warm ground and every living thing, and it is growing still. The opening is the Bleeding Maiden, the first gift, the
side laid open so that something could come out. The growing is the Mother.

The Mother has no mind. This is the heart of the faith, and the penitents say it without shame. She does not choose and
she does not refuse. She takes what she is given and makes from it, and what she makes has no head unless someone tells
her what it is for. So the faithful give their blood back into the wounds of the world, a measured cup once a year, and
say the Words over it as it pours. The Words tell her whose blood it is and what it is for. Blood given with the Words
comes back, in time, as a saint on the stair. Blood given without them is given to no one. Blood given is not blood
taken.

Of the old one's death the processions remember nothing. The wound was already open when they came to it, and the cup
came down to them from somewhere. Their faith begins with the first cup filled.

There is another telling, and the processions do not tell it. The few who remember the Wasting say the Maiden was a
girl, once, who stepped into an iron maiden out in the waste and closed it on herself, and did not die, and bled, and
went on bleeding for longer than anyone could stand to watch; and that the wound in the ground where the first cup was
poured is the channel her blood wore under the iron's foot (`03-the-age-of-terrors.md`, the Wasting). A maiden in a
maiden. The processions say the wound came first. The grandmothers say the girl did.

*Behind the myth.* What the flesh is, nobody knows. The faith's own creed, that it has no mind, is the most
frightening thing it says. Deep in Shog-Mire it is whispered that something in the red water is learning the words.

## The Myriad: the Breath

*What they look at:* the breath, and what rides it. The Shrine Keepers say that nothing a person thinks stays inside
them. It goes out on the breath, and it does not go out alone.

Every thought held long enough grows a kami, a spirit the size of the thing it was made of: a grudge nursed through a
winter, a promise nobody meant to keep, a lie told so often the liar half believes it, a worry for someone who has not
come back, a hope gone sour. The keepers call the ones born of words **kotodama**, the souls in words, and all of them
together the myriad, because nobody has ever counted them.

The kami live in the breath. A thought spoken aloud goes out on the breath, and its kami goes with it, out of the mouth
and into the air, looking for another mouth to get into. Breathing in is how they enter. So whoever is left on the Hide
breathes shallow near strangers, covers the mouth before speaking of anything that matters, and never says a grudge
aloud: said, it is loose, and someone will breathe it, and it will grow in them.

When the old one beneath died, it breathed out its last, and every thought it ever had went out on that one breath, a
myriad of kami at once, into the grey. That breath is the one wind, which never turns. Everyone breathes it. Every mind
on the Hide has some of the dead one's thoughts in it, breathed in on the wind, and does not know which.

**Discord, which is good.** Anyone would think peace among the kami a blessing. The keepers think it the worst thing
there is. Every belief contradicts some other, and the kami fight in the breath: a promise against the lie that broke it,
a hope against the fear under it. That quarrel is what keeps each of them small, and keeps each thing itself: a stone
stays a stone because it disagrees with the river, a person stays a person because what she believes argues with what
she fears. **Discordia**, the keepers call it, and they tend it like a fire.

**Concord, which is the end.** When kami agree, they run together, and many kami of one mind are one great one, and a
great one is a power with a will of its own: an Ythraal, as the old powers were. Every great horror of the old age began
as a crowd of small beliefs that stopped arguing. **Concordia** is the keepers' word for that, and they say it the way
other people say plague. A world in perfect agreement, they say, is a world in which nothing is anything in particular.

**Miasma** is breath gone foul: so crowded with kami, so many of them rotten (spite, shame, the kami of old lies nobody
remembers telling), that it sickens whoever breathes it. It hangs in low places and in the rooms of the dying. It is the
dead one's last breath, going bad as it goes out.

**Still, steal, steel.** The Shrine Keepers are no purifiers, whatever the streamers on their belts suggest. Those who
fear them call them **breath stealers**, or plain **thieves**, and the keepers do not argue: to them it is the same
word. They say they **still** a breath. To still a breath is to steal it and stop it: to take it out of the air where
miasma hangs thickest, out of the sick, out of the dying (whose last breath is the fullest of all), and hold it, with
its kami still in it, longer than anyone living can hold anything. A keeper holds a breath through a night, then a season
of nights; the eldest say they have not truly let one go in years. And a breath held long enough sets in the chest like
**steel**, so that when a keeper breathes it out, she breathes out a blade. Still, steal, steel: novices learn the three
as one word, and so does everyone who has been breathed on.

Where the keepers let a stolen breath go is their whole art. Into a shrine, a small house on a post with a door that
does not open, where the caught kami are shut in and fed a whispered quarrel each season so that they never agree. Or
into the face of whoever the keeper wants harmed. A keeper breathing out at you is the last thing many on the Hide have
seen.

Some come to them on purpose. A widow asks a keeper to take her grief out of her, and the keeper leans close and steals
it, and the widow feels lighter, and the keeper keeps it. Nobody asks what becomes of everything a keeper holds, or
which shrine along the road holds a grief, which a curse, and which a dead man's last thought.

*Behind the myth.* The kami are the law of the world at its smallest (`00-the-deep-lore.md`): every thought told enough
takes a body, and breath is how a thought is told. The keepers are the only ones who handle belief with their own
bodies, and it shows: they go grey, they cough, their lips go dark, and a keeper who holds too much grows horns and walks
down into the water, still holding. The faith calls those ones **kegare**, the word for the filth they carry. Nobody has
followed far enough to see what they become.

## The Second Mouth: the Two Mouths

*What they look at:* the same flesh as the processions, from the other side: its hunger. This is the one cult, hidden
inside what is left of the processions, and its beginning is theirs turned inside out.

The old one had two mouths, the cult teaches. With the first it breathed. It breathed in once, at the beginning, and
everything there is was inside that breath; and since it died it has been breathing out, and the world is the long
exhale going out of it, the wind the Myriad's keepers ride. With the second it ate. The second mouth is still open,
somewhere under the Hide, and it is still hungry, and it is the only one of the two that matters now.

So the world is not a wound to be bled into, whatever the processions say. It is food that has not been eaten yet. The
processions give their blood back to the Mother a cup at a time, so that she will stay fed and quiet under the skin;
the cult says that is starving her, because blood poured into a wound runs into the ground, and the ground is only the
outside. Only what is eaten is carried. Only what is carried is kept. So the faithful eat: the warm earth of the mires,
the meat of the things that come up, and, at the great feasts, their own dead, so that nothing of the faithful is ever
lost to the outside. What they carry, the Mother has: eyes again, and hands, and a voice.

The first of them cut the first second mouth. She opened a mouth in her own body, over the breastbone, and sewed its
lips open with gut, and fed it before she fed the one in her face, and every one of the faithful since has done the
same. The Words of the processions run both ways in the cult: said forward they give, said backward they take, and the
Mother cannot tell who is taking her.

Creation is not finished, the cult says. It will be finished when the second mouth has eaten everything the first one
breathed out.

*Behind the myth.* The cult's founder has outlived her name, and the mouths cut into her have begun to answer one
another. The processions burn a body wherever they find a sewn breastbone. Whether anything is walking out through the
second mouths, nobody outside the dark has seen. Its end is the Swallowing (`06-the-ends-of-the-world.md`).

## The shore folk: the Fallen One and the Red

*What they looked at:* the blood. A sea of it at the edge of the world, and the dead it gives back. The shore folk gave
it no face at all, only a body, and they never called it water: it was **the Red**, which was its name and not its
colour.

Before there were people, and before there were trees, there was the Fallen One. It came down out of somewhere higher
than the sky and lay down, and the land is its body. The forest grew out of its back, the way young cedars grow out of
a trunk that fell long ago, standing in a row along it and drinking from it, and that is why the great trees are so
tall.

But it did not lie down whole. As it fell, it was struck, or it struck something on the way down; the keepers never
agreed which. It came down with a wound in its side, the side that faces away from the trees, and it bled. Blood runs
downhill. It ran past the edge of the trees and past the edge of the land and pooled at the low edge of everything, and
it lies there still. That is the Red. It has no other side.

The black sand is the Fallen One's edge, burned black where the Red touches it and ground fine. And whatever goes into
the Red comes back. Of the tide the keepers told two things, and never chose: that it is the Fallen One's heart, still
beating, very slowly, because it has not finished dying; and that it is the Grandmother of the Red, at the bottom of
it, drawing in her braid and letting it out (`06-the-ends-of-the-world.md`).

*Behind the myth.* The Red is blood, and nobody has reached a far shore. The canoes that went looking found only more of
it, warmer the farther they went. What lies at the warm end is unknown (the shell, some say, `03-the-age-of-terrors.md`).
The wind of the world goes out over it and never comes back. The mountains above the shore that bleed into it are the
Fallen One to the shore folk, and the One That Unfolds to the oldest books; both stand.

## The older voices

The peoples who were here before the Tithed left little but stone, ash and a few words. These are their beginnings, as
far as anyone has made them out, and what the peoples after them made of them.

**The cities in the sky** left the most and are understood the least (`03-the-age-of-terrors.md`). Their beginning is
known from the words cut on the undersides of their fallen cities, where only someone looking up from below would read
them, and it is a boast. In the beginning, it says, there was a heavy world, and everything in it was held down: the
rivers held in their beds, the people held to the ground, the gods held to the people who feared them. Then the first
of us opened a bone and found the weight inside it, and drank it, and was light. *We were the first thing in the world
that did not fall.* Everything after that, in their telling, was going up, and every line of it ends on the same word,
*higher*. The few who have read it note that the cities it is cut on are lying in the ash, upside down, and that whoever
carved it never imagined anyone would read it from above.

What they could do is told as if it were magic, because nobody now can tell it apart from magic. They made doors that
opened onto other doors a hundred leagues off. They made lamps that have never gone out, and some of those lamps still
burn in the ash where Ghenn came down, under the grass, so that on a dark night parts of the lowlands glow faintly from
beneath. They wrote with light. Who they were, the peoples after them have never agreed:
- **the lowland grandmothers** say they were the old one's own thoughts, which got out through the top of its head while it
  slept and could never get back in, and went up looking for somewhere else to be thought;
- **the Pale Order** says there were never any people in the cities at all, only the lamps, and that the lamps built
  the cities to hang themselves in;
- **the few diggers** who still go under the lowland grass for the lamps say they were people like us, and that we will
  be them again, and that is why they dig;
- **the shore folk** say nothing about them, and do not look at the dark moon.

**The Choir**, behind the ribs: *We were the part of the old one that told it what it was.* Their beginning was the first
note, and they were eleven hundred and one voices singing it in turns so that it was never dropped.

**The Angles**, the many-angled host, who cut the halls inside the thing, left no word for their beginning, only a
sign: a sigil cut at the head of every stair they made, all angles, more angles than its lines can hold, which hurts the
eyes to look at and cannot be copied by any hand that has tried (`04-the-held-space.md`). The oldest songs sing of them
the way other songs sing of something else: the host of the Angles, the Angles of the first room, the Angles who fell,
and an Angle at the shoulder of whoever sleeps in their halls. The singers will not say which word they mean. Those who
have looked at the sigil long enough say it is a picture of the moment they began, seen from every side at once, and
that it is not finished.

**The Unison**, the single mind that lived in many bodies in the mire: the whole of the beginning was one thought, a
long one, and they were inside it, thinking it together, and it was the only time they were warm all through.

**The Ghauul**, the emptied folk of the fallen cities: they say nothing of a beginning. They remember being full.

**The Vhoor-Qhan**, who came from outside: *Something was opened, and we came in where it was open. There was never a
beginning. There was only a way in.*

**The Deep**, under the skin of the world, in the iron dark: *The blood stood still, and grew heavy, and the weight of
everything lay on it, and it hardened, and we woke in it.*

## The sky: the sun and the moons

Over the Hide the sky is grey. It has been grey for as long as anyone remembers, by day a low pale lid of cloud with the
sun behind it as a white smear that gives light and no warmth, and at night a darker lid with the moons behind it. On
some nights the cloud thins and the moons stand out clear, and on very few days the sun does, and whoever is left goes
out to look. Every people that has lived on the Hide has a telling of what is up there. Here are the ones told most.

**The grandmothers of the lowlands** say that when the old one died, somebody laid coins on its eyes, so that it would not
see what came after. Those are the two moons. One is silver, and that is the pale moon, the great one, whose light the
night roads are walked by. The other was tarnished before it was laid, and that is the dark moon, small and red-brown
and dull, which is hard to see except when it crosses the face of the pale one and bites a piece out of it. And the
sun, the grandmothers say, is the third eye, the one in the brow, which would not close. There was no third coin for
it. It stares down at the world through the grey, clouded white as a dead man's eye is clouded, and that is why the
day gives no warmth: it is a dead eye looking.

**The shore folk** say there were three moons once, and one of them fell. It fell out of the sky and lay down, and the
land is its body, and the Red Water is its blood (see above). The two left in the sky go round and round looking for it.
On the nights when they come close together, nose to nose over the sea, they are looking hardest, and the Red rises
higher up the black sand than on any other night, reaching for them. The shore folk do not go down to the water on
those nights.

**The grey itself**, everyone agrees, is newer than the sun. The oldest tellings say the sky went grey on the
first morning. The Myriad say the grey is the old one's last breath: it breathed out as it died and the breath is still
going out, rising off the whole Hide and hanging between the world and the sun, and the sky will not clear until the
breath has all gone. The circles of the Veiled Crone say the grey is her veil, drawn over the whole sky, and that the
two moons are her eyes behind it, one open and one shut, and that when the dark moon crosses the pale one she is
blinking.

**The Gilded Peak** taught that the sun is the open hand of the Silence, Radiance, which shows everything and so takes
everything away, and that the dark moon is the closed hand, Absence. The pale moon between them is the world, being
weighed. The monks watched the dark moon's crossings and wrote down how much of the pale one it covered, every time,
for as long as the Peak stood, and the numbers were getting larger.

**The Pale Order** says that when the Mother fell, the first brothers needed light to lay her bones by, and they lit
two lamps and hung them where nothing could put them out. The pale moon is the lamp they lit first. The dark moon is
the second, which has been guttering since the first night. The brothers count the moons' turns as they count
everything, and in the deepest books the count of the dark moon's turns is written in a different hand from the rest,
older, and does not agree with it.

**The processions of the Bleeding Maiden** say the sun is not an eye or a hand but a wound in the sky, and the light is
what bleeds through it from the other side.

**The cities in the sky** left their own telling, cut in the undersides of the ones that fell. *We made the second
moon*, it says. *We lifted a city so high it would never come down.* Those who have read it have looked a long time at
the dark moon through the gaps in the cloud. It is not round. It has edges, and it turns, slowly, and on the clearest
nights there are, some of them swear, lights on it.

*Behind the myth.* What the sun and the moons are, nobody knows, and it stays unknown. The pale moon is the night's key
light in every scene (the moonlight the pilgrim walks by). The dark moon is rarely seen, and its crossings are an
omen in every faith. In the world, whether it is a coin, an eye, a lamp or a city is never settled, and every telling stands; the makers
know it is Qhaloth, the great city in the sky, dead and rusting where it stopped rising (`03-the-age-of-terrors.md`).
The sun stays unknown in the world; the makers lean on the Maiden's telling, a hole in the sky with the light of
somewhere else bleeding through it, under a grey lid clouded like a dead man's eye.
The grey sky is the Last Breath's, and the deep lore rhymes it: the old one's breath is still leaving the world.
