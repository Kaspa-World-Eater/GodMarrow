# The creation myths

Nobody was there. Every people of the Hide tells the beginning, and every telling is a face put on something that has
none. What the world is made of was too large and too strange for any of them to see whole, so each people took the
part it could bear to look at and gave that part a face. The faces are masks. The myths are what the masks say.

They agree on almost nothing. They agree on this much:
- the ground is warm;
- the dead do not stay down;
- things still come up out of the body;
- the world is a body, or was.

Children in the lowlands count it on their fingers:

> *Soul for the thread and Bone for the frame,*
> *Flesh for the filling, and none for the name.*

## The Tithed: the First Morning

*What they look at:* nothing they can name. The common people have no beginning of their own. They have a morning.

Every memory the Tithed have starts in the same place. A grey morning; ash underfoot and in the mouth; the sky low and
the light coming from one side; and everyone lying down, scattered over the ash in the cold, as if they had been set
there in the night. Nobody remembers lying down. Nobody remembers anything before it. The grandmothers say "before the
morning" the way other people say "before you were born", and they mean the same thing by it: a place nobody can get
back to, and nobody is to try.

That much every hearth agrees on. How the people came to be lying there, no two hearths agree, and they have stopped
expecting to. The tellings heard most often:
- **Dreamed.** The grandmothers of the lowlands say the god was dying under the ash that night, and the dying dream,
  and what the dying dream of most is company. It dreamed of people around it, and dreamed so hard that in the morning
  they were there. That is why, some nights, you dream a dream that is not your own, of warm dark and a sound like
  breath: it is the old one's dream, still running in you, and you are in it.
- **Grown.** The farmers of the lowlands and the diggers say people came up out of the Hide the way everything comes
  up out of a body left lying: the god died, and the warm ash bred, and what it bred was them. They say it without
  shame. Everything that lives here grew out of something dead, they say. Why should we be the only ones ashamed of it?
- **Given back.** The shore folk say the first people came up out of the Red on the first morning, red to the hair,
  crawling up the black sand on their hands, because the Red gives back whatever goes into it, and that morning it gave
  back a people. Nobody asks them what had gone in.
- **Counted.** The Pale Order says people were always here, laid in the Mother like the bones of a hand, and the morning
  is only when the first of them was counted. Before the count, nothing is anything.
- **Left.** Some old women say the people were here before the morning, a long time before, living under the old powers
  in the old age, and that whatever took the god's names that night took everyone's names with it, and left the bodies
  lying on the ash to wake up with nobody in them. They say: we are not new. We are what was left.

None of the tellings says who the people were before, or whether there was a before. The Tithed have learned to leave
it there.

**The debt.** Every telling agrees on one thing more: the people owe. Whoever or whatever gave them the morning gave it
on credit, and a tenth of everything is still owed. That is the name. The Tithed leave the tenth row of every field
standing, the tenth loaf on the sill overnight, the tenth word of a long telling unsaid. Every tenth night they keep
awake, so that whatever comes to collect finds someone up to pay it. Who they owe, they do not say; they do not know.
What they say is: *we are behind on it*, and they work the way people work who are behind on something, from dark to
dark, and do not rest well.

**The Hide.** They called the land they woke on the Hide. It is the skin of the thing that died beneath it, the
grandmothers say, and anyone who lies on it at night can feel that it is warm. And it is where the people hid, ever
after, from whatever had taken the names and from whatever came up out of the ground, keeping their heads down and
their true names behind their teeth. Both meanings are meant. A child who asks which is told: *yes*.

*Behind the myth.* Nobody knows where the people came from (Derek's ruling, 2026-10-09), and the bible never picks.
Each telling is a little true, as everything told here is (`00-the-deep-lore.md`). The pilgrim's waking in the opening
cave, up out of the Red with no before, rhymes with all of them and answers none.

## The Pale Order: the Falling

*What they look at:* the bone. The frame of the world that will not lie down. The brothers gave it a mother's face,
**the Bearing Mother**, because a frame that carries everything had to be someone who carries.

Before the Count there was no count. That is all a brother may say of it, and in the Order's oldest books that time is
written as a gap, a space left on the page.

Then the Mother fell. She came down out of that time nobody speaks of, the frame that the whole world hangs on, and
where her bones came to rest, there the world was. The ridges are her bones. The white ground of the Ossa is bone
ground fine. The lowlands are her far edges, where the flesh still lies over her and hides what she is.

The first brothers were there. They stood beside her as she fell, and they did what had to be done: they laid her
bones. Every bone went to its place, and every bone was given its name, and none was dropped and none was broken. When
a bone came to them whose name they did not know, they did not lay it nameless. They named it for what it was and where
it lay. And as they laid them, they counted them, because a name can be taken from a thing and a number cannot. That
was the first Laying, and the first Count, and the Order has been laying and counting ever since.

The world was set down, the brothers say, as a load is set down at the end of a long road, and it is waiting to be
set down once more. Over the deepest stair of every bone-house it is cut: *WE WERE HERE WHEN SHE FELL. WE
WILL BE HERE WHEN SHE RESTS.*

*Behind the myth.* The bone is not a mother. It bears nothing, chose nothing, and is not lying still. **It is
growing.** Every year the Count comes out higher than the year's dead can account for, and rooms appear that nobody cut.
The dust weighs more than the bone it came from, and the pale sky over the Ossa thickens and hangs lower. The
bone-houses were never the Order's work: they are the upper floors of the builders' halls, which the Order found and
hides. The Rule on the deepest stair says only *Keep her where she is.* None of this is ever said in the game; it is
only let show.

## The Gilded Peak: the Mouth

*What they look at:* the Silence itself, and the world as a pause inside it. Of all the faiths, the monks came nearest
to looking at the thing without a face, and they fell.

Before anything, there was the Silence, and it lacked nothing. Everything that could ever be meant was already in it,
the way everything a sentence is for is already in the pause before it is spoken.

In the Silence a mouth opened, and the opening was the god. It drew one breath in, and held it. Everything that has
ever been was inside that one held breath: the light, the ground, the dead, the mountain, every people. It was a small
thing. The Silence it opened in did not notice it. Creation, the monks taught, is a pause held too long.

For an age the god held its space open and held everyone inside that space, and it grew tired, as a held breath always
grows tired. When it could hold no longer, the Silence came back into the place where it had always been. The lowlands
call that the death of the god and are afraid of it. The Peak called it rest: the god grew tired, and the Silence came
in to let it lie down.

The Silence, the monks taught, has two hands, and both are empty. The open hand is Radiance: light at noon with no
shadow, which takes things away by showing all of them at once. The closed hand is Absence: the dark, which takes
things away by showing none of them. So the monks gilded what they honoured, because a thing covered in gold shines so
that it is looked at and not seen.

They said one more thing, always on the same stair: *Everything on the Hide is a wound that has not yet been allowed to
close. We are wounds too. We have tried to be clean ones.*

*Behind the myth.* The gilded masters in the Peak's sanctum were not dead under the gold; the gold kept them from
finishing. The chant did not hold the breath-sickness off the mountain: the Silence was so near that breath went
quiet. Whether the Silence is a power, a place, or simply what is there when nothing is being said, is unknown, and
stays unknown.

## The Veiled Crone: the First Thread

*What they look at:* the threads, the way lives pull on each other, the sense that something somewhere is weaving what
happens. The circles gave it an old woman's face, veiled, because what cannot be seen must be covered.

Before her, everything was like a dry wall in a shut room. It was dry in every season, it did not move, and nothing
held it.

Then she came, and she drew the first thread out of nothing, and she tied the first knot, and the knot was the world.
Every life is a length of that one thread. She spins it out of the knot, and weaves it into her cloth, and when a life
is over its thread goes through the eye of her loom and into the cloth for good. Nothing is lost; it is only woven in.

She has four faces. She is the Spinner, who draws the thread out. She is the Weaver, who sets it in the cloth. She is
the Unraveller, who takes back what was woven wrong. And she is the Hollow, the eye of the loom that every thread
passes through at the end, whose name is never spoken, only hummed with the mouth shut.

When the god died, the threads of the world went slack, the way a weaver's hand tires at the treadle. The circles were
set to tighten them again, and that is what remembering is for: a thread that is remembered stays taut. So they sit in
rings from dusk to the grey hour saying her names, until the names and the selves saying them are one thread.

The keepers do not say the last part in front of novices, and the novices always learn it anyway. Someone had tied a
knot before she began.

*Behind the myth.* There is no hand at any loom. Whatever pulls the threads is not a weaver and finishes nothing; the
heresy of the loose winter, "There is no loom. It goes on and on, and it never finishes," comes nearer the truth than
the faith can bear. One thing the circles know is real: a soul cannot pass its own reflection. What they hum instead of
naming may be the Silence.

## The Bleeding Maiden: the Wound

*What they look at:* the flesh, the warm, growing, mindless stuff of the world. The processions gave it two faces:
**the Maiden**, the opening that gives, and **the Mother**, the growing.

In the beginning there was a wound. It was a wound in nothing, for there was nothing yet to be wounded. It opened, and
it did not close.

What came out of it was flesh. The flesh grew because nothing told it to stop, into the hills and the mires and the
warm ground and every living thing, and it is growing still. The opening is the Bleeding Maiden, the first gift, the
side laid open so that something could come out. The growing is the Mother.

The Mother has no mind. This is the heart of the faith, and the penitents say it without shame. She does not choose and
she does not refuse. She takes what she is given and makes from it, and what she makes has no head unless someone tells
her what it is for. So the faithful give their blood back into the wounds of the world, a measured cup once a year, and
say the Words over it as it pours. The Words tell her whose blood it is and what it is for. Blood given with the Words
comes back, in time, as a saint on the stair. Blood given without them is given to no one. Blood given is not blood
taken.

Of the god's death the processions remember nothing. The wound was already open when they came to it, and the cup came
down to them from somewhere. Their faith begins with the first cup filled.

*Behind the myth.* What the flesh is, nobody knows. The faith's own creed, that it has no mind, is the most
frightening thing it says. Deep in Shog-Mire it is whispered that something in the red water is learning the words.

## The Myriad: the Breath

*What they look at:* the breath, and what rides it. The Shrine Keepers say that nothing a person thinks stays inside
them. It goes out on the breath, and it does not go out alone.

Every thought held long enough becomes a small god. Not a great one; the great ones need whole peoples to fear them for
an age. A small one needs only a single mind and a little time: a grudge nursed through a winter, a promise nobody meant
to keep, a lie told so often the liar half believes it, a mother's worry for a child who has not come home, a hope that
has gone sour. Each grows a small, hungry god of its own, the size of the thing it was made of. The keepers call them
the myriad, because nobody could count them, and their faith is named for them.

The small gods live in the breath. A thought spoken aloud goes out on the breath, and its god goes with it, out of the
mouth and into the air, looking for another mouth to get into. Breathing in is how they enter. That is why the people of
the wet country breathe shallow among strangers and cover their mouths when they speak of anything that matters, and
why nobody there will say a grudge aloud: said, it is loose, and it will be breathed in by someone, and it will grow.

When the great one beneath died, the keepers teach, it breathed out its last, and every thought it had ever had went out
on that one breath: all its small gods at once, more than there are stars, into the grey. That breath is the one wind
that blows across the Hide and never turns. It is full of them. Everyone on the Hide breathes it, all the time. Every
mind on the Hide has some of the dead god's thoughts in it, breathed in on the wind, and does not know which ones.

**The quarrel.** The small gods are not good, and they are not kind to each other. Every belief contradicts some other
belief, and their gods fight in the breath, the god of a promise against the god of the lie that broke it, the god of a
hope against the god of the fear under it. The keepers call this **discord**, and they bless it. While the small gods
quarrel, they stay small. When enough of them agree, they run together into one, and one large enough is no small god
any more: it is an Ythraal, a fear grown great, the way the old gods grew. **Concord** is the word the keepers use for
that, and they say it the way other people say plague.

**Miasma** is breath gone foul: breath so crowded with small gods, and so many of them rotten (spite, shame, the gods of
old lies nobody remembers telling), that it sickens whoever breathes it. It hangs in low places and in the rooms of the
dying. It is the dead god's last breath, going bad as it goes out.

**The keepers.** The Shrine Keepers are not purifiers, whatever the paper streamers on their belts suggest. They steal
breath, and are proud of it. They take it: from the air where miasma hangs thickest, from the sick, from the
dying, whose last breath is the fullest of all, and they hold it in their own lungs with the small gods still in it.
They can hold a breath longer than anyone living. They learn to hold one through a whole night, and then through a
season of nights, and the old ones say they have not truly let one go in years. And they breathe it out where they
choose: into a shrine, which is a small wooden house with a paper door and nothing inside but what was breathed into
it, where the caught gods are shut in and fed whispered beliefs to keep them quiet; or into a paper figure, which takes
the breath and is burned; or into the face of whoever they want harmed. A keeper breathing out at you is the last thing
a great many people on the Hide have seen.

People pay them. A widow pays a keeper to take her grief out of her, and the keeper leans close and breathes it in, and
the widow feels lighter, and the keeper keeps the grief. A village pays a keeper to take the miasma out of a sickroom.
Nobody asks what the keeper does afterward with everything she holds. Nobody wants to know which of the shrines along the
road hold a grief, and which a curse, and which a dead man's last thought.

*Behind the myth.* Read through the deep lore (`00-the-deep-lore.md`), the small gods are the law of the world at its
smallest: every thought told enough stands up, and breath is how a thought is told. The keepers are the only people who
handle belief with their own bodies, and it shows: they go grey, they cough, their lips go dark, and a keeper who holds
too much grows horns and walks down into the water, still holding it. The faith calls those ones **kegare**, which is
the word for the filth they carry. Nobody has followed far enough to see what they become.

## The Second Mouth: the Door

*What they look at:* the same flesh as the processions, from the other side. This is the one cult, hidden inside the
processions, and its creation is the processions' turned inside out.

The world is the outside of a body. The Mother is inside, and every wound in the world is a door she is
trying to come out of. What comes up when a wound opens is her, looking for somewhere to go.

Worship starves her. Every cup poured and every Word said forward keeps her down under the skin, blind and still, and
the faithful call that keeping her holy. Taken into a living body, she has eyes again, and hands, and a voice. The Words
run both ways: forward they give, backward they take, and the Mother cannot tell who is taking her.

Creation is not finished. It will be finished when she has walked out of the world wearing her people.

*Behind the myth.* The cult's founder has outlived her name, and the mouths cut into her have begun to answer one
another. Whether anything is walking out through them, nobody outside the dark has seen.

## The shore folk: the Fallen One and the Red Water

*What they look at:* the blood. A sea of it at the edge of the world, and the dead it gives back. The shore folk, at
the start of the game, gave it no face at all, only a body.

Before there were people, and before there were trees, there was the Fallen One. It came down out of somewhere higher
than the sky and lay down, and the land is its body. The forest grew out of its back, the way young cedars grow out of
a trunk that fell long ago, standing in a row along it and drinking from it, and that is why the great trees are so
tall.

But it did not lie down whole. As it fell, it was struck, or it struck something on the way down; the keepers never
agreed which. It came down with a wound in its side, the side that faces away from the trees, and it bled. Blood runs
downhill. It ran past the edge of the trees and past the edge of the land and pooled at the low edge of everything, and
it lies there still. That is the Red Water. It has no other side.

The tide is the Fallen One's heart, still beating, very slowly, because it has not finished dying. The black sand is
its edge, burned black where the blood touches it and ground fine by the water. And whatever goes into the Red comes
back.

*Behind the myth.* The Red Water is blood, and nobody has reached a far shore. The canoes that went looking found only
more of it, warmer the farther they went. What lies at the warm end is unknown. The wind of the world goes out over it
and never comes back.

## The older voices

The peoples who were here before the Tithed left little but stone, ash and a few words. These are their beginnings, as
far as anyone has made them out, and what the peoples after them made of them.

**The cities in the sky** left the most and are understood the least (`03-the-age-of-terrors.md`). Their beginning is
known from the words cut on the undersides of their fallen cities, where only someone looking up from below would read
them, and it is a boast. In the beginning, it says, there was a heavy world, and everything in it was held down: the
rivers held in their beds, the people held to the ground, the gods held to the people who feared them. Then the first
of us opened a bone and found the weight inside it, and drank it, and was light. *We were the first thing in the world
that did not fall.* Everything after that, in their telling, was going up, and every line of it ends on the same word,
*higher*. The scholars who read it note that the cities it is cut on are lying in the ash, upside down, and that whoever
carved it never imagined anyone would read it from above.

What they could do is told as if it were magic, because nobody now can tell it apart from magic. They made doors that
opened onto other doors a hundred leagues off. They made lamps that have never gone out, and some of those lamps still
burn in the ash where Ghenn came down, under the grass, so that on a dark night parts of the lowlands glow faintly from
beneath. They wrote with light. Who they were, the peoples after them have never agreed:
- **the lowland grandmothers** say they were the god's own thoughts, which got out through the top of its head while it
  slept and could never get back in, and went up looking for somewhere else to be thought;
- **the Pale Order** says there were never any people in the cities at all, only the lamps, and that the lamps built
  the cities to hang themselves in;
- **the diggers** who go under the lowland grass for the lamps say they were people like us, and that we will be them
  again, and that is why they dig;
- **the shore folk** say nothing about them, and do not look at the dark moon.

**The Choir**, behind the ribs: *We were the part of the god that told it what it was.* Their beginning was the first
note, and they were eleven hundred and one voices singing it in turns so that it was never dropped.

**The Angles**, who cut the halls inside the thing, left no word for their beginning, only a sign: a sigil cut at
the head of every stair they made, all angles, more angles than its lines can hold, which hurts the eyes to look at and
cannot be copied by any hand that has tried (`04-the-held-space.md`). The oldest songs sing their name so that it can
be heard two ways. Those who have looked at the sigil long enough say it is a picture of the moment they began, seen
from every side at once, and that it is not finished.

**The Unison**, the single mind that lived in many bodies in the mire: the whole of the beginning was one thought, a
long one, and they were inside it, thinking it together, and it was the only time they were warm all through.

**The Ghauul**, the emptied folk of the fallen cities: they say nothing of a beginning. They remember being full.

**The Vhoor-Qhan**, who came from outside: *Something was opened, and we came in where it was open. There was never a
beginning. There was only a way in.*

**The Deep**, under the skin of the world, in the iron dark: *The blood stood still, and grew heavy, and the weight of
everything lay on it, and it hardened, and we woke in it.*

## The sky: the sun and the moons

Over the Hide the sky is grey. It has been grey for as long as anyone remembers, by day a low pale lid of cloud
with the sun behind it as a white smear that gives light and no warmth, and at night a darker lid with the moons
behind it. On some nights the cloud thins and the moons stand out clear, and on very few days the sun does, and the
people go out of their houses to look. Every people of the Hide has a telling of what is up there. Here are the ones
told most.

**The grandmothers of the lowlands** say that when the god died, somebody laid coins on its eyes, so that it would not
see what came after. Those are the two moons. One is silver, and that is the pale moon, the great one, whose light the
night roads are walked by. The other was tarnished before it was laid, and that is the dark moon, small and red-brown
and dull, which is hard to see except when it crosses the face of the pale one and bites a piece out of it. And the
sun, the grandmothers say, is the third eye, the one in the brow, which would not close. There was no third coin for
it. It stares down at the world through the grey, clouded white as a dead man's eye is clouded, and that is why the
day gives no warmth: it is a dead eye looking.

**The shore folk** say there were three moons once, and one of them fell. It fell out of the sky and lay down, and the
land is its body, and the Red Water is its blood (see above). The two left in the sky go round and round looking for it.
On the nights when they come close together, nose to nose over the sea, they are looking hardest, and the Red rises
higher up the black sand than on any other night, reaching for them. The shore folk do not go down to the water on
those nights.

**The grey itself**, everyone agrees, is newer than the sun. The oldest tellings say the sky went grey in the year
of the coming out. The Myriad say the grey is the god's last breath: it breathed out as it died and the breath is still
going out, rising off the whole Hide and hanging between the world and the sun, and the sky will not clear until the
breath has all gone. The circles of the Veiled Crone say the grey is her veil, drawn over the whole sky, and that the
two moons are her eyes behind it, one open and one shut, and that when the dark moon crosses the pale one she is
blinking.

**The Gilded Peak** taught that the sun is the open hand of the Silence, Radiance, which shows everything and so takes
everything away, and that the dark moon is the closed hand, Absence. The pale moon between them is the world, being
weighed. The monks watched the dark moon's crossings and wrote down how much of the pale one it covered, every time,
for as long as the Peak stood, and the numbers were getting larger.

**The Pale Order** says that when the Mother fell, the first brothers needed light to lay her bones by, and they lit
two lamps and hung them where nothing could put them out. The pale moon is the lamp they lit first. The dark moon is
the second, which has been guttering since the first night. The brothers count the moons' turns as they count
everything, and in the deepest books the count of the dark moon's turns is written in a different hand from the rest,
older, and does not agree with it.

**The processions of the Bleeding Maiden** say the sun is not an eye or a hand but a wound in the sky, and the light is
what bleeds through it from the other side.

**The cities in the sky** left their own telling, cut in the undersides of the ones that fell. *We made the second moon*, it
says. *We lifted a city so high it would never come down.* The scholars who read it have looked a long time at the dark
moon through the gaps in the cloud. It is not round. It has edges, and it turns, slowly, and on the clearest nights
there are, some of them swear, lights on it.

*Behind the myth.* What the sun and the moons are, nobody knows, and it stays unknown. The pale moon is the night's key
light in every scene (the moonlight the pilgrim walks by). The dark moon is rarely seen, and its crossings are an
omen in every faith. In the world, whether it is a coin, an eye, a lamp or a city is never settled, and every telling stands; the makers
know it is Qhaloth, the great city in the sky, dead and rusting where it stopped rising (`03-the-age-of-terrors.md`).
The sun stays unknown in the world; the makers lean on the Maiden's telling, a hole in the sky with the light of
somewhere else bleeding through it, under a grey lid clouded like a dead man's eye.
The grey sky is the Last Breath's, and the deep lore rhymes it: the god's breath is still leaving the world.
