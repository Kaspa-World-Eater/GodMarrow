# Before the god died: the Uncounted, the Age of Terrors, the Starving

The world the congregation walked out into was already a ruin. Long before them, other peoples lived on the thing
beneath, in the open and the dark, and their fears grew gods. This is what is told of that time. Almost all of it is
wrong, and it is all that is left. Nothing here has a date; the ages before the god's death were never counted.

## How the loud gods were made

Every people that has studied the old ruins agrees on one law, and it is the oldest law in the world: **a belief held
long enough by enough people grows a body.**

Fear grows one fastest. Worship feeds it, and terror feeds it best of all, so the old powers ruled by terror: a city
that trembles is a city that feeds its god. A god that nobody remembers starves. Belief does not pass through a god and
away, either: it settles, in the bones of anything long feared or long prayed to, and hardens there into **marrow**,
which is why every faith of this age digs. And when people sleep, the powers walk in their dreams. A nightmare is
something feeding.

Not everything that walked was a god. There were great things that never learned a name, older than any fear. There
were the dead. And there were things from outside the world that owed nothing to anyone's belief, and some of those
ate gods.

*Behind the myth.* This law is the deep lore showing at the surface (`00-the-deep-lore.md`): everything here is told,
and what is told enough stands up. Every faith of the Last Breath lives off the marrow of what was believed before.

## The dragons

In the Uncounted the peoples lived in the open, without walls, and what they feared most was what came down out of
the sky. The fear grew bodies, vast and burning, that came down out of the sky as the people had always feared they
would, and took what they wanted. These were the dragons. They were one power among many in that age, perhaps the
greatest, perhaps only the ones that lasted longest. They ruled the open country, and the peoples crawled.

Then the peoples built walls, and behind the walls the sky was smaller, and the fear was smaller, and the dragons
began to starve. They starved one by one, slowly, the way a great fire goes out. In a valley of the white country stand
ribs, twenty-three of them or twenty-four, depending on who counted, and some say they were one of the dragons.

One did not starve. When its worshippers grew few, it ate them, and when there were none left, it began on its own
flesh, a little at a time, to stay awake. It sits on a broken throne in a citadel it has consumed from the inside,
wearing an iron crown it forged when it still had subjects to forge for. It is very thin now. It wants nothing in the
world so much as to be feared again. It is called the Starved Crown.

The grandmothers of the white country tell their children: when you go out at night, do not be afraid of the dark.
That is how it finds you.

*Behind the myth.* The Starved Crown is the last dragon and the dracolich (Derek's ruling). It is still awake,
somewhere, thinner every age.

## The Famines

Some gods grew so vast that their bodies outlasted their lives. These were the Famines: god-corpses the height of hills,
which kept walking after whatever moved them was gone. Whole kingdoms worshipped them by climbing the long ramps of
their bodies and going inside, and were not seen again. Most of the Famines have fallen and lie where they fell, and
grass grows on them. One still walks. It takes an afternoon to cross the edge of the sky, and where it has trodden the
ground keeps a pulse you can feel in your feet.

High on its side, at dusk, there are lights. They are the lamps of Dhum the Swallowed, the last city that climbed up
into it, still lit after all these years. Nobody knows who lights them.

*Behind the myth.* Note the rhyme. Kingdoms climbed into the Famines to worship, and the congregation knelt inside the
thing beneath. Whether the world itself is the last and greatest of the Famines, no myth says. It is left for the
player to wonder.

## The walls that stood up

There were cities that worshipped their walls. A wall keeps a people alive, and a people that lives behind its wall
long enough begins to pray to it, and a thing prayed to long enough stands up. The greatest of these was Vesk. One night
its walls stood up and walked, and the city went with them, because the city was inside them. Its people turned to
iron where they stood, in the streets and doorways and at the windows, and went too.

Vesk walks still, a grey line moving on the far edge of the world. Where it has stopped to rest it leaves a square of
flattened ground with the marks of streets in it, and sometimes an iron child in the middle, too heavy to carry,
left behind.

## The tower that drinks

There is a tower full to the top of one thing that moves, and the thing is full of eyes, and every eye is someone's. It
is awake. It has always been awake. A town in its sight dreams no dreams of its own: the tower drinks them, and the
people lie down and close their eyes and open them in the morning with nothing between.

## The reaper below

In a cavern floored with the dead of a whole age lies a skeleton too great to measure, whose right arm ends in a scythe
grown out of its own bone. It may be asleep. Nobody living has seen the cavern; a drawing of it is the oldest thing
anyone keeps.

*Behind the myth.* Every bone-house of the Ossa is cut in the shape of the reaper's cavern. The builders who cut them
copied it, or remembered it, or were told to. The Pale Order does not know this, or will not.

## The shell beneath

Under a delta of blood lies a shell the size of a cathedral, filtering the red water through itself, slowly, the way
the sea filters through sand. What drowns near it does not stay drowned; its slime raises them. A city called Thessar
sank itself on purpose to be near it, every street and tower going down in one night, and the lights of its windows
were seen under the water for a year.

## The serpent of the wet country

In the low wet country lay a god that was long, longer than a day's walk, so long that nobody ever saw both its ends at
once. It lay in the black water with its back above the surface, and the peoples of the wet country walked on its back
from one dry place to another. It let them, because they fed it. Where it coiled round on itself there was a pit of
black stone with gutters cut to it, and once a year the throats of the chosen were opened over the gutters, and the
blood ran down into the dark, and the serpent drank. In return the water stayed in its place, and the road on its back
stayed above it.

The boatmen of the later age remember a king of that country who heard the serpent calling him from under the water.
He put on his crown and walked out along its back with his whole court behind him ringing bells, and where its back
went down under the water, he walked down into the water as if it were a stair, and his court went down after him,
ringing. The boatmen say he is still walking down, and that on still nights you can hear the bells.

The serpent was still being fed when the thing beneath died, and long after (`05-the-last-breath.md`). Its true name
is lost. Everyone who has walked its back calls it something else:
- **the Long Back** (the bog folk, who think their own people laid it as a road);
- **Saint Uss's Causeway** (pilgrims, after a saint in no calendar whose name was worn down like a step);
- **Old Coil** (children);
- **the Stair of the Drowned King** (boatmen);
- **the Long One** (the shore folk, who fed it once).

*Behind the myth.* A demon serpent god, long dead (Derek). It starved when the feeding stopped, and broke, as starved
gods do. Its spine is the Sunken Bog's road, and its pit of offering still glows red at the throat, from out of sight
below. What the red is, nobody knows. Whether it was the serpent that was fed there, or something under it, is told
both ways.

## The peoples of the age

They are gone, or nearly, and they left ruins in every act.

**The climbers** of the heights believed the sky was an open mouth and that the whole world was inside it. A people
that knows it is inside a mouth does not stay. They built a stair up into the cloud and climbed it, all of them,
carrying their children, leaving nothing behind for the mouth to close on. The town at its foot was left with every
door open. None of them came down.

**The One Swallow** was one mind in many bodies, in the mire that is now Shog-Mire. They built the stepped tower and
the causeway that runs to it, and nobody has lived in either since. A clay tablet of theirs tells their beginning and
then ends in a row of marks nobody can read, repeated to the edge of the clay.

**The tall hollow ones**, the scholars' Marrow-Eaters, were made of wanting. They are courteous. They knock at a door
after midnight, softly, three times, and ask whether there is marrow in the house, and thank you when you say there is
not, and do not go away. They are looking for a warm room where once they stood and wanted nothing.

**The lords** were men and women believed into small godhood: kings, abbots, matrons of great houses, generals, feared
and prayed to so long that they rose above their kind. Some were too believed-in to lie down when they died. Those are
the liches.

**The Unforgotten** drink the belief that blood carries, a little from many, over many years, and do not age. Iron,
fire and water do not kill them; only being forgotten does. One lives among a people, never two, and that people keeps
its name in every prayer without once saying whose it is.

**The mouths at the wick** were rivals of the gods, eaters of souls. They are still on the roads at night, at the edge
of every lantern's light, because a lantern has a soul in it.

## Outside of everything

Some things owe nothing to the thing beneath, to any god, or to the Silence. The scholars call them the Visitors.
- **The Tallyman** comes wherever a count is wrong, to take the difference.
- **The Ferryman** still takes fares across a river that left long ago, and you come out on the far side wet to the
  knees.
- **The Gaunt Pilgrim** has been walking upward since before the god's last breath, on roads that go up even where the
  ground is flat, and where he passes the dead lie back down, and some of the living.
- **The Lectern** is a reading-stand with a book open on it, never found twice in the same place. The book is the life of
  whoever reads it, one page ahead.

## The Starving

It ended the way it had to. The peoples who fed the loud gods died, or stopped believing, or were eaten by what they
believed in, and the gods starved. **A god that starves to the end breaks apart into lesser things**, as a dead thing
breaks in the heat into flies. Those lesser things are the demons. They are hungry for what their god was fed and cannot
get it, so they bargain, because a bargain is the nearest thing to a prayer they can get.

The cities emptied. Knowledge died with the hands that held it, and what was written was written in scripts nobody now
reads. Some powers did not die: they slept, or went under, or kept walking because nobody told them to stop. The world
went dark and quiet, and the thing beneath lay under all of it, warm.

One telling, held by those who study the old marks and by no one else, says that in the last of the Starving, when the
open country was going dark, one people walked into the thing beneath, through its mouth, to kneel where it was still
warm. They carried salt, because the open world had salt and the thing beneath had none. The Tithed do not remember
this. They remember only that they were already kneeling.
