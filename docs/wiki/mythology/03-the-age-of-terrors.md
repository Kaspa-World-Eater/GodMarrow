# Before the old one died: the Lying Down, the age of the Ythraal, the Wasting

The world the Tithed woke up in was already a ruin. Long before them, other peoples lived on the thing
beneath, in the open and the dark. They rose higher than any people since, and their fears grew gods. This is what is
told of that time. Almost all of it is wrong, and it is all that is left. Nothing here has a date; the ages before the
god's death were never counted.

## How the Ythraal were made

Every people that has studied the old ruins agrees on one law, and it is the oldest law in the world: **a belief held
long enough by enough people grows a body.**

Whatever is held hardest grows one: hunger, grief, pride, need, love, and fear among them. Each makes its own kind.
Hunger makes a thing that eats; pride makes a thing that must be bowed to; grief makes a thing that will not let go; fear
makes a thing with teeth. A people that hungers for a hundred harvests finds one spring that the hunger has a mouth of
its own. A city that prays to its walls finds the walls listening. The later peoples call the powers of that age **the
Ythraal**, a word from a tongue nobody speaks, which everyone who says it says quietly. Each was made of what its people
held, and each ate the same thing it was made of.
A power nobody holds to any more grows thin, and then hungry, and then comes looking. So the Ythraal ruled by what they
were made of, and the peoples fed them, and the more they fed them the larger they grew. And when the people slept, the Ythraal walked in
their dreams, because a dream is a door left open. A nightmare is something feeding.

Belief does not pass through a god and away. It settles. In anything long feared or long prayed to, in a god's bones
and in the bones of the ground the old one walked on, belief sinks down and hardens into **marrow**: grey, warm, faintly
moving, heavier than lead. Marrow is power. Whoever eats it, burns it or draws it out of a bone can do what gods do,
for a while. Every great people of that age learned to dig it, and every faith since has dug it too.

But the oldest marrow of all, the deepest and the heaviest, was not the gods'. It was the world's: the marrow of the
thing beneath, laid down in its bones before any people stood on it, and it was what kept the thing beneath alive.
Every age that dug marrow dug a little of the world's own life out of it and burned it. A field dug for marrow gives
nothing afterward. A hill dug hollow sinks. The peoples who dug deepest called the grey country they left behind the
waste, and did not stop.

Not everything that walked was an Ythraal. There were great things that never learned a name, older than any fear.
There were the dead, who in that age did not lie down unless they were told. And there were things from outside the
world that owed nothing to anyone's belief. Nobody had dreamed them, so nobody could stop dreaming them. They were
worse than the Ythraal, because an Ythraal at least wanted to be feared, and these wanted nothing that could be given.

*Behind the myth.* This law is the deep lore showing at the surface (`00-the-deep-lore.md`): everything here is told,
and what is told enough takes a body. Marrow is belief settled into bone, and the world's own marrow is what kept the old one
alive. Every power that has been taken out of the ground since, by the Ythraal, the cities in the sky, the faiths and the
pilgrim's own hands, has been taken from the old one. Nobody in the world has put this together. The game never says it.
It lets the player notice that every source of power in the world is a wound in it.

## The Thrae, and the herald

In the Lying Down, before the Ythraal were many, three things came into the world.

Nobody knows from where. The few who have tried to say it say "from outside", and then, because outside is only the
dark between the stars and the dark between the stars is still the world's, they say "from outside the outside", and
stop. The later peoples called them **the Thrae**, an old word that sounds like *three* and may mean nothing more.

The first was the One That Unfolds: wherever it lay, the ground opened like a flower, layer under layer, and kept
opening. The second was the One That Eats the Edges: a mountain it had passed no longer ended anywhere, and a man it
touched could not tell where his hand stopped. The third was the One That Is Seen Sideways, and nobody who looked at it
straight ever described it, because nobody who looked at it straight said anything again.

They brought nothing with them: no servants, no spawn, no host. They did not hate the world and they did not want it.
Where they moved, rivers left their beds and wandered off, forests grew downward into the ground, and a people woke one
mourning with their faces on the backs of their heads and did not remember that it had ever been otherwise. Nobody has
ever said what they wanted. Nobody has ever been sure they knew the world was there.

**The fourth.** Every telling that counts the Thrae says three, and every telling says, a little later, that there was a
fourth, the way there is always one more step on a stair in the dark. The fourth did not come in. The fourth was the way
in: the opening the three came through, which nobody saw, because there was nothing there to see. The few who ever knelt
to the Thrae (there are always some who will kneel to anything vast enough) called it **the Whole**, the thing of which
the three were only parts. Those who feared it called it **the Hole**. In the oldest tongue on the Hide the two are one
word, and it is also the word for holy, and the old priests said it with their mouths open and nothing coming out.
Whole, holy, hole: what the three came out of, and what two of them went back into. Some say the fourth is the Silence.
Some say it is what the Silence is afraid of.

**The herald.** Then the herald came against them. What it was, the tellings agree only so far. It was a great beast,
long in the neck and winged, that had died once already and had not stopped. It was half bone and half meat: the meat
gone black and hard as old leather where the bone came through, the ribs open on one side, the belly empty. Its wings
were long fingers of bone with the hide still hanging between them in rags, and the rags caught the wind and held it.
It had no fire. It had weight, and it had rot: where it passed low, what grew beneath it blackened, and where it came
down, the ground sank under it. Its jaw hung open always, because what once closed it had rotted away, and the only
sound it made was the wind going through its throat. The Pale says the old one beneath raised it out of its own dead to
be its hand in the world. The shore folk said it came up out of the Red. Nobody else knows.

It fought the Thrae for longer than anyone has counted. And the strangest thing told of that war, told by every telling,
is that the Thrae never fought back. They did not turn. They did not seem to know. The herald tore at them and broke its
bones on them, and they went on through the world as if nothing were happening at all, the way the tide goes on while a
man beats at it with a stick.

In the end one of the three lay down at the edge of the Red Water and did not rise again, and its body is the mountains
there. That is why those mountains are made the way they are, fold under fold, black and broken into columns, and why
they bleed. Red runs out of their high clefts in falls and rivers, down through the folds and out across the flats into
the sea, and it has never stopped. The shore folk called those mountains the Fallen One and told them another way
(`02-the-creation-myths.md`). The other two went back into the Hole. Whether they found what they came for, nobody knows.

The herald fell. It had been broken against them too many times, and somewhere in the Pale it came down out of the sky
and struck the ground so hard that it made a crater a day's walk across, and lay at the bottom of it, still. A people of
that age found it there, a valley of bone and black hide in a cup of glass, and knelt. They built a city round it, ring
on ring up the crater's sides, and called it **Uth-Kharesh**, and worshipped the thing at the bottom as the thing that had
saved the world. It lay there and was worshipped, and it woke, hungry: a thing that is dead already does not need to eat,
but a thing that is believed in needs to be believed in. When the worship thinned, as all worship thinned in the Wasting,
it ate the worshippers, and when there were none left it began on itself. It sits now at the bottom of the crater on a
broken throne, in the city it has eaten from the inside, wearing an iron crown they forged for it when it still had
subjects. It is very thin. It wants nothing in the world so much as to be believed in again. The Pale calls it **the
Starved Crown**; the oldest texts, **Morrh-Ythraal**. When you go out at night, the grandmothers of the Pale said, do
not be afraid of the dark. That is how it finds you.

*Behind the myth.* The herald is the one dracolich (Derek's ruling), never called a dragon in the world: a great undead
beast of rotted hide and bone, without fire, killing by weight and rot. The Thrae are three, and the fourth is the hole
they came through, whole and holy and hole in one word; what they were and what they wanted is never told. The One That
Unfolds is the bleeding mountains above the Red (the blood rivers and their estuaries, `areas/the-red-shore.md`); the
shore folk's Fallen One is the same mountains told another way, and both stand.

## The Famines

Some Ythraal grew so vast that their bodies outlasted their lives. These were **the Famines**, whom the oldest texts
call **the Gorrhaun**, and they are named for what they left behind: wherever one walked, the harvest failed behind it
for a generation, the wells went dry and the marrow was gone out of the ground, because a god that has outlived its
people goes on eating anyway, and eats what is under its feet. They were god-corpses the height of hills, and they kept
walking after whatever moved them was gone.

Whole kingdoms worshipped them by climbing the long ramps of their bodies and going inside, and lived there, in the
warm, in the dark, in the old one, for generations, and were not seen again outside. Most of the Famines have fallen and lie
where they fell, and grass grows on them, and the few who live round them call them hills.

One still walks. It takes an afternoon to cross the edge of the sky, and where it has trodden the ground keeps a pulse
you can feel in your feet. High on its side, at dusk, there are lights: the lamps of **Dhum the Swallowed**, the last
city that climbed up into it, and the last city under the sky that still walks. The lamps are lit every evening, after
all these years. Nobody knows who lights them.

*Behind the myth.* Kingdoms climbed into the Famines and lived inside them for generations without seeing out. The rhyme is
meant to be heard. Whether the world itself is the last and greatest of the Famines, a god that
outlived its believers and lay down, and whether the Tithed are only the last kingdom that climbed up into one, no myth
says outright. The player is meant to come to suspect it. Dhum is the one walking city under the sky; Vesk, the iron
city, walks below, in the Deep (`04-the-held-space.md`), and is no Famine, whatever the readers say.

## The Needle

In a forest that no road goes to, there is a spike of iron standing up out of the trees.

It is higher than the trees by many times their height: straight, black, narrowing to a point that is lost in the grey.
It has no door, no window and no seam. Rust runs down it in long red streaks like the tracks of tears. The few
who live nearest it, as near as anybody lives, call it **the Needle**, and say it is a spike broken off the inside of an
iron maiden the size of a mountain, standing point up where it fell, and that one day whoever owns the maiden will come
back for it. The lowlands call it **the Sleepless**. The Citadel's oldest book calls it **Vhul-Oghra**, and says nothing
else. Nobody knows who made it, or whether anybody did, or how long it has stood. The trees round its foot are older
than any other trees in that forest, and all of them lean away from it.

It is not hollow. The few who have climbed it as far as the crack, high up, where the rust has eaten through, and put an
eye to the crack, say that it is full to the top of one thing: a liquid, clear and faintly yellow and very slow, like the
water in a blister, and that the liquid is working. Everything in it is coming apart. Bones hang in it at every height,
whole skeletons and pieces of skeletons, turning slowly, going soft at the edges like bread in broth, more of them the
lower you look, and at the bottom they lie packed like the sediment in a jar. And among the bones there are eyes: great
eyes, wider than a man is tall, white and veined, with no faces round them and nothing behind them, drifting in the
liquid. They are alive. When somebody looks in at the crack, they turn to the crack. Whatever is in the Needle is
digesting, and the eyes are what it cannot digest, or will not.

It drinks dreams. Whoever lives in its sight dreams no dreams of their own: they lie down and close their eyes and open
them in the mourning with nothing between. At first they do not mind. Then they find that they have stopped having ideas,
because an idea is only a dream that came in the daytime, and the Needle drinks those too. Then they cannot picture the
faces of their dead. The last thing to go is the ability to imagine tomorrow, and a people that cannot imagine tomorrow
sits down where it is and waits. There were villages like that once, in its sight. What is left of their people is
sitting there still.

And it calls. One night a sleeper gets up out of bed without waking and walks out into the forest, toward the Needle,
and does not come back, and in the mourning the others find that they can remember the sleeper's dreams, every one, as if
they had been handed round. The few who live near it bar their doors from the outside at night and tie their sleepers
by the ankle. How a sleeper gets into the Needle, when it has no door, nobody has seen. The rare traveller who passes
near it at night says that for a while afterward they dream other people's dreams, as if the Needle had spilled.

*Behind the myth.* Dreams are what gods are made of (`00-the-deep-lore.md`). Every dream the Needle drinks is a god that
will not be born. The few in the Last Breath who still keep records have noticed that no new power has been born anywhere
in all their records; none of them has looked toward the forest. The liquid is a gut's, and the Needle is digesting;
what it is digesting toward, and what the eyes are kept for, is never told. Its likeness to the spikes of the iron
maidens of the Wasting is meant: nobody in the world knows whether the maidens were made in its image or it in theirs.

## The reaper below

Death is a mantle too, and **Oss-Karrhath** wears it now.

When the old one beneath came to lie across the dark, something lay down under it, in the lowest place there is. It was
not a fear and not a belief. It was older than either, and older than the old one: it was the end of things, and here
everything that is wears a body, so the end wears one too. It lies in a pit at the bottom of the world, a skeleton the
length of a mountain range, curled on its side. Its right arm does not end in a hand. The bone of the forearm runs on
past the wrist and curves, thin as a new moon and as long as a river, a scythe grown out of itself.

It does not kill. Killing is too small a thing for it, and is left to others. It ends. Every life that closes, every age
that runs out, every power that wastes to nothing comes at the last to the edge of its pit, and the scythe moves, once,
and what was is over. It is very slow. The few who have studied it say it has moved its arm only a handful of times since
the world began, and that each stroke is an age: the Wasting was one; the old one's last breath was another. The next
stroke is coming. Nobody knows when; only that the edge has been drawn back a long way.

The few who know of it call it by many names. The Citadel's oldest book has one word under its drawing, which the last
reader there writes Oss-Karrhath. The Pale Order does not say its name at all. The children of the Pale, when there were
children there, called it Old Scythe and did not say it after dark.

**The pit.** Its mouth is round, a stone's throw across, in a country where nothing grows. It has no bottom anyone has
ever heard: a stone dropped into it is never heard to land. On the longest nights, those who once lived near it said, a
sound comes up out of it, slow and even, like a whetstone drawn along a blade.

**The mantle.** The old songs say death has not always worn the same body. Something wore the mantle before Oss-Karrhath,
and something will wear it after, and the songs say the mantle passes the only way a mantle can: whatever ends the end
becomes it. Nobody sings what ended the last one.

**The Ossuarch.** The Pale Order keeps one thing about it, and keeps it close. Its first brothers, the Order says, laid
the Mother's bones over the pit, layer on layer, to keep the reaper under, and the Order has kept it under ever since.
The Ossuarch is that keeping, made into an office: one man in a helm, sworn to the bone, with a sliver of the reaper's
own scythe-bone set in his plate where no one sees it. The bone answers him because it knows whose it is. The Order does
not say what happens when an Ossuarch dies with the sliver on him; it says only that another always takes the helm.

*Behind the myth.* A deity of death, a Titan of it: one of the world's oldest mantles, not grown from any people's fear
and not tied to any count. Its scythe moves once an age; the next stroke is coming, and it is what the pilgrim's turn
of the world is heading toward. The Ossuarch's bone-craft is its, borrowed through the sliver (the calling is a keeper of
death, not a servant of it). Whether the pilgrim, who takes up the mantle of what it came to end, will one day take up
this one, is never said.

## The shell beneath

Far out under the Red Water, past where any canoe has gone and come back, at the warm end where the blood grows hot,
there is a shell. It is not the size of a house or a cathedral. It is the size of a country, ridged and whorled, its
mouth turned downward into the dark, and the blood of the whole sea has silted against it for so long that its ridges
are mountains under the Red. Something lives in it. Something sleeps in it, and has slept since before the Lying Down,
and dreams.

When it breathes in, it draws the Red toward itself, and the blood goes back from every shore and bares the black
sand and the benches of rock and the dead. When it breathes out, the Red comes in. That is the tide, the drowned say:
the sleeper's breath, in and out, so slow that a whole day is one breath. The shore folk say the tide is the Fallen
One's heart. The two tellings have never agreed and never will, and on the black sand nobody minds which you believe,
as long as you are off the sand when the Red comes in.

What drowns near the shell does not stay drowned. Its dreaming reaches them, down in the red dark, and they wake, and
walk on the bottom of the sea, toward it or away from it, and on the out-breath some of them are carried back to the
shore. This is why whatever goes into the Red comes back. A city called Thessar sank itself on purpose to be near the
sleeper, every street and tower going down in one night, singing, and the lights of its windows were seen under the
water long afterward. The shore folk say that in the long calms you can still see them, far out, very deep, a city of
lights going on and off, as if someone were walking from room to room.

Some say that when the sleeper wakes it will not need to breathe the Red in and out any more, because it will drink it.

*Behind the myth.* Whether the sleeper is an Ythraal, one of the Thrae, or something older than the old one, nobody knows,
and it stays unknown. Its breath and the dying heart are rival tellings of the same tide; both stand. The dead the Red
gives back to the black sand are, read this way, its dreams washing up.

## The serpent of the wet country

Before the Ythraal, before the Thrae, before anyone counted anything, something long died in the low country.

Nobody knows what it was. It was long past reckoning, longer than a day's walk: a serpent's length, with a crowned head
on it and arms, many of them, folded under. That much the bones say. Whether it was a god, or a beast, or the Ythraal of
a people so old that nothing else of them is left, nobody knows, and the bones do not say. It died, and it lay where it
died, along the low country in a long slack coil, and it did what dead things do. It rotted.

It rotted for an age. Its flesh went down into the ground and the ground went soft. Its bile ran out of it, green-black
and bitter, and pooled in the low places and never drained, and its guts slid out and swelled and burst and spread over
everything, and that is the wet country: a whole country of its insides. The water of the Sunken Bog is its bile still.
That is why it is that colour, and why nothing drinks from it, and why the reeds that grow in it are bitter to the
root. The mounds in the mire are its guts gone to peat, and the gas that comes up through the water in slow bubbles and
burns blue on still nights is the last of its rotting.

Its bones did not rot. The bile soaked into them as they lay, and the bog, and the age, and they went to stone: a spine
of grey stone, each knuckle of it as big as a house, running in a long curve right through the wet country, the only dry
ground in it. So the peoples who came walked on it. It is the road. It has always been the road. Every people that has
lived in the wet country has used it to get from one dry place to the next, and most of them never knew it was a spine,
and walked along the top of it and sat down to rest on its knuckles.

Some knew. Where the spine rises at its far end toward the skull, which is under the mire, a people long gone cut a pit
of black stone at the throat, with gutters to it, and fed the dead thing. Once a year they opened the throats of the
chosen over the gutters, and the blood ran down into the dark, because they believed it was only sleeping, and that if
it were fed it would keep the water in its place. The pit still glows red from down there, out of sight. Nobody feeds it
now. The shore folk say their grandmothers' grandmothers did.

The boatmen remember a king of that country who heard the serpent calling him from under the water. He put on his crown
and walked out along the spine with his whole court behind him ringing bells, and where the spine goes down under the
water he walked down into the water as if it were a stair, and his court went after him, ringing. The boatmen say he is
still walking down, and that on still nights you can hear the bells.

Its true name is lost. Everyone who has walked its back calls it something else:
- **the Long Back** (the bog folk, who think their own people laid it as a road);
- **Saint Uss's Causeway** (pilgrims, after a saint in no calendar whose name was worn down like a step);
- **Old Coil** (children);
- **the Stair of the Drowned King** (boatmen);
- **the Long One** (the shore folk, who fed it once).

*Behind the myth.* A demon serpent god, long dead (Derek): a corpse and nothing more, and that is the horror of it. The
whole wet country is its rot, and its spine is the Sunken Bog's road, half sunk, its vertebrae poking up through the
water. What it was is never told. The pit of offering at its throat was cut long after it died, by a people who thought
it slept; what glows red down there, and whether anything ever answered them, is never told either.

## The peoples of the age

They are gone, or nearly, and they left ruins in every act.

**The cities in the sky.** Once there were cities in the sky. Nobody knows who built them. Nobody knows what the people
of them were, or whether they were people at all: no body has ever been dug out of their ruins that was not hollow.
What is known is this. There was one great city, and it hung in the air over the world, and under it, on chains, hung
lesser cities, two or three of them, each of which would have been the greatest in the world if the great one had not
been over it. The peoples under them lived their whole lives looking up at the undersides of cities. The oldest texts
give the great city a name that no throat can quite say, which the Citadel writes **Qhaloth** and admits is a guess.
The lowland grandmothers called it **the Lid**. The shore folk had no name for it; they did not look up.

What held the cities up was marrow. Their builders, whoever they were, drew it out of the deep bone of the world through
shafts a mile down, and burned it, and the burning held the cities in the air, the way the heat over a fire holds up a
cinder. And hanging was not enough for them. The words cut on the undersides of the fallen cities, where only someone
looking up from below would read them, say one thing in a hundred ways: *higher*. They meant to go up through the sky
and out of the world altogether, to wherever the light comes from, and they would stop for nothing.

When the marrow under the world began to give out, they did not stop. They took it from the lesser cities instead: out
of the stone first, and out of the lamps, and then out of the people, out of their bones, drawn up the chains to the
great city to be burned. A little, and then more, until there was nothing left in the bones of anyone below, and that
was not dying; they went hollow. Then the great city cut its chains.

The lesser cities fell. **Ghenn** came down on the lowlands in one night, and its ash lies under the lowland grass for a
hundred leagues, and its lamps, which never went out, still glow faintly up through the grass on dark nights.
**Sorrath** fell slowly, over lifetimes, tilting and shedding towers, and broke at last on the pale ridges, and
lies there upside down, so that the words cut on its underside face the sky. If there were others, they fell too far
away for anyone to find. Their people came down with them, and climbed out of the wreck, and went walking, hollow.

The great city went up. It rose through the grey and was never seen to come down, and the peoples under it said it had
got out. It had not. It is still there. It is the dark moon (`02-the-creation-myths.md`): not small, only very far off, a
city the size of a country hanging past the cloud where it stopped rising when the burning ran out, too high now ever to
fall. It is brown because it is rust. On the clearest nights, those who have looked at it longest say it has edges, and
turns, and that there are lights on it, moving. Nobody living has been there. The grandmothers say that its streets are
walked by the pale ghosts of whoever built it, still going about their business as if the city were still rising, and
that on its highest throne sits one dead thing that still gives the order to rise, every day, to a city that cannot
obey. The shore folk call that one **the Man in the Rust**. The Citadel calls it **the Regent**, and does not like to
talk about it.

**The Ghauul**, the emptied ones, are what came down out of the lesser cities: very tall, very
thin, courteous, hollow to the bone. They remember being full. They knock at a door after midnight, softly, three times,
and ask whether there is marrow in the house, and thank you when you say there is not, and do not go away. The few who
meet them on the roads call them the Marrow-Eaters, and say they crack the bones of sleepers.

**The Unison** were one mind in many bodies, in the mire that is now Shog-Mire. They built the stepped tower and the
causeway that runs to it, and nobody has lived in either since. They thought one thought at a time, all of them
together, and it took them a generation to think it. A clay tablet of theirs tells their beginning and then ends in a
row of marks nobody can read, repeated to the edge of the clay. It is thought to be the last thought they had, and that
they are still thinking it, somewhere, all of them, very slowly.

**The Hallowed** drink the belief that blood carries, a little from many, over many years, and do not age. Iron, fire
and water do not kill them; only being forgotten does. One lives among a people, never two, and that people keeps its
name in every prayer without once saying whose it is. Hallowed be the name.

*Behind the myth.* Who built the cities in the sky is never told, and nobody in the world knows. Their story is hubris
and marrow: they drank their own lesser cities hollow to climb, and climbed to nowhere. The dark moon is Qhaloth, dead
and rusting, walked by ghosts, with the one lich of that age on its throne; the sky myths half know it
(`02-the-creation-myths.md`). Ghenn's lamps under the lowland grass and Sorrath's upturned underside on the pale
ridges could be places the pilgrim stands.

## Outside of everything

Some things owe nothing to the thing beneath, to any Ythraal, or to the Silence. Nobody dreamed them up; they came, or
they were always there. The Citadel keeps them in a book of their own and gives them no name.

- **The Unborn** lies curled in the dark between the stars, a shape like a child the size of the night, not yet born.
  When it stirs the ground shakes; the Pale's grandmothers say the earthquakes are its kicks. When it is born,
  they say, it will be very hungry, and the world will be the first thing it sees.
- **The Drowned Twin** stands on the other side of every still surface: the Red Water in a calm, a puddle, a polished
  blade, the onyx. When you look in, it looks out, wearing your face, a little late. On the other side there is a world
  exactly like this one, where it lives your life a breath behind you, and it is tired of being behind. One day, the
  circles say, it will step through first.
- **The Gaunt Pilgrim** has been walking upward since before the old one's last breath, on roads that go up even where the
  ground is flat, and where he passes the dead lie back down, and some of the living.
- **The Lectern** is a reading-stand with a book open on it, never found twice in the same place. The book is the life of
  whoever reads it, one page ahead.

## The Wasting

It ended the way it had to. The marrow was gone out of half the world, burned in the sky by the cities there and dug out
by everyone else, and the ground the Ythraal walked on had nothing left in it to give. The peoples who fed the Ythraal died
of the waste, or stopped believing, or were eaten by what they believed in, and the Ythraal began to waste away. The
later peoples call this age **the Wasting**.

A god that wastes to its end does not die cleanly. It breaks apart into lesser things, the way a fallen tree breaks
down into the grubs that eat it, each one carrying a piece of its power's hunger and nothing else. These are **the
Wants**. A Want is a single hunger that was once part of a power, walking on its own: the want to be feared, the want to
be fed, the want to be remembered, the want to be held. It cannot be satisfied, because the old one it belonged to is gone,
so it bargains, because a bargain is the nearest thing to a prayer it can get. Every bargain anyone has ever made with
something in the dark has been made with a Want.

The peoples did not let their gods go easily. They fed them what they had left, and when they had nothing left, they
fed them pain. Pain is belief made sharp; a little of it goes a long way. So the Wasting was an age of penance. Whole
peoples walked in processions, scourging, to the places where their powers lay thin. They built **the iron maidens**:
upright coffins of iron, hinged, lined inside with spikes, and they stepped into them of their own will, and closed
them, and stood in rows in the wastes so that their suffering would go up to their power all at once. The iron drank their
blood. That is why the iron of that age is red at the heart when it is broken: it is old blood, gone stale and hard.
Some of the maidens still stand in their rows out in the waste, closed, rusted shut, and on a still day some of them
still hum.

Some of the penitents were looking for something else. A power that is wasting leaves an empty place behind it, and a
person who suffers long enough and is watched suffering by enough people is believed in. Here and there, in the iron,
one of them did not die. They became something: a saint, or a Want, or a god for a winter. This was **apotheosis**,
and in the Wasting every people that had lost its god tried it. Almost nobody survived trying. The ones who did were
not what anyone had hoped for.

One of them is remembered, though not by name. In the last of the Wasting, out in the waste, a girl stepped into a
maiden of her own will and closed it on herself, as thousands had before her, and did not die. The spikes went in and
the iron drank, and she went on bleeding: a day, a season, longer than anyone could stand to watch, and still they came
to watch, more of them every year, and the more of them came, the less she could die. Her blood ran out from under the
iron's foot and wore a channel in the waste, and the channel deepened into a wound in the ground that does not close.
The people who knelt round it began to call her by the only name they had for her: the maiden, the bleeding one. Then,
because a name said often enough takes flesh, the Bleeding Maiden.

The processions of the Bleeding Maiden do not tell it this way. They say the wound came first, before anything, a wound
in nothing (`02-the-creation-myths.md`), and that the Maiden is the opening that gives. The grandmothers of the
lowlands say the girl came first, and the wound after, and the faith last of all, and that the
processions' scale, their scourges and their cup are what is left of the crowd that came to watch her. Where the
processions poured their first cup there is a wound in the ground. Whether an iron maiden ever stood over it, closed,
the processions will not say, and nobody has dug.

The cities emptied. Knowledge died with the hands that held it, and what was written was written in scripts nobody now
reads. Some powers did not die: they slept, or went under, or kept walking because nobody told them to stop. The world
went dark and quiet and grey, and the thing beneath lay under all of it, warm, emptier than it had ever been.

*Behind the myth.* The Wasting is the marrow's cost come due: every power taken out of the ground was the old one's life,
and by the end of the age there was not enough left to keep it. Whether the old one died of the Silence, of its own
emptying, or of both, is unknown (`04-the-held-space.md`). The Bleeding Maiden's saints on the stair, the Pale Order's
brothers who outlive their deaths and the iron maidens are all the same hope: suffer, be watched, become. The game
lets the player see the rhyme and never says it.
