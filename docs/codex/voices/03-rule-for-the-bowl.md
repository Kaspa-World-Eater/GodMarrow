# The Rule of the Bowl, Given Down the Road

*Found folded in a grey smock on a milestone south of the Cheek, with a begging bowl of bone set on it against the wind. I took the leaves. I tried to take the bowl and could not lift it, and left it there.*

To the one who carries the bowl after me. I have left wide room between the lines, because the room is where it is written, and you were taught on the stair to read the room.

We went down from the Peak four, as bowl-bearers go, eleven noons before the night the courtyard became glass. We saw the mountain lit from inside with no sound, and in the morning the stair was full of the Pale Order's dead, standing, facing up. We did not go back. Of the four, the bowl and I are left. These are its rules as they were given to me.

**I. Of the bowl.** It is turned from one piece of the mountain's bone, ground thin at the lip, never painted. At the Peak we laid gold on everything we honoured until it could not be seen. The bowl was never gilded. My teacher said a thing that holds the pause must be seen, so that people know where to put what they give. Carry it in both hands, below the heart. Never set it on the ground of a town. Never wash it.

**II. Of asking.** Come to the edge of a town in the hour before noon. Do not knock and do not call out. Say the one syllable once, as we said it on the stair, and then hold the pause after it, longer than your breath allows. The pause is the asking. Those who hear it will come. When they stand before you, say only: give the name you can spare. Ask nothing more. Take what is given. They will give the names of their dead, of a child who did not stay, of a well, of the small god of a ford, sometimes their own. Every one is the same weight in the bowl. Do not choose.

**III. Of receiving.** The giver puts the mouth to the lip of the bowl and speaks the name into it, low. Hold still. Do not answer. When the name is said, hold the pause after it, and the name goes down into that pause the way a stone goes into still water, and the water closes. Watch the face of the giver. Something leaves it, and it is quieter after. Leave before dusk. Do not sleep in a town that has given.

Do not count the names. The Pale Order counts. They say a number outlasts a name. A number is a name with its meaning ground off, and they stand their dead in rows with numbers for faces, and that is what climbed our stair.

**IV. Where the names go.** A word means something only because of the quiet on either side of it. Take the quiet away and every word runs into the next, and the whole world is one noise. In that quiet, between every two words ever said, everything is held. Its name we write as a gap: [ … ]. The lowlands call it the Hush and fear it, and give to us anyway, which is the bravest thing I know.

A name kept in a mouth is kept badly. Mouths are warm and wet; they mumble, lie, wear a name thin with use, and then they die and the name goes with them into the ground. A name given into the pause is kept where the meaning of a word is kept, and fire does not reach there, nor marrow, nor the counting of bone.

**V. Of the open hand and the closed.** At the Noon of No Shadow the abbot raised the open hand, and that is Radiance: light laid on a thing until nothing is seen, a face looked at until it is gone. Then the closed hand, and that is Absence: dark laid on until nothing is seen. Neither hand keeps anything. Both erase.

Put the two hands together, neither open nor closed, cupped, and you have a bowl. I have never found the bottom of that. The bowl is what the hands make when they stop choosing.

The Gilt have misunderstood the open hand, and I say it gently, because some of them were my brothers on the stair. They lay a leaf of gold on themselves each day until no one can see them, and call that the last holiness. Radiance is a hand opened once, to let something go. They have opened the hand and climbed into it and stayed. I passed one on the Moor road at noon. I knew it only by the gap it made in the people walking, and afterward no one on that road could say who had been there, and I could not either, and I had known him.

**VI. Of the held breath.** The Peak teaches it so. Before anything, there was [ … ], and in it a mouth opened, and the opening was the god. It drew one breath in and held it, and everything that has been is inside that one held breath: the bone, the roads, the gods, you. It was small. The [ … ] it opened in did not notice it. The world is a pause held too long.

The end is the pause completed. The breath is let go, and what remains is the quiet it was held in, which held the meaning all along. Nothing is destroyed. The sentence is finished, and a finished sentence has been said. Our office, since the courtyard, is to be the empty space outside when it ends, holding nothing, so that nothing is kept wrongly. The names in it are being carried to the end of the sentence, where each will mean, at last, what it meant.

**VII. What I have noticed and cannot explain.** These are mine, and stand outside the Rule. Read them anyway.

The bowl is heavier each year. In the first year after the glass I carried it on two fingers. Now I carry it in both arms and rest it on my knees when I sit. It is empty. I have turned it over at noon, and nothing fell. At Four Wents a salt-carter set it on his scale for me. The beam went down and stayed down, and he would not have the scale back.

The towns behind me forget more than I took. At Mere we were given eleven names, and I know them, because I broke the Rule and counted. When I came back through Mere the next spring, the people there had lost the name of their ford, and of their well, and the word for the hour before noon. Their milestones were smooth where the letters had been cut. A woman stood at her door looking at her own son and could not say what to call him. No one had given his name. I wonder whether a name pulled out brings its neighbours with it, as a thread pulled from cloth brings the next. I do not know.

I swept the gilding hall for three years as a novice. The saints sat there gilded while still breathing, their faces to the east wall, and under the gold their eyes were open. The gold was laid thinnest over the eyes. All of them looked at one place on that wall, where nothing was. I swept beneath that place every morning and never once lifted my head to it. I do not know what they were looking at. On the road I have wished that I had looked.
