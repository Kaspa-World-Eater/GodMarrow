# Minutes of the Late Assize at Wendle Ford

*Leaves from the minute-book of the lowland circuit, bound in among the ordinary sittings at the reeve's hall at Four Wents. The reeve let me copy them if I read them aloud to no one.*

**Assize of the lowland circuit, sitting at Wendle Ford in the tithe-barn, the fourth night of the thaw, late, the lamps lit. Justice: Osric Hale. Clerk: Wat Ember, for the circuit.**

**Present, by the roll:** the justice; the reeve of Wendle Ford; a brother of the Pale Order, number four thousand four hundred and ten, attending for the tally of the field-dead; the plaintiff, Joan Crike, widow, holding the east strip below the barrows; the defendant, Aldo Venn, carter, holding the west strip; witnesses, four; the lantern-tender; the clerk. Eleven.

**The cause.** Of the merestone between the east strip and the west strip, and of the ground beside it, which the plaintiff says is a span wide and hers, and the defendant says is a span wide and his.

1. Plaintiff states that the merestone stood a span further west in her father's time and in her husband's, and that this thaw she found it a span east, with the spade-marks of the west strip run up to it. She asks the court for the span.

2. Defendant states that he has turned the west strip to the stone and no further, as his father did, and that if the stone walked, it walked by itself. He asks the court for the span.

3. Witness, Hob Tarrant, setter of stones for the parish. States that his grandfather set the stone. States that the old line between the strips was lost before the count and could not be found by cord or by eye, and that his grandfather found it by standing on the ground until he felt it. The justice asks what was felt. Witness states: the place where a man stops talking.

4. The brother states that two field-dead lie under the span, laid long ago, numbers entered, names not kept. By the Order's reckoning the span is theirs until the Count closes. The justice notes it and sets it aside.

5. At the trimming of the lamps the reeve counted the hall, as the circuit requires. He counted twelve.

6. The hall counted again, by the clerk. Twelve. The door is barred against the thaw-wind and the bar is down.

7. The twelfth is found standing in the aisle between the plaintiff's bench and the defendant's, where the flags are worn hollow. Of it I enter what I can. It stands the height of a tall man. It wears a robe the colour of the barn wall behind it, wherever it stands. It has a face. I have looked at it three times to enter it, and can enter only that it is turned toward the bench.

8. The justice orders it to give its name for the roll. (No answer.)

9. The brother names it the Hush. He states that it takes the names of things one at a time, and that the court should rise. I was taught the same as a child and enter it as true. The justice orders the sitting to continue. The roll stands at twelve. The twelfth line I have entered so: [ … ].

10. Plaintiff is asked to restate her claim in her own words, as the circuit requires. She states: the span is mine. Asked again, she states: the span is. She says it four times, and each time it ends in the same place, and the fourth time she puts her hand to her mouth.

11. Defendant restates. He states: the span is. He stops in the same place as the widow.

I find I cannot write the word either. I have tried it on a waste leaf. The pen stops before it the way a spade stops on a stone.

12. Entered, as the circuit requires all things lost at a sitting to be entered. Gone from this hall: mine; hers; his; ours; own. The clerk has gone back over the minutes above and finds them standing. They are written there. They cannot be brought down this far.

13. Witness Tarrant recalled. Asked where the old line ran, states: here, and points into the aisle. The aisle runs east to west. The strips lie a league off, north of the barn. I enter that the witness pointed true.

14. The justice asks the plaintiff to give the name, for the restatement. (No answer.) The name is at the head of these leaves. I read it there. I cannot bring it down. From here the parties are the widow and the carter.

15. The justice orders that nothing at this sitting be lost. I enter the pauses now, since the sitting has gone into them.

Between the justice's question and the widow's answer: a pause, the length of the span.

In the pause after the carter's oath: the father turning the west strip, and the grandfather of the witness standing on the ground with eyes shut, and the line under both, which nobody made. It was there to be read, plainer than anything said tonight.

In the pause after the brother's prayer: the two under the span, with the names that were not kept. Kept.

16. The brother counts the hall aloud to close the roll. He counts one, and two, and three, and the wait after three, and stays in the wait. (No answer.)

17. The lamps trimmed the second time. The lantern-tender cut the wicks and did not light the new ones. Nothing in the barn went dark.

18. The justice confers with the bench. The bench is the justice. (No answer.)

19. **Judgment.** The justice gave judgment. I enter the words, which were few, and I enter that they came from the justice and from nowhere else. The judgment itself was in the spaces between them. I enter it in the order the words came, as the circuit requires.

The court finds that the stone stands where it was set.

The court finds that the span was held by neither party, and by neither father, and by no one before the count.

The court finds that the span is held, and has been held always, as every line between one thing and the next is held.

The court finds for the span.

The east strip and the west strip go to the span, to the walls of both holdings. The holdings go to the span. The widow and the carter go to the span. The two under it are there already.

Costs to be paid at the bar tonight, by every person on the roll, in names.

The court rises.

20. Costs. The widow paid. The carter paid. The witnesses, the four, paid. The brother paid, and the number with the name. The reeve paid. The lantern-tender paid. The justice paid from the bench and did not come down.

Where each one stood at the bar there is a little glass on the flags, and a little dust. I have swept none of it. The roll stands at two. The twelfth stands in the aisle, between where the benches are, and the face is turned toward the clerk's table.

21. The court has risen. I enter that it is standing.

22. The clerk is to pay last, and enter the payment. The name is at the head of these leaves. I read it there. I am to bring it down to the bar and give it. I enter the clerk's costs as paid in full, in the sum of

*(Below, in another hand:)*

Book found on the clerk's table at first grey, open at this leaf, ink dry. Barn barred from within. Glass on the flags at the bar in eleven places, in a row, and the aisle swept clean between them. Walked out to the strips. Both merestones stand, a span apart. The span is turned by nobody. East of it and west of it, ground to the road, and nothing on it. The parish book enters no holdings there and no holders.

I have entered the sitting as concluded. The judgment I have not entered. I have read the leaf through four times and find only the justice's words on it, and they say nothing that a court would rule.
