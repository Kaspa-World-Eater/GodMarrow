# The Confession of the Count-breaker Taken at Kell Cut

*Copied from a tallier's leaf kept loose in the sentence-book of the lesser ossuary at Kell Cut; the brother who let me copy it would not let me copy the facing leaf, and turned it face down on the board while I worked.*

Taken at the foot of the Third Stair of Kell Cut, before two talliers of this ossuary, in the eleven hundred and twelfth year of the Count, root five. The confessor is of the lowlands, of the barrow-ground at Sallow, and has no number. The confession is set down as given. Where the confessor's figures are wrong, the true figure is written beside them in the margin, as the Rule requires, so that no false number goes into the book uncorrected.

I unmade your counts. I will say where and how many, and you will write your numbers next to mine, and that is the whole of the trouble between us.

I began with cords. On the low road below the white stones I found a novice asleep against the yoke, his dead teacher standing over him in the dark with its jaw on its breastbone. I took the cord from his wrist while he slept, two hundred and six knots, and I cut it between every knot with a salt-knife, and threw the pieces into the ash on both sides of the road. The teacher on the yoke sat down. It sat down like an old man sits, by halves. I saw it.

[First tallier: The novice is known. His cord was found whole on his wrist at morning. His teacher was standing.]

[Second tallier: The cord was found whole. It was counted at two hundred and seven knots.]

After that I cut the barrow-cords at Sallow, the ones your counters tied to the lid-stakes when they came down the low road with the knights of the Kostyak and counted our dead where they lay. Fourteen cords. The lids at Sallow had been lifting since my mother's time. For one winter after I cut them, they sank again from the middle, as lids should.

[First tallier: The Order keeps no count of lowland barrows by cord. Nothing was tied at Sallow. There were nineteen.]

This is what we hold, and I held it before I had a word for it. Every count is a chain. A thing that is counted is held where the number is, and a dead thing held is a dead thing that cannot lie down. Before your brothers came onto the ash with their cords and their chalk, our dead went into the barrow and the lid sank, and that was all. The Order says a number outlasts a name. We say so too. That is the charge against you. A name is let go when the last one who said it is dead. A number is cut in stone, and the stone goes on saying it, and the bone in the niche goes on hearing it, and stands.

My father was laid in the barrow at Sallow in the spring of the low water. In the summer your counters came with a tallier and two knights and stood at the barrow-mouth, and the tallier said figures with his eyes shut for the length of a morning, and at noon my father came up out of the barrow with the others, with the earth still on him, and walked up the low road behind the plate. I followed him to the white stones and no farther. I was a long time getting farther.

That is why. You asked why, and I have told you.

I came into Kell Cut by the sweepers' shaft in the dark of the moon, nine times. I went up the Third Stair to the Third Wall, where the lowland dead stand. I found my father by his feet, which I knew. Over his niche was a name that was not his and a number. I scraped them to the stone with a flint, and then the next niche, and the next, the whole run of the wall from the stair-foot, forty niches. I scraped until the grit was in my teeth.

[First tallier: Thirty-eight niches scraped. Two were scraped by another hand, earlier, with a finer tool. Root two.]

[Second tallier: Forty-one.]

When the grey came down the shaft I looked along the wall. Nine of them had lain down. They had folded at the knee and gone down into their niches the way the living sleep in a cart, with their heads against the stone. My father was the third. I touched his foot and it was cold. The others on that wall are warm, as the ridge is warm. I count nine. I know I am counting. I could not help it.

[First tallier: The Third Wall was counted at the Feast of the Laying following, every frame upright and facing the stair. The wall stood one over its count, which is the Mother's gift.]

[Second tallier: Upright. I set my lamp on each. The third from the stair-foot stands with his chin up. His feet are clean.]

Then I did the worst thing, and I did it on purpose. At the far end of the wall there is a niche cut and never filled. I have seen that in every ossuary of the ridge: one niche at the end of the run, empty, with the chisel-marks still sharp. I cut a number over it. I cut it as you cut them, in your figures, which I had learned off the wall in the dark by feeling. I meant that number to be a false notch, a chain with nothing on the end of it, so that the wall's count would come out wrong and stay wrong, and a count that is wrong cannot hold.

I came back at the next dark of the moon. There was a frame in the empty niche, standing, facing the stair. It had not been washed. There was no chrism on it, no shine at the joints. It was whole to the eye at a glance, and I counted it by touch, which is the only way I know how, and I had two hundred and six, and then I counted again and had two hundred and seven. It stood under the number I had cut. It had no name over it because I had cut none.

[First tallier: The niche at the end of the Third Wall is laid and entered. See the book.]

[Second tallier: See the book. The entry is in no hand of this ossuary.]

There was one more thing that night, and you will not write it. There was someone else on the stair. Tall, and no brother, with no lamp, going down the Third Wall at the pace of a man reading. He stopped at each niche and put one finger to the stone where the number goes. Where I had scraped, he stopped longest. At the end of the wall he stopped at my niche, the new one, and stood there a while, and then he turned and looked down the stair at me as if I were the next figure in the row.

[Not written.]

I have nothing more to unmake. I am told I will be laid here. I know the Rule: washing, chrism joint by joint, niche, name, number. Keep the name. I have not used it since Sallow and it is no good to anyone. But the number you will cut, I have to know it before you cut it. A chain that is told to me is a chain I can pull against. I ask the talliers who keep the open counts in their heads, who have every figure of this ossuary behind their eyes from the stair-foot up: you have already counted me. I know you have. You counted me the night I came up the shaft the first time, when I passed under the lintel and the lintel took a notch.

So tell me. Which wall. How far from the stair-foot. What number will you give me?

[Given: the Third Wall, the niche at the end, as cut. Root
