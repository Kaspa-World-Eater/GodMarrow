# The Charter of the Wick-Binders of Ennow under the Hill

*Found rolled tight inside the dark lantern at the ninth stone of the Ennow road, under a wick burned down to the collar; the glass was whole, and the back of the leaf is in a second hand.*

**Of the founding, to be read entire at every binding.**

Before there were lanterns on the roads of the lowlands, a soul at its leaving went out into the dark, and what waits at the edge of the road took it before any god could, and the dying knew it and died afraid. In the winter the Mere froze to the bottom, Hesse Wran, chandler of Ennow, sat up with his son, who was dying of the cough, and at the lamp-hour a gentleman came down the hill road and knocked.

He was dressed well for the cold and carried no lamp. He asked to come in, and was let in. He would take nothing to eat. He looked at the boy, and at the candles on the shelf, and he laughed, softly, and went on laughing at whiles through all that followed, as a man laughs at a thing only he has heard.

He showed Hesse the craft that night, at the boy's bed, with the chandler's own cord and a jar of Orrin Pell's, the glazier across the lane. He showed how the boy must ask, and how the name is laid in the twist, and how the glass is shut. When the jar cut Hesse's hand, the gentleman took the hand and put it to his mouth, as one stanches a child's cut, and gave it back closed. The boy asked. The boy was bound. The jar burned on the sill until morning with a small flame that stood straight and gave no smoke, and in the morning the gentleman was gone up the hill road, and the snow on the road showed only his coming down.

He took no fee. He asked one thing: that it be told, and told as it was, the hill and the laughing and all. Hesse and Orrin and five others swore the craft at the next thaw. This is that telling. It is not to be shortened.

**The articles of the craft, as sworn by the seven.**

*The first, of who may be bound.* None is bound but at their own asking. The asking is made three times: once well, once ill, and once at the last breath, with two of the craft present who are not kin. Kin may not ask for the dying. A binder may not ask for them, and may not put the question. A child is not bound. One who cannot speak at the last asks with a hand on the glass; if the hand will not go to the glass, there is no binding. A soul bound unasked does not burn clean. The seven saw it once. It is entered that they saw it, and the road that lantern was hung on is not walked.

*The second, of the wick.* The wick is the name. The binder twists the cord while the dying say their own name into it, once, low, and the cord is twisted shut on the last sound of it. After that the name is said by no mouth. A lantern is called by its stone: the ninth of the Ennow road, the fourth of the Mere bank. A wick whose name is spoken near it gutters, and the craft holds that this is the bound one turning to answer. When the wick is burned to the collar the lantern is dark and is not lit again. Where the bound one goes then, the craft has no article.

*The third, of the glass.* The glass is blown whole by one of the craft, without seam or bubble, and is never silvered. No glass that has held a face is to be used: no window, no cup, no reading-glass of the Crone's circles. A crack lets in the Mouths. A cracked lantern is broken out by daylight at the stone where it hangs, and the wick is buried at the foot of the stone, and the stone keeps its number and has no light.

*The fourth, of the price.* A lantern carried follows whoever carries it, and the flame takes its price of that one, a little at each falling. Each time the flame falls low, as a flame does in wind or where the road turns, something goes out of the bearer into the light. What goes, the craft has never agreed. Hesse said warmth. Orrin said years. The eldest of the seven said it was what the bound one gave at the binding, taken slower. Whatever is said, the bearer is told before the lantern is put in their hand. A lantern hung at a stone follows no one. That is the reason for the stones.

*The fifth, of the binder.* A binder does not bind themself, and asks of the craft at their end like any other. A binder's wick is twisted by the youngest of the craft present. The roll of every binding is kept at the hall in Ennow, by stone and by year, and never by name.

*The sixth, of the reading.* The founding is read entire at every binding, before the first asking, so that the dying hear how the craft came to us. Where there is not time for both the reading and the binding, it is not the reading that is left out.

**The article added at Hobbet's ford, in the year the hall at Ennow was burned.**

*The seventh.* The hall at Ennow is burned, and the roll in it, and eleven of the craft in it, by the circles that cover glass, who came at the lamp-hour with their faces wrapped and said that a soul kept from its road is a theft from the loom, and broke every lantern on the Ennow road from the first stone to the eighth. They did not break the ninth. They stood at it a long while and went back.

Hereafter the craft keeps no hall, no roll and no mark. A binder asked their trade is a lamp-man. The articles are kept in the mouth, and this leaf in the oldest lantern on the road, where no circle will open a lit glass. A binder taken by the circles is to ask, before the fire, three times, aloud, in any words; and those of the craft not taken are to be among the crowd, and if they cannot bind, they are to have heard the asking.

The roll that burned held four hundred and nine bindings. The lantern-men of the lowlands keep more lanterns than that between Ennow and the Mere. Their wicks are trimmed, and their glass is whole, and they burn. No binder has an article for this. None is to be written.

*On the back, in another hand.*

I was the youngest at the burning, and twisted Orrin Pell's wick, the grandson's, and he told me this first, because he said the seven had agreed it should not go into the charter and should not be lost either.

When the gentleman of the hill was going out of Hesse's door in the morning, Hesse asked him what the joke was, since he had laughed all night. He said there were two. He said the first was the price, and that we would learn it at the fallings, and that it was the better of the two. He said the second was the head of the charter. Hesse did not understand him, and wrote the head as he had been asked, and read it at every binding of his life, and so have we all. I have read it at sixty-one since the burning. I read it to Orrin.
