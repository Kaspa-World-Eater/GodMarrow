# The Log of the Thirty-First Crossing of the White Pan

*Salt-stiff leaves sewn into a hauler's harness-pad, sold to me at Saltgate as a pad. The last leaf is in a second hand.*

Log of the salt road from the Ossk Wells to Saltgate, over the White Pan, kept by the master. This is my thirty-first crossing and my third as master. At the traces: twenty-four haulers. Sledges: nine. Six of salt, two of water, one of our dead going home to be laid. Water: forty skins.

The rules, as Master Uhl gave them to me and his master gave them to him, entered at the head of every log and read at the first fire:

Cross its track straight, at the narrowest place. Never walk in it or along it, though it is the smoothest going on the Pan.

Leave a block of salt at the crossing, with the caravan's sign cut in it. Do not look at the old blocks.

Count the traces before the crossing and after.

Camp a full day's pull from any place where it has lain down.

If it comes in the dark, lie down where you are with your face in the salt, and stay down. Whoever runs has only its way to run in.

Do not give it a name. Every caravan has put names on it and none of them has held. On the road we say the Weight, because a rule needs something to say.

**First day.** Ossk Wells to the cairn of Three Blocks. Eleven pulls. Water: thirty-eight. Crust firm and white.

**Second day.** Ten pulls. Water: thirty-five. A keeper of the Myriad rides with the dead sledge for her passage. She says she has crossed four times looking for what small god rides the Weight, as one rides every stone and ford, and has found nothing riding it, and that this is the worst thing she knows of. The shrine people frighten themselves with their tallies of little gods. The salt has no god in it, and we haul it.

**Fourth day.** Nine pulls. Water: thirty. At the hour after noon a hill on the east edge where there was no hill at the fourth pull. By evening it had moved the width of my thumb held out at arm's length. The keeper said it was the size of the ridge above Ossk. I enter it as the size of a hill. I will not measure it nearer.

**Fifth day.** Eight pulls, to the track. Water: twenty-seven.

The track runs north and south. I paced its narrow at four hundred and twelve. Across all of that the crust is pressed down into a glaze as hard and smooth as a fired bowl, a hand lower than the Pan on either side, so the edge stands up like a step. In the glaze are the shapes of what lay under the weight when it came down: a sledge-runner, a rib longer than our longest sledge, the curve of a back, a jar on its side. None of them is a foot. And down the middle, as far as the eye goes, small round hollows each the size of a drinking bowl, in rows of seven, close as stitches.

Where the glaze is cracked, the ground under it is dark and wet. Nothing else on the Pan is wet. It is warm, and it smells as a laying-table smells when the washing is half done. On my first crossing I put my hand in a crack. Uhl struck it out and made me wash it in salt and then in water, a whole skin. The keeper says the Pan is a skin and the Weight eats what is under it. I enter that she said so.

The old blocks stand along the near edge, from the south of sight to the north of sight. Each has a caravan's sign. Most of the signs I do not know. Some are cut in strokes no caravan uses now. None has been moved, licked or broken. It takes no toll. We keep the rule.

**Sixth day.** Counted at the traces: twenty-four. Crossed at the narrow. Set our block. Counted: twenty-four. Hauled a full day's pull beyond and camped. Water: twenty-four.

**Seventh night.**

First the salt moved. Fine grains walking east on the crust with a hiss, the way sand goes off a struck drum.

Then the stars on the east side went out from the ground upward, row on row, until half the sky was gone.

Then the air. It came over the camp warm and wet for as long as it takes to drink a skin slowly, and drew back the same way, and the fire leaned toward it and then away. That was its breath. There was no other sound from it but that and a sound under the ground like a cart going over boards a long way off, which was its feet.

We lay down as the rule says. It came through the north end of the camp. I saw one leg of it by the light of our fire. It was as wide as the smithy at Saltgate, grey, its skin folded and cracked like a dry pond-bed, with salt crusted in the cracks. Dannet lay face down an arm's length from where it came down. It did not move aside for him and it did not come down on him. It came down on the two water sledges. It came down on the boy Ennick, who had stood up to run. He ran across its front, because there was nothing else to run to on the Pan. It did not turn its head for him. It did not lower itself. It did not stop.

At the distance of a long shout it lay down, and fed until near light. Tearing of the crust like sailcloth, and a swallowing, and the ground under us drawing in with each swallow.

Then it got up and went north, and the stars came back from the bottom.

**Eighth day.** Lost: both water sledges and twenty-two skins. One salt sledge broken. Ennick. Water: two skins in the camp, and eleven we had in the salt sledges against need. Thirteen, for four days and twenty-three.

It did not touch the dead sledge, which stood a stone's throw from where it lay. It took none of us who lay flat around it, and we were easy to take.

In the morning the keeper and I walked its new track one full pull north and one full pull south. It runs straight from the edge of sight to the edge of sight. It does not bend toward our fire. There is no place where it stood and looked, or set its weight to one side. Every hunting thing leaves a turn in its track, at the place where it chose. This track has no turn. Our camp lay across its line as a stone lies in a road, and it went over us as a road goes on. We were passed by.

I write it twice, because the haulers will want it read at the fire. We were passed by.

We found Ennick in the new glaze, pressed in among the other shapes, flat and whole, a hand under the surface, with the glaze over his face like still water. Two chisels broke on it. We set a block over him with our sign. Saltgate will enter him as lost on the Pan, as it enters all of them.

Beside him, at the same depth and facing the same way, is a hauler in harness of the old cut, straps crossed at the back, that Uhl told me of from his master's time. Beside that one is another. The glaze was made last night. I watched it made.

**Ninth day.** Seven pulls. Water: nine. The keeper walks with her face to the ground and does not say what she is looking for.
