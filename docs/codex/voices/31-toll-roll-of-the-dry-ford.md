# The Toll-Roll of the Dry Ford at Wenlow

*Found rolled in the toll-house strongbox at Wenlow, the box standing open on the stones of the old bed a long throw below the house; the roll was dry, and the box was full of cold water to the lid.*

Toll-roll of the ford at Wenlow, kept for the town by the keeper of the toll-house. Toll: one copper the foot, two the handcart, a heel of salt the load, the dead carried over free. The town says the toll is owed for the road, and the road is still here.

**Of the river.** It left in the spring of the year eleven hundred and one by the Order's count, the ninth Cutting by the shrine's. Osk Fenner, keeper before me, set down that it went in one night with no drought after, and the morning bed was dry stones with the weed lying flat on them, all of it pointing downstream, the way hair lies when a hand has passed over it. The shrine-woman of the ford said the Coldfoot had gone out on the breath, as all of them will, and that it went early because it was small and light. She set a paper figure for it on the step of the ford-shrine. Nothing has moved it, though the stones round it move.

The brothers of the Pale Order who cross here twice a year say no river has left, it is only not yet counted. A number never kept water in a bed. I hold with the shrine-woman. What came after she will not speak of.

**Of the second column.** Fenner began it the first summer. At its head he writes: *Those who would not pay the house, and paid him.* He gives the name once, as the shrine-woman gave it him, Nhar-Ghulim, and after that writes only *him*. I keep it as Fenner kept it: who, what they paid, which way they went, whether seen again.

---

**1101, high summer.** *House:* a salt-carter and his son, one handcart, two coppers and a heel of salt. *Him:* Berrit Ash, a tinker's widow of the Mere side. Paid: her wedding cord. Went: eastward, the way the river ran, which is along and never across. Seen again: no.

**1102, the thaw.** *House:* eleven foot, a dead woman carried over free. *Him:* a needle-pedlar. Paid: his pack; the needles made no sound falling. Went: across, forty paces of dry stone, and he was in the boat until the lamp-hour. Seen again: on the far bank that evening, asking which side he was on. His beard had grown a hand.

**1104, the long light.** *House:* nine foot. *Him:* two sisters of the Tarrow road, Marra and Lisse Hobbe. Paid: one of them paid; I could not see which, nor what. Went: downstream. Seen again: see the year nine.

**1106, the frost.** *House:* none; the road was shut with ash. *Him:* none. The boat came up to the house-step in the night and lay there till grey with the pole laid across it. I did not go out.

Fenner's hand stops at this leaf. The town says he went to his daughter at the Mere.

---

**Of the boat.** In my hand, Corra Dene, keeper after Fenner.

It lies on the dry stones of the bed. It is long and black, the planks lapped, the pole pale and longer than the boat. It is never where it was left. Each evening I set white stones at its prow and stern. By morning it lies a length up or a length down, or turned with its prow to the house. It leaves no track over the stones between.

The stones under it are wet, and the wet goes as far as the hull and no further. Lie flat with an ear to the bed and you hear water running the way the river ran. Once, the other way. I have put my hand in under the keel. It was cold to the wrist, and came out dry.

There is a mark cut in the prow. The shrine-woman would not read it. She said it was older than the Coldfoot and older than fords, and that he was taking fares at crossings before there were small gods in them to keep, and will take them when the last has gone out.

Of him I have seen: the pole, a hand at the top of the pole, a hood. When the boat moves he leans as a man leans against a current, and it goes over the stones at the pace of wading. He has never called to anyone. They go down to him.

---

**1107, the Cutting.** *House:* a brother of the Pale Order with his standing dead, the dead free, though they walked. *Him:* none. The brother counted the boat from this bank and said one. He counted it from the far bank and wrote the number down and did not tell me it.

**1108, the frost.** *Him:* a girl of twelve winters or near it. She stood in the lamp at the door to pay the house and had no copper, and went down to him. Paid: when she came back up the bed toward the boat, the lamp threw nothing behind her. Went: upstream, toward where the river came from, where no one goes. Seen again: no.

**1109, the thaw.** *Him:* none. *Seen again:* Lisse Hobbe, at the house door, older than her mother would be now, with her sister's face. She paid the house one copper to cross back the dry way and asked if Marra had come through. I have no Marra after the year four. I have no Lisse after it either.

**1110, the long light.** *Him:* Tobin Reede of Wenlow, cooper. Paid: one copper, the same as the house; he held it up to me as he went down so I would see it. It was taken. Went: across. Seen again: in the same hour, on this bank, coming down to the boat with the copper in his hand, holding it up to me. He is in Wenlow and coopers. His wife says he sleeps with his feet wet.

**1111, the Cutting.** *Him:* a purifier of the shrine, horned at the brow. Paid: she breathed into the boat, a long breath, longer than a chest holds. Went: down. I can set it no plainer. The boat went down the bed, and the bed went down with it. Seen again: at the next Cutting, standing in the dry bed at the ford, to the knees among the stones as one stands wading. The paper figure on the shrine step was turned to face her.

**1112, the frost.** *Him:* Osk Fenner. I write it because it is so. He came to the house-step at the lamp-hour, dry, his coat dry, with no year on him at all, and asked me the date. He says it is high summer of the year eleven hundred and one, and the river has just gone, and he has come to begin a column. He reads his own hand and does not know it. In the mornings he goes down to the bed and sets white stones at the boat, and they are where I set mine.

I did not write that line. It is my hand, the long-tailed *d* of my mother's teaching, and the ink is the house ink, and the leaf was dry when I found it. I have gone over what I have. The copper in the box is right to the copper. I remember the girl, and the brother counting, and Fenner.

I have stood an hour on the house-step trying to remember the river. I was a child on its bank eleven summers. I know it ran, because the roll says so. I know there was water.
