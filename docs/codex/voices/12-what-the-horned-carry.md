# What the Horned Carry

*A paper figure with its arms open, found standing on the lowest step of the Well-Shrine stair where the stair goes under the fen, a hand's depth down and dry to the touch. The boatman who lifted it out would not have it in the boat, so I held it the whole way across.*

## The front

I was folded in the Cutting of the keeper Ysse, and my birth-name went up in a paper figure the size of my thumb, and the name they gave me in its place I write here once, so that the Folded can scrape it: Ilke of the Ford-Stone.

I write with a nail, pressed hard, because ink runs and a nail does not. Whoever reads this will have it out of the water.

This is what I carry. I set it down so that the count is somewhere, since the cord has stopped being a count.

The breath of the miller at Seddle who held his brother under the race. I took it off the race in my first Cutting and gave it back to him by the rite, and he breathed it and died coughing it up, and it came out of him whole, the same breath, and I took it in again because there was no mouth left to give it to. Held since.

The breath off the pit in the fang-ring on the low road, where walkers step in and come apart. That breath has no maker. It comes up from under. Given back to no one. Held.

The breath of the fever-room at Brakemoor, nine beds. Some of it was theirs. Most of it came up through the boards. Held.

The breath that has sat in the bell at Corrow chapel since it began to ring flat. I could not tell whose. Held.

A breath I took from a child's mouth in the night that was not the child's breath. Held. The child is well. I asked after her twice.

Others. I tied a knot for each. I tied three hundred and eleven. The cord at my wrist has four hundred and two, and when I count it at night with my fingers it has more, and the knots I did not tie are tighter than mine and I cannot open them with my teeth, and my teeth are not where they were.

## The back

The Folded teach that every wrong breath has a mouth that made it, and that the purifier's office is to find the mouth and give it back. For some breath that is so. I gave back what I could for nineteen Cuttings, and I was good at it, and the elders wrote my name in the day-book for it.

But most of what I took in had no mouth. It came up out of the ground, out of the ford, out of the boards of the fever-room, out of the pit. A breath cannot be given back to the ground. The Folded hold it, and hold it, and call the holding a failure of room, and weep when the jaw begins to lengthen, and the elders send them first to the day-book and then to the stone that is turned to the wall.

We say the holding is the office. We say the long jaw is the shape a mouth takes when it has to stay shut a long time. The horns come in slow, like a child's teeth, and ache at night the same way, and come through the same way, a little blood on the cloth in the morning. I pressed my thumbs on them at first, to keep them under. Then I stopped.

The Cutting-Away was the old rite. Once a Cutting the year's wrong breath was folded into figures and given to the water, and the purifiers walked home light. The water kept it a hundred Cuttings and then gave it all back up through the fen in one season, and the purifiers who went down to meet it are the first of us. The Folded say the water failed. We say the water was never meant to keep it alone. Somebody has to stand in it and hold it shut. The water is only where we stand.

The Folded call this despair, and will not say our names, and fold our figures with no faces. I call it room. There is room down there.

The one who walks beside every purifier in the haze, turned the wrong way round, has gone down the stair in front of me. It has never gone in front before.

## The left arm

The water at the stair-foot is clear. It closed over my knees and my knees were dry. It closed over my mouth and I did not breathe it and did not need to. I am full already.

The floor below is dry. Dust lies on it. The dust does not lift when I walk.

The bell of the prayer-hall still hangs. Its tongue is drawn to one side and held there.

They are here. They stand in rows on the dry floor, horned, hands out in front a little and full, the way a woman carries a bowl to the brim. They are patient. None of them breathes out. Their cords lie on the floor behind them and go back up the stair, longer than the stair.

I know one. Fan-Bearer Oda, who folded me. She went down eleven Cuttings ago. Her horns have come round and closed under her jaw like a hand. She did not turn her head. They do not turn their heads. They all face one way.

There is a place in the third row with no one in it.

## The right arm

The Folded teach that the great one breathes out and we ride it to the end. Down here the breath goes the other way. The dust lies in lines toward the deep. Oda's hair lies forward over her face. The water draws in, slow, the way a sleeper draws in.

I do not know what is at the bottom. No one in the rows has said. It is breathing in.

My hand is large now. The point will not stay small. Fewer words.

## The edge

To the Folded.

You will come. The cord will tell you.

Come willing.

Do not burn this. Do not turn my stone.

Your knots are not your own. Count them.

There is room.

It is drawing in. I am going
