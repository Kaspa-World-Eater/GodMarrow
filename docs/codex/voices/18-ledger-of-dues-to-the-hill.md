# The Book of Dues of Collick, Owed to the House on the Hill

*These leaves were cut from the reeve's book of Collick under the Hill and sewn into the binding of a Pale Order tally-roll I bought at the Four Wents crossing; the brother who sold it had not noticed them, or had, and wanted them gone.*

[b]The dues of Collick to the House on the Hill, kept by the reeve, as they were kept by the reeve before, and owed in their seasons without remission.[/b]

[font_size=26][color=#c9974a]Thaw quarter[/color][/font_size]
[b]Tale-due.[/b] Owed by every child of the town in the spring of its seventh winter, to be told it by the eldest of its own hearth, at the lamp-hour, with the door barred and the shutter toward the hill left open the width of a hand. Told this quarter to nine. Told whole, the stair first and the gate last, and the child asked at the end which window is lit. Eight answered the window. The cooper's girl answered the door, and was told again the next night, and answered the window. Paid.

[b]Lamp-due.[/b] The lamp at the foot of the hill road to be kept burning every night of the quarter, its oil from the common store. Paid. The lamp is noted to have been found lit on the second morning of the quarter, before the lamp-man came to it, with the wick trimmed shorter than he trims it. He trims it shorter now.

[font_size=26][color=#c9974a]Long-light quarter[/color][/font_size]
[b]Place-due.[/b] At every board in Collick one place to be laid and left empty through the quarter: bowl turned down, stool pushed in, nobody to sit or to move it. Paid in every hearth by the count of the beadle, who goes in without knocking. The tanner's widow had set her bowl right way up. The beadle turned it down and entered her for it. She says it was the wind.

[b]Name-due.[/b] The House to be spoken of in every hearth once each night, in any words, and by no other name than the House. The reeve cannot count this due and enters it paid, as the reeve before did.

[font_size=26][color=#c9974a]Ash quarter[/color][/font_size]
[b]Hill-due.[/b] One to be sent up the hill road on the first night of the quarter, at the lamp-hour, carrying nothing, by the lot of the stones in the reeve's bowl. The lot fell this year to Ebbe Strand, carter, of the lower lane. He went up at the lamp-hour and the town watched from its doors, as is owed. He came down on the third morning, on his own feet, white to the lips, with the inside of his left elbow bound in a strip of clean linen that is woven finer than any loom in Collick weaves. He does not remember the road. He remembers a room that was warm and a great many people in it, standing, and all of them, he says, listening. He sleeps much. Paid.

[b]Grey brothers.[/b] Two brothers of the Pale Order came to count our yard and asked what was on the hill. The reeve told them a house, which is true, and they wrote it down. They count the dead and never what keeps the living, and the reeve has never known them be of use to a town.

[font_size=26][color=#c9974a]Dark quarter[/color][/font_size]
[b]Dark-due.[/b] On the longest night every lamp, candle, hearth and coal in Collick to be put out from the lamp-hour to the grey, the lamp at the foot of the hill road with them, and every soul of the town to sit up in the dark facing the hill, and be still, and hear. Paid by the whole town, save as below.

[font_size=26][color=#c9974a]Of the woman Tamsa, come from the fen country[/color][/font_size]
[b]Entered the Thaw quarter.[/b] Tamsa, a woman of the fens, with two children, took the empty cottage at the top of the lower lane, the one whose shutter faces the hill. She keeps a fen shrine-stone over the door and puts water on it. Owes all dues from her coming.

[b]Long-light.[/b] Her younger is seven winters. The tale-due was told to it by the beadle's wife in Tamsa's stead, Tamsa having laughed at the telling of it in the yard and said she had heard worse of a ford. The child answered the window. Paid, by the beadle's wife. Tamsa did not lay the place-due. The beadle laid it for her at her board, with her own bowl, and entered it paid.

[b]Ash.[/b] She went to see Ebbe Strand on the third morning, and looked at his arm, and said in the lane that any fen-woman knows a leech-cut when she sees one, and that a man could be bled for less in her country and nobody would make a tale of it. Eleven heard her. The reeve has entered their names.

[b]Dark quarter, the longest night.[/b] Tamsa's lamp lit, in the window of the shutter that faces the hill, from the lamp-hour. She sat by it. She had told the lane she would.

[b]What the town owed for it.[/b] The lane did as follows, and the reeve enters it as dues paid on her behalf, the lane having paid them: the place-due laid for Tamsa at every board in the lower lane, though she lives, her bowl turned down at eleven boards. Her name not said in the lane from the longest night to the thaw. Her children told the tale again, both, and a third time, by every eldest in the lane in turn, with her shutter open the width of a hand. Her water left off the shrine-stone; it is dry. Nobody has touched her. The reeve forbade it, and nobody needed forbidding.

[b]The thaw following.[/b] She put her lamp out herself, on the ninth night, and has not lit it since at any hour. She asked the beadle's wife which window it is that is lit. She keeps the place-due, and keeps it twice: a bowl turned down at her own board, and another on the step outside her door, on the side toward the hill. No due requires the second bowl. The reeve has not told her to stop.

[font_size=26][color=#c9974a]In the reeve's hand, smaller, on the inside of the back board[/color][/font_size]
I have to write this down because I have begun to lose it.

This thaw I sat with my grandson for his tale-due and I could not find the middle. I had the stair, and I had the gate, and between them there is a thing that is said, and I had told it to his mother and to two others, and it was not there. I said the gate twice and he did not know the difference. His grandmother finished it for me from the door.

Then the place-due. I laid it and I could not say whose place it was. I have laid it fifty-one years. I think it was always somebody's.

I am afraid. I have not been afraid of the hill since I was seven, because I have been afraid of it every hour since, and a man does not feel the coat he never takes off. Now there are hours it is off me. In those hours I am afraid of what comes to a town whose reeve forgets the dues. I do not know what that is. Nobody has written it here. I have read back to the first leaf.

And I am glad. I set that down plain, so it is owed somewhere. Last night I could not remember for a while what the lamp at the foot of the road was for, and I slept until the grey, and in the morning it was out, and nobody had put it out, and I stood in the lane in the cold and I was glad, and I do not know who I am glad for.
