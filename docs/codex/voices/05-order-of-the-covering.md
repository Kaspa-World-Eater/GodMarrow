# The Order of the Covering of Glass at a Death

*Copied from a book bound in grey felt that hung on a nail inside the door of an empty lodge on the cold-lakes road. One corner of the felt had been cut away, square, the size of an eyelid, and the margins carry a second hand, which I copied where it stood.*

**Of the beginning, said by the circle-keeper before the first glass.**

Before her there was the dry wall. Nothing was held to anything. The Veiled Crone drew the first thread out of the nothing, and the drawing was the first going-on, and she tied the first knot, and the knot is the world. Every life is a length of that one thread. When a length is spent she draws it back through the eye of her loom and weaves it again into the cloth,

This is said over the novice on the night her thread is cast on. It is said again over every dead one, so the dead hear it last and go the way they are told.

The loom is there. The keeper says it before each step and the house says it after her. Since the loose winter it is said twice at each step, and the second time louder. Saying it is the work.

**Of the coverer.**

The coverer is sent for when the breath stops: before the washers, before the bearers. She brings the felt, grey, uncut, unhemmed, rolled on a spindle; a needle; a cord of her own length. Every glass in a house of death is a door. A thread that is loose in the house goes looking for its own face, and where it finds its face it stops, and a thread that stops is a tangle in the cloth. The coverer shuts the doors so that the only way out of the house is the eye.

**The first step. At the sill.**

The coverer stands on the sill and says *Veiled Crone*, and the house answers, *The loom is there.* She does not cross until it answers. If the house is silent she says the name twice more. If it is silent still, she covers it alone and writes it in the book as a silent house.

**The second step. The eyes of the dead.**

The eyes are shut with the thumb. An eye is wet glass, and the dead look out of it longest. Two squares of felt are laid on the lids, and the coverer says *Weaver*.

*Rubric, since the loose winter:* each square is stitched to its lid with one stitch, and the stitch is tied off. In the loose winter the lids came up under the felt. This is the reason. It is enough reason.

**The third step. Hanging glass.**

Every glass that hangs: the mirror, the polished bronze, the window that looks back at night. Felt over each, the corners tied behind. At each the coverer says *Grandmother Spindle*, and counts aloud, and the count goes in the book.

**The fourth step. Lying glass.**

Water lies as glass lies. The bowl is emptied onto the ash outside the door. The bucket is emptied. Knives are laid edge-down, pans turned over. At each the coverer says *Knot*.

**The fifth step. The glass in the living.**

The household keep their eyes down from this step until the drawing is done. A child who will not is held with her face in the coverer's skirt. The name said here is *Tangle*, and it is said for the thread that would tangle if a living eye and a dead one met.

**The sixth step. The drawing.**

The lantern is pinched. The household sit in a ring about the dead, and the keeper begins the names and goes round: *Veiled Crone, Weaver, Grandmother Spindle, Knot, Tangle*, and round again, from dusk to grey. At the end of every round she says the sixth name, the Hollow, which is said with the mouth shut, down in the throat, so the name goes into the chest and stays. The Hollow is the eye of the loom. The dead go through it.

In the grey the keeper lays her hand flat on the dead one's breast and feels for the pull. The old book says: *she will feel the thread drawn, as thread is drawn through the closed fingers of a spinner.* When she has felt it she says *drawn*, and the house writes *drawn*.

*Rubric, since the loose winter:* the keeper says *drawn* at grey. The house writes *drawn*. The keeper is not asked what she felt and does not say.

**The seventh step. The taking-down.**

The felt stays up nine nights. On the ninth dusk the coverer takes it down in the order it went up, and the squares on the eyes last.

*Rubric, since the loose winter:* the felt stays up. Where a house cannot spare it, the coverer leaves her own.

**Rubrics added since the loose winter, to be read at every lodge at the casting-on and at the Folding.**

*Of uncovered glass.* Glass found uncovered in a house of death is broken in the yard by the coverer, and the pieces burned in the lantern-fire until they run and show nothing. If a face is in the glass, and the face is the dead one's, the coverer lays felt over it before she breaks it and does not look under the felt. It will say her name. It will ask which way. She is not to answer which way.

*Of the Unveiled at the door.* They come with the kept on their backs in glass, and they come to widows. The door is not opened to them. The keeper stands behind it and says the names through the wood, the whole round, and the Hollow last, until they go. A widow who has paid them is put out of the ring for one winter.

*Of the Unveiled who come asking to come home.* One question is put to her through the door: where is your felt. If she says she burned it, she is turned away. If she has brought it, she is let into the yard and no farther, and she covers her own glass there, every face in it, one by one, while the circle says the names, and at each face she says *The loom is there.* If she cannot say it at one face, she is turned away with that glass. If she asks to be shown the eye, she is told the eye is the Hollow, and the Hollow is said with the mouth shut, and there is nothing to be shown.

*Of the eldest who looked into the eye.* Her name is cut from this book and from every book of the circle,. Where she is spoken of she is [ … ]. What she said is not written here and is not to be said back.

**Of the end, said by the circle-keeper when the felt comes down.**

The loose winter was a slack in the weave. The dead came home up the valley because the houses of the valley had left their glass bare, and each thread came home to its own face and stood there asking, as threads will that are let stop. This is all it was.

One winter the cloth is finished. Every length that ever went through the eye is woven in, and the Veiled Crone ties off the last thread at the last knot, and folds the cloth, and lays it by. Nothing moves in it again. The candle does not burn down. We are in the fold, and she sits by it with her hands in her lap, and she is there, and the loom is there, and the eye is there. The keeper says it three times. The house says it three times after her.
