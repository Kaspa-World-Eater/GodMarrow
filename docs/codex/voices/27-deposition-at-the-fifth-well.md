# Deposition of the Last Witness at the Fifth Well

*Found folded small in the ring of the cap of the Fifth Well at Nine Stairs, bound in with a knotted cord that I did not count. The last statement is in a hand the leaf itself says its deponent did not have.*

Taken under the Eighth Stair at the requirement of the Wardens in Agreement, concerning the office of the Kneeling, which the old rolls write Oss'khalath and the lowlands call Old Upright. Deponent: the Brother of the Well entered as Four Hundred and Eleven, root six, who has drawn marrow at the Second and the Seventh Wells for fifty-one years and draws now at the Seventh only. He does not write. The tallier writes as he speaks and reads each statement back, and he sets his thumb in chrism to each one that is his.

**1.** I am the last brother living who stood at the Fifth Well when the cap was lifted. Nine stood at the lip that night: the Warden of the Fifth Stair, six Brothers of the Well, the tallier of the Kneeling, and the one being sent. The Warden is laid. The six are laid; I drew the marrow for four of their Layings. The tallier is laid. The one sent is not laid, and I am told I may not give his number, because it is not read.

**2.** The Order teaches that the office is filled and emptied and filled, and that a brother goes down only when the one before him has closed. We were told at sundown that the one before had closed. I asked how it was known. I was told the marrow in the Second Well had gone still in the bucket, and that this is the sign. I have drawn at the Second for fifty-one years. It goes still in the bucket every winter.

**3.** The cap is a disc of bone the width of a grave, sealed at the rim with chrism and wax. We cut the seal with the well-knives and six of us lifted. It came up as a lid comes off a bowl. A cap of that width is cut to weigh what six brothers can barely raise. The underside was wet, and warm, as the ground is warm.

**4.** The Order teaches that the stair inside the well was cut for one going down to kneel and never for one coming up, and that this is why its steps are too deep for a man's leg. I saw eleven steps of it by the lantern before the light gave out. The steps are deep. They are also worn. Each tread is hollowed in the middle as the Second Stair is hollowed by every brother who has climbed it. Eleven men going down once each do not hollow a stair. On the top step lay white dust, fresh, and the marks of a chisel with their edges still sharp. No mason had been sent down. The cap had been sealed since before I had my number.

**5.** The one sent laid his name in a niche at sundown. His cord he kept on him. By custom the cord of the one sent is tied at its crown-knot to the ring of the cap and paid out after him, so the brothers at the lip may tell by its running how far down he has gone. I tied it. Mine were the steadiest hands that night. I was young.

**6.** He went down on his hands, facing the stair, as the Order climbs every old stair. He took no lantern. I paid the cord, and told the knots through my fingers as they ran: the crown, the bones of the face, the jaw, the neck. At the seventh bone of the neck it stopped, and I thought he had lost his footing. Then I understood he had come to the bottom and was kneeling. The Fifth is deeper than that. I have let a bucket down the Second on a longer cord.

**7.** The Order teaches that the Mother lays the one before with her own hands, and that no brother has seen it. I set down what I saw, and I do not say whether it was the laying. Out of the dark below the lantern's reach there came up a pair of hands, pressed together tip to tip, as a brother holds his hands at the Laying. Beneath them came another pair, pressed to the first. Then more. They rose the way marrow rises in a well when the bucket goes in, slowly and all together. They were bone, and the joints were bright with chrism, and the chrism was wet. I counted fingers, because I am of the Order and I count. I came past thirty and lost my place, and began again, and lost it at the same finger.

**8.** The hands opened, easily, as the cap had come up. The one sent went down between them. They closed. The cord ran again, and ran to its end, and drew tight on the ring.

**9.** Then the counting. The Order teaches that the office counts whoever comes, crown to sole, each number laid on its bone, and then says one of two words. I heard it. The voice is slow, slower than any tallier, with a breath between numbers long enough to say the short prayer in. It counted to two hundred and six. Two hundred and six is eight, and eight is open, and I waited for the word.

**10.** It counted two hundred and seven. It counted two hundred and eight. The tallier of the Kneeling began to count along under his breath, and the Warden put a hand over his mouth. The voice went on. We stood at the lip while the lantern was trimmed twice. I do not give the figure it came to. The tallier asked us that night never to give it, and he is laid, and I keep what the laid have asked me to keep.

**11.** It said one thing that night that was no part of the count, after the counting stopped, and it said it upward, toward the lip. It said my number. Four hundred and eleven. Once, at the same pace, as though it were the next figure.

**12.** The Warden ordered the cap set, and we set it. Before the seal I cut the cord at the ring, as the custom is, and drew up what hung above the cut. It should have been a hand's length of cord with the crown-knot on it. I drew up the whole cord.

**13.** I counted the knots that night on the floor of the Fifth Stair by the Warden's lantern: two hundred and seven. The Warden counted two hundred and eight. The tallier counted, and stood, and went down to his wall without giving his figure. The knots past the two hundred and sixth are tied tip to tip, in a way no novice is taught. The cord was warm.

**14.** The cord was given me to keep, as the one who tied it. I have it. I have counted it at every Feast of the Laying for fifty-one years, and at every Feast it stands higher than at the Feast before. When the walls do this the Order calls it the Mother's gift. I do not know what the Order calls it in a cord. I have not asked.

**15.** The Brothers of the Well do not draw at the Fifth, and the Rule does not forbid a brother to kneel at the cap. I have knelt there at every Feast with my ear to the seal, and every Feast I have heard it: the slow voice, counting, a breath between each number. I did not tell the Wardens before today because no Warden asked me. For fifty years it counted upward. At the last Feast it was counting down.
