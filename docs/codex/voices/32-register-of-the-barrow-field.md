# The Register of the Barrow-Field at Lidding

*Taken from a box under the spade-rack in the sexton's hut at Lidding, on the old road where it climbs out of the low country; the rack was empty, and the last two entries are in a hand that forms its numbers better than its words.*

Register of the barrow-field at Lidding, beside the old road, kept by Orrin Sedge, sexton, who had the spade from his father, who had it from his. The field holds four hundred and some of ours under lids, and the old barrows at the top besides, whose dead nobody here laid. Wage from the town: a copper for each one put back, paid gladly or late. The count is the Order's count, because the town reckons wages by it.

The dead walk here because something is still feared here. My father said it was the road. The brother of the Pale Order who comes at the Laying says the Mother's count is open and the dead stand up to be counted, and then he goes back up the ridge before dark. I have not seen the Mother in this field. I have seen the road.

**The work, as my father gave it.** Meet a risen from behind. The hook at the back of the knee. Face down, always, so that if it rises again it rises into the ground. A flat stone between the shoulders. The lid back over, pressed from the edges in, because every lid sinks from the middle. Say its name if it is known, and the field's name if not.

**Helpers this season.** Hew Cotter, hook and spade. Tamsin Wold, lantern and stones.

---

**Night the first.** Up: three, from the low rows. Put back: three. All three walked to the road and stood on the verge with their faces turned up the road. They do not cross it. I have never seen one cross it.

**Night the fourth.** Up: five. Put back: five. One was Mother Arn, laid in the spring, with the grey shawl on her that her daughter wears. Her daughter has the shawl. I went down and looked.

**Night the ninth.** Up: eight. Put back: eight. One of the old barrows opened from under, the lid lifted at the middle. What came out was older than ours, brown through like a root, and stood at the verge longest. The stone did not hold it the first time. Paid six of eight.

**Night the fifteenth.** Up: eleven. Put back: eleven. Tamsin's lantern went out at the verge and would not light again until she had stepped back off it.

**Night the twentieth.** Up: fourteen. Put back: twelve. Two stood on the verge until grey and would not take the hook, and at full light they lay down of themselves, on their backs. That is wrong. Turned them. Wenna, the lamp-tender's girl from the road-post, was at the verge in the grey, watching. Seven winters. Sent her home. She said they were waiting for somebody to come up.

**Night the twenty-sixth.** Up: nineteen. Put back: nineteen. Hew has the hook-burn across both palms. The season's count stands at one hundred and sixty-nine, more than my father's worst.

**Night the twenty-ninth.** Up: twenty-two. All of them on the verge in one line, faces up the road. We worked from the low end. Wenna at the road-post with the lamp; I had sent her home twice.

A little before grey a man came up the road from below. I write only what I saw. Hooded. Tall, and thin the way a staff is thin. A staff, set down every second step. He walked up. He did not stop, and he did not turn his head, and the lamp on the road-post did not lean to him or away. I was at the hut door. As he drew even with each of the risen on the verge it lay down, one after the next, the way stakes go over in a row, and he did not look at them. Then he was above the field. Then he was gone up the road, and I heard the staff a long while after I could not see him.

**The same night, at grey.** Twenty-two on the verge, on their backs, hands at their sides, straight, as I lay them and better. Turned them face down. They were light to lift. Put back: twenty-two.

Also lying: Hew Cotter in the low rows, the hook by his hand. Tamsin Wold on the verge among the risen, her lantern standing upright beside her, lit. Wenna at the foot of the road-post. On their backs, hands at their sides. No mark on any of them. They had the warmth of the ground and no more. Their faces were easy. I have looked at a great many faces in this field and I had not seen one easy.

**Night the thirtieth.** Laid Hew and Tamsin in the high row, face up, as the living are laid when they die. The lamp-tender would not give me Wenna. She is laid at the road-post where she lay, and he sits by her with the lamp. Up: none.

**Nights the thirty-first to the fortieth.** Up: none. Up: none. Up: none, each night. The lids in the low rows have sunk back flush with the turf. The old barrow that opened has closed at the crown with nobody's hand on it. The town pays nothing, there being nothing to put back. The town sleeps. From the hut I can hear it sleeping, which I never could.

**Night the forty-first.** The brother of the Order came out of season and counted the field twice, and it came out the same both times. He said no field had ever done that to him, and he went back up the ridge before dark with his mouth still moving. A reader of the Crone's circle passed on the road the same day and said the field had been sent on, all of it, and my two and the child with it. That is loom talk. There is no loom in this field. There are lids.

**Of whether to wish him back.** I set it here because the register is the only thing I write in.

In twenty-nine nights, with two helpers and one lantern, I put back one hundred and ninety-one, and some of them twice. In the time it takes to walk past a field he put back twenty-two and every lid in it, and the old barrows, and he did not stop to do it. He took three. I cannot see that he chose them. Hew was at the work. Tamsin was at the work. The child was watching. I was at the hut door, and he took nothing of me, and I do not know why I am left.

If he came up the road every season, the field would lie down every season, and some would lie down with it. Three, or thirty, or the town. If he does not come, the dead will stand again, because whatever is feared here is still feared, and more now. What Lidding fears now is the road. They will not walk it after the lamp-hour. They send their dead up to me by day.

I will not wish him back. I have not wished him away. I have cut a flat stone and set it at the road-post, as for a risen, and I do not know which way to lay it.

**Night the fifty-second.** Up: one, from the low rows. Walked to the verge and stood facing up the road. Put back: one.

**Night the fifty-fifth.** Up: three. Put back: three, alone. The hook is slow in one pair of hands.

**Night the fifty-ninth.** Up: five. Put back: five. None of them Hew or Tamsin. Their lids in the high row have not sunk at all, not a finger, and the turf has taken over them as it takes over a stone. Wenna's lamp-tender still sits at the road-post, facing down the road, which is the way he came.

**Night the sixty-first.** Up: 7. Put back: 6.

**Night the sixty-second.** Up: 9. Put back: 9. The sexton on the verge with his hook, standing. Facing up the road. Will not come in. Not put back. Not counted.
