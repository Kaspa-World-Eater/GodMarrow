# The Lid at Callow Ford

*A coffin lid of Barrows pine, found leaning on the shrine-stone at Callow Ford with its written side to the stone and the iron nail that cut it still driven in the corner; under it was a little grave-earth and nobody, and the stone faced the road. One line near the head has been scraped to the wood with the same nail, and the name at the very top has been gone over so many times the letters are trenches.*

HWENNA. HWENNA IS THE NAME. SAY IT AT THE STONE.

First. I am ——— of Callow Ford, who sets the ford-stones after the spring water. I am dead. I know it the way you know it is night without looking. I came up through the lid where it had sunk in the middle, and I have kept the lid, because I will want to set things down and there is nothing else on the road to set them on. I have the nail from the corner.

There is one thing. The stone at the ford has a name, and I am the last who says it. Every morning I set the stones I said it, wet to the knee, and the stone kept its face to the road. They put me in the Barrows in the hard frost and nobody at the ford knows the name. A stone nobody says turns to the wall. I am going to say it there. Then I will lie down. I do not know why this is the thing. It is the thing.

What I have: my man Corran, a carter, in the Barrows before me. My daughter Ebbe, who went up the road to the Order's town and never sent word, because she had no letters. The cold of the water at the knee. The ford is eleven days for the living.

Two marks. The tender at the first lantern-post saw me coming and put his lantern out, and stood in the dark with me rather than be seen by it. I passed him close. I said a good night. He did not give it back. I am cold now only in the hands.

Five marks. A brother of the Pale Order on the low road, a cord of knots at his belt and a dead man carried on a yoke behind him. He did not run. He told me to stand and I stood, because walking costs the day and standing costs less. He counted me through the shroud from the feet up, joint by joint, touching each, drawing a knot along the cord for each. Noon to lamp. He said two hundred and five. He counted again and said two hundred and five. He said I was one short and must come to the ridge to be made up, and that a frame may not be left open on a road. I asked what I was short. He did not know. He said the Count would know. I walked on, and he walked behind me some while, and then he stopped in the road writing on the back of his hand. I have felt all along myself since and cannot find what is gone. I think a small one, in the ear. I think he has it.

Six marks. A woman of the Myriad at a wayside shrine, with a fan, and a cord on her wrist with more knots on it than one pair of hands could tie. She said I was a wrong breath going along the road and she would take me in and give me to the water. She put her mouth to mine and drew. I felt it go. Nothing that walks went. A face went. She stood back from me and wept, and said there had been a man with a split lip who smelled of axle-fat, and asked me who he was, and wept for him, and I could not tell her. She tied a knot on her cord for me. She said the one thing was all of me left to hold, and it would not go into her. She said it was too small. I think she meant too hard.

Seven marks. Corran. I have written Corran. It says a carter. I have no face for it.

Eight marks. Four grey ones on the road with bowls, from the mountain that fell, people say. The living go round them in the fields. They did not go round me. They asked me for my name for the bowl, gently, and I gave it, and I felt it go down into the bowl like a stone into a well and not strike. I have scraped it off the lid so I do not give the other by mistake, reading. They asked had I another. I said no. That is the first lie I have told dead, and I would tell it again.

Nine marks. Read the lid from the top this morning. HWENNA I know. Callow Ford I know. Corran is a carter because it says. Ebbe I have written twice, so she mattered. The Order's town is up the road behind me, the other way. I am not going up it. I would like to say I am sorry to someone about that and I do not know who.

At the bridge below Haddow they had heard I was coming. Every door had its own dead's lid stood against it outside, face out, so I would see whose house it was and pass. I passed. One lid had no house behind it. I did not look at whose.

Ten marks. I can hear the water the way you hear a thing in the next room. The cold is at the elbows. There is a woman in the first lines of this lid who sets the ford-stones and I believe her.

Eleven. The ford. The spring water has had the stones out across the bed and nobody has set them. The stone has its face to the hill. I set the ford-stones first, all nineteen, because they were in my hands to do and the hands knew it before I did. Then I put my shoulder to the stone and turned it to the road. It came easy, as if it had been leaned on lately from the other side.

At its foot there is a paper figure, fresh, folded small by somebody's child, with a pebble on it against the wind, and a name written on its chest in a child's letters. It is not HWENNA. Someone comes here. Someone says a name to this stone that is the wrong one, and says it often, the figure is not a week old. I will set the child's name down here so it is somewhere: AUD. I do not know who that is. It is no name of any stone I ever stood in water by.

I have only the lid. I will say mine. I will say it once and loud and lean the lid here with the writing to the stone, so it can read it after, if stones read, and I will lie down where the earth is soft by the bank. The hands are gone to the shoulder. Here. HWENNA. I am saying it now, Hw
