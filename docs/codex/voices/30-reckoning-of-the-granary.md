# The Reckoning of the Town Granary at Haskel Market, Threshing to Closing

*Taken from the shelf of the granary office at Haskel Market, which I found unlocked and swept; the shelf had room for a second book, and the dust showed there had been one.*

Reckoning of the town granary of Haskel Market, kept by the factor for the reeve and for the Order's assessor, from the threshing to the closing of the bins, in the eleven hundred and ninth year of the count. Every total brought forward. Every figure reduced to its root, as the Order requires of any book that owes it tithe.

**Brought forward from the summer book.** On the floor, 1,204 sacks, root seven. Owed by the bakers on tally, 61 sacks, root seven. Owed to the ossuary at White Notch, one sack in nine of all brought in.

**Note to the 3rd.** Counted twice, once by me and once by Ebbo the clerk, and three short both times. A miscall at the gate, most likely. Entered as shrinkage.

**Noted for the reeve, the 3rd, at dusk.** A man at the edge of the market by the weigh-post after the stalls were down. Thin, in grey, one finger raised, the way a man holds it who is counting heads in a crowd and has not come to the end. The lantern-man went round him and lit the post-lamp and did not look at him.

**Note to the 5th.** Sealed both nights under the reeve's wax; the wax whole both mornings. Three gone each night, out of the bin the 3rd day's carts were tipped into. Writing my book down does not close it. I sat with the gate tally and found it: the gate clerk entered 110 sacks in on the 3rd, and my floor took 107. I paid the gate clerk a day's wage to write 107 over 110. The night of the 5th, nothing went.

So it is this. While two books disagree, the difference is taken, and taken again at every count, until they agree.

**The 6th, at dusk.** He was at the weigh-post. The finger came down once, and up.

The shrine woman at the Ford Gate says he is a wrong breath and offers to take him in, for a cake of wax. The Myriad would breathe at a debt and call it paid. The brother who assessed us at midsummer, when I told him of the man by the post, wrote a name on his slate, and I read it, Ykkoth-Taal, and he wiped it with his sleeve and walked on to Wenlow in the dark. In the market they call him the Tallyman. The Order counts what is its own. He counts what is not his.

**Note to the 7th.** The Esk mill's reckoning, sent with the last carter, says nine carts came to Haskel. The gate counted eight. The mill book lies at Esk, and I cannot write in it.

**The 8th.** The Esk cart that stood in the granary yard overnight, laden, fourteen sacks, is gone. The shafts were let down on the cobbles when the two carters went to their beds, and the marks of the shaft-ends are on the cobbles still, and the wheel-marks run from the gate to where it stood, and stop. The sacks were never on my floor, so I enter nothing against the floor. I enter one cart, the miller's, lost in the town's keeping.

From the 8th I have gone through every roll I keep, so that no two disagree.

**Note to the 9th.** The ninth porter on the roll is Gerrit Pell, who died at midsummer and went to White Notch on the Order's cart. His wage has been drawn every week since, eleven weeks, in Ebbo's hand. I struck Pell from the roll with one line at the lamp-hour, and the roll stood at eight and eight.

**The 10th.** Ebbo did not come to the office. His stool is at the board, his pen cut fresh, his coat on its peg, his ink not yet dry in the well. His wife has not seen him since the night. I struck Pell. I did not strike Ebbo. And the roll was right, eight and eight, when I closed the book.

I have found it. Eleven weeks of wage drawn for a man who was not there. The coin-box is short by that, and the coin-box is a count too, and Ebbo was the difference in it.

There is no one else to take for that line but the next clerk, or me. I have struck Ebbo Tamm from the roll of clerks. I write the factor in his place, at no wage.

**The 11th, dusk.** Nearer. He stands now at the corner where Coopers' Row comes into the market. Seen from the granary step, the finger is going along the eaves.

The Count-breakers say a tally unmade is a mercy. A book burned is a book wrong by everything in it. I have thought of it and I will not.

**Note to the 11th.** The Order assesses the tithe by streets and by hearths, and its brother counted us twice, as the Rule says, and came out one street higher the second time, and kept the second. His roll is at White Notch on the Ninth Stair, and no lowland hand writes in the Count. The hearths agree. The streets do not.

**The 12th.** Coopers' Row is gone. The tanners' back wall stands against the chandlers' back wall, and there is no lane between them, and no mortar between them, and the stones sit as if they were laid together. I walked the market's length with the rod. It is short by the width of the Row, and by nothing else. Fourteen hearths stood in Coopers' Row.

**Note to the 12th.** A debt with no debtor is a wrong count. I have struck Widow Marl from the tally and entered her three sacks as forgiven, so that no line stands owed by no one.

**The 13th, by lamp.** I have worked it on the slate four times and it comes out alike.

The Order's count does not come down. The Feast of the Laying teaches that every count rises and that the rising is the Mother's gift, and I believed it, and I believe it still. He takes the difference, and the taking makes it wider, and the Order will not count fewer, and I cannot make a street.

Haskel has thirteen streets tonight and two hundred and ninety-seven hearths. Root nine. The Order has three hundred and eleven. I have not worked what fourteen hearths come to.

I have one more count I can make right. The reeve's roll names me twice: once as factor of the granary, at the head of the book, and once as householder of the second hearth in Wainwright Street, under my father's name, which I took.
