# Of Reading the Spaces, in Eleven Parts

*Sewn leaves from the desk of the copy-room at Four Wents, left there by the copyist when he walked out on the Moor road before noon and did not come back. The leaves are wider in the margins than any I have handled; I have kept every blank where he left it.*

**I. Of what I first noticed.**

For thirty-one years I copied other people's pages. Death-rolls for the Pale Order, at so much the leaf. The Words for the Maiden's processions. Claims for reeves. A hymn-leaf of the Crone's circles, once. A copyist is paid for the letters. I left the spaces the width of my smallest nail and gave them no more thought than a ditch gives the field.

In my twenty-third winter I copied a death-roll from a leaf so old the ink had gone the colour of the leaf. I could not read the names. I could read the gaps between them. When I laid my copy beside the old leaf I saw that I had made every gap one width, and on the old leaf no two were the same, and the gap after the fourteenth name was wider than my hand. I had copied the words right and the page wrong.

**II. How I learned to read them.**

By copying nothing else. I took old leaves and copied only the spaces, leaving the letters out, so that my page showed where words had stood and nothing of them. I measured each gap with a thread and tied a knot at its width. The first year was only knots.

In the second year the knots began to agree. A reeve's claim from the Moor and a tally of the Fifth Ossuary, which had never lain in one room, had the same gap at the same place, after the same count of words. The gaps ran across forty leaves the way a crack runs across flagstones laid at different times.

In the third year I put the thread away. Measuring was in the way of it.

I returned the leaves to their owners. Two of them have told me since that their leaves came back blank. Nobody had scraped them. I had only copied the spaces.

**III. Of the monks.**

Two bowl-bearers of the Gilded Peak stopped at my door one forenoon to ask for ink. A leaf of my spaces lay on the board. The elder stood over it until the hour was nearly gone, and then said I had heard it, and named it by its old name, which those who know it write as a gap: [ … ]. I belong to no faith. **IV. What the spaces say.**

I will give the shape and never the saying. The saying will not go into letters; I have tried, and each time the sentence went thin and ended.

A space after a name is shaped like the one who bore it, turned away. A space between two of the Order's numbers is narrow and deep and has a floor, like a niche. A space at the end of a letter is always the same shape, whoever wrote it, and the same shape as the space at the end of a death-roll. The space between the Crone's names, on that one hymn-leaf, was shaped like the eye of a needle with nothing through it.

Some spaces lean, the way a door leans toward the room it lets into. Some are closed at the bottom, like a bowl. A few are open at both ends, like a cord cut, and those I do not stay with.

            

The gap above is the truest thing in this part, and I did not leave it on purpose.

**V. Where it is written.**

In margins, first: the largest gap on any leaf. In the pause a chanter holds after the syllable. In the still air between two breaths of a man on his last bed, which grows wider, which I have read by a lantern and set down nowhere.

In the beat between a heart's strokes. Put two fingers to the throat and wait. The stroke is the word. The wait after it is written in the same hand as the margin of the oldest leaf I have held.

In the empty place in a ring of standing stones. At Ashwake there are eleven stones and a place where the twelfth would stand. Walkers step around it. I stood in it from noon to the lamp-hour, and it was the most legible thing I have ever stood before, and I came out of it with ink on my fingers, though I had carried none.

In the line of lanterns on the Barrow road where one has gone out and the tender has not yet come.

**VI. What the Pale Order gets wrong.**

The Order teaches that the Hush unsays: that it took the god's names one at a time until the god forgot itself and died. So the talliers allow no gap in a Count. Where a niche is empty they write the number of it anyway. They have packed their rolls so tight that the spaces have nowhere left to stand.

The spaces are still there, pressed thin, held between numbers like a breath behind teeth. I have read them. Each holds the name the Order says was taken, kept close, the way two hands hold water. The Order reads a gap as a theft because it counts what is missing. It has never once read what is kept.

**VII. What the Maiden's faithful get wrong.**

The rule of the processions forbids a space between the Words. They are written run together, one breath long, so that nothing lies between them. I left the spaces in once, from habit. The leaf came back to me cut through at every gap, and the pieces counted, and not one missing.

They fear the Hush because their end is the Filling, when every hollow is filled, the wound and the grave and the gap between one word and the next. They say so carefully, as they should. They are right that the Hush lives in the beat between the strokes of a heart. They are wrong to fear it there. A heart with no wait in it is one long stroke, and I have seen what lies in the road when a heart does that.

**VIII. What the Peak gets wrong.**

The Peak waits for it. They hold that the world is a breath held too long and that the Hush comes at the end, when the breath is let go. They wait the way a man waits at a door for someone already in the room behind him.

It is written now, on every leaf in every press on the Hide, in the margins of the tallies that climbed their stair.

**IX. Of the gaps in this treatise.**

I have left gaps here, and some I left by hand and some I found after. Do not read past them. Stop at each until it has given its shape. If you have read this treatise and found only the words, you have read it as I read the death-roll in my twenty-third winter, and you have copied it right and the page wrong.

**X. Of what I cannot tell.**

When I read a space I find a meaning in it. I cannot tell whether I found it there or brought it.

A copyist's hand learns the letters of whoever it copies. After thirty-one years mine cannot tell my letters from the dead's. Last winter I laid this treatise beside the oldest leaf I own and the spaces in my hand were the same shape as the spaces in that one, every one, the fourteenth gap and the wide one and the one that is open at both ends.
