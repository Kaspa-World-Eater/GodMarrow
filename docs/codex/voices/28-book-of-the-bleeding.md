# The Book of the Bleeding, Kept at the Hospice Under Red Stair

*It lay open on the reading-stand of the hospice under Red Stair, at the last written leaf, in a ward where every bed was stripped and the bowls were turned down on the sills. The ink of the last line was still soft, and the hospice had stood empty since the thaw.*

Book of the bleeding of the hospice under Red Stair, kept by the infirmarian as the Rule of the chapter asks: every soul who comes in bleeding to be entered, with the wound, with what was done, with what was given and to what line. Blood that leaves a body under this roof leaves with the Words said over it, into the bowl, and goes down the stair to her. Nothing here is spilled. That is the whole of the work.

**The ninth of the dark month.** Arnolt, quarryman of the Stair. Thigh opened on the edge of a fallen course. Corded above the knee, the Words said, the bowl held under. Gave to the line and a thumb over. Closed with the iron. Walked out the fourth day.

**The ninth, the lamp-hour.** Wenna, wife of the lamp-keeper. Bleeding after a child; the child dead. Bowl, Words, cloths packed. Bled through the cloths, and through the second cloths.

**The tenth, before light.** It came for Wenna.

I set down what was seen, as the Rule asks of anything seen under this roof. It came in at the yard door and stooped under the lintel, and was still stooping when it stood up inside, being taller than the door by a head and a neck. It is wet dark from the throat to the floor, all one dark, as a shift is after a long bleeding, and it wears no shift. It smelled of copper, as this ward smells, and over the copper of bread warm from the oven, which this ward has never smelled of. The night sister wept at the smell. She says she did not know she was weeping.

It went to Wenna's bed and knelt, and kneeling it was the height of me standing. It laid its hand flat over the cloths. It asked her if she was tired. It asked kindly. I write kindly because there is no other word, and I have looked for one. It said it would take the bleeding, all of it, and she would never have to do it again.

I said the Words. It waited until I had finished them, the way one waits for a child to finish its prayer, and then it asked her again.

She said yes.

**The tenth, morning.** Wenna sits up. The cloths are dry. Under them the opening is closed without a mark, and when the sister pressed it, it gave and came slowly back, as dough does. It smells of the oven. The bowl from the night is empty. I had covered it with a board, and the board was still on it.

**The twelfth.** Wenna walked out. Her husband came for her at noon and stood at the door and asked for his wife, and I said she was in the yard, and he looked into the yard and asked which. He could not give me her name. I went to the book to give it to him. Her name is in the book, in my hand. I read it, and it went out of me while my eye was still on it. I have read it eleven times since. I cannot say it after.

**Rule set by the infirmarian, the thirteenth, to be read aloud at every lamp-hour.** No one under this roof is to let it take the bleeding. Blood is the Mother's. A wound is her door into us, and the bowl is how we answer at the door. One who cannot bleed cannot give at the Measure, this year or any year, and owes her a whole life with nothing left to pay it in. If it comes, say the Words and keep saying them. If you are too weak, the sister at your bed will say them for you. Do not answer it. It is kind. That is how you will know it.

The Elder holds that it is the Mother's own, sent in mercy to those who have given more than the line. The Elder has never sat a night in this ward. Her mercy would not keep the name.

The Pale Order's counter, down for our yard tally, wrote that a wound which closes is grace upon the frame. They count a body as one whatever is taken out of it. They have never held a bowl.

**The second of the thaw.** Ilo, a boy of the Stair. Bleeding at the nose three days. It came the third night. Refused, for him: I said the Words, the sister said them, the boy said them with us through the cloth. It waited at the foot of the bed until first grey and went out by the yard door, stooping. The wet on it was higher than at Wenna's bed. It reached the chin. Ilo stopped of himself at noon. His name is Ilo. I write it a second time to see. Ilo. It stays.

**The ninth of the thaw.** A carter of the Four Wents road, named [ … ]. Crushed under his own wheel. It took him while I was at the bowl at the next bed, and he answered it before I could turn. I have left the space for his name. I had it at the gate.

**What has become of those who took it, so far as the Stair knows.** The lamp-keeper's wife works at the trough and does not bleed. In the frost she opened her palm on a quarry edge and stood looking at it. The lamp-keeper says it was pink inside, and dry, and close, and smelled of the oven, and that she was glad. No one at the trough calls her anything. The carter has gone back to the road, where the carters call him the one who doesn't. At the Measure neither came to the cup. The Elder sent a runner for them by name, and the runner came back and stood in the chapter door and could not say whom he had been sent for.

They come to the yard at night, both of them. They stand outside the yard door with their faces to it, close, as one stands at a door one has been asked to wait at. They do not knock. The beds nearest the door sleep easier when they are there.

**The twentieth of the thaw. The infirmarian.** Entered in my own hand, as the Rule asks of every soul under this roof who is bleeding.

The line opened at the lamp-hour. The old line on the inside of the left arm, where I have given at every Measure for thirty-one years, and where the bloodletter's knife has gone in thirty-one times and been closed thirty-one times with the iron. Nothing opened it. I was at the bowls. I looked down and my sleeve was dark.

Corded. The Words said. The bowl under, and the second bowl, and the third. I closed it with the iron myself and it opened behind the iron. I am the one who opens others and by old rule never herself, and I did not open this, and it will not shut.

It came at the second hour. The wet is at its mouth now. It knelt at my stool and was the height of me sitting, and it waited while I said the Words, and it asked me if I was tired. I said no. It asked again near the fourth hour. I said the Words instead. It said it would wait, and it is kind, and it waited, and the bread smell was so warm I could not keep my eyes on the bowl.

I woke at first grey. It is gone. The cloth on my arm is dry. I have not lifted the cloth. The bowls on the sill are empty, and I do not know if I emptied them, and I do not know what I said while I slept, and no one sat the night to tell me.
