# The Confession of Ysolde, Keeper of Faces, Taken at Cold Tarn

*Found folded inside a coverer's felt in the burnt lodge at Cold Tarn, the felt scorched at one corner and the page whole; the last line is in a second hand, and I have not found whose.*

Taken before the circle of Cold Tarn, every felt drawn, in the ninth winter since the loose winter. Her thread read before the questions: forty-one hands. She is the reader called Ysolde, of the circle that was at Hask Water, which took the felt off the glass. Questioned by the circle-keeper. Set down by me, coverer and scribe of this lodge, for the burning.

**Q.** What do you keep?

**A.** Faces. Each in a hand-glass the size of a palm, ground hollow and backed with pitch, sewn into the lining of my coat at the breast, at the ribs, down the skirts. A dead one looks into its own face in the glass at the hour of its going and stops there. I sew the glass in uncovered and I keep it uncovered. That is the whole of the craft.

**Q.** How many?

**A.** I took thirty-one. Thirty-one by my own hand, from bed and road and ditch, and I can say every name.

**Q.** How many are in the coat?

**A.** Ask the coat.

[The coat was laid on the bench and turned out by the coverers, felt over our hands. Thirty-one glasses are sewn in the lining. I counted them myself. In the glasses at the hem I counted two faces apiece. The keeper says these are one face seen twice, as in water. They are two faces.]

**Q.** Why do you hold there is no loom?

**A.** Because Aud of Hask Water looked. She was eldest of our circle and wore the coat every eldest before her had sewn. In the loose winter the valley's dead came home up the water to every door asking which way. She sent them as the circle sends: covered the glass, said the names dusk to grey, drew each one through the eye. They came back the next night and asked again. The same ones. So she took the felt off the deepest glass of the coat, the one at the heart that no eldest uncovers, to see the eye they were going through. She looked from grey to dusk, and said, There is no loom, in the voice she used for salt. She took off the coat and gave it to me.

**Q.** Your eldest was sick in the mind.

**A.** She counted the stitches of the lining that night and had every one right.

**Q.** Then where do the sent dead go?

**A.** Where they went in the loose winter. Up the water to the door, asking. Your circle sends them faster now. You have more at the doors than you had.

[The keeper made no answer to the reader. What the keeper said after, the keeper said to me, and I have left it out.]

**Q.** What do the kept dead say?

**A.** Mostly their names, to learn them again. A face in glass forgets slower than a thread in the weave, I hold, because it has itself to look at. A carter says the load. A child says the stair. They ask after the living, and I tell them, and I lie when it is kind.

**Q.** Do they ask which way?

**A.** Every one. Every night.

**Q.** What do you answer?

**A.** Here. I say here is the way. You are at your own face and you are home.

**Q.** Do they believe you?

**A.** They have stopped asking it of me. They ask it of each other now, glass to glass, in the lining, when they think I sleep. Lately one of them answers.

**Q.** Which?

**A.** I cannot tell which. The voice comes from the hem.

**Q.** What does it answer?

**A.** It draws breath, as a person draws breath to say a word, and stops. That is all it says. And the others go quiet to listen to the stop, longer each night.

[I write that the reader touched the hem as she said this, as a woman touches a wound that has been sewn.]

**Q.** What does it cost you?

**A.** Warmth. I wear the coat next to the skin, because a shift over it would cover them. I have been cold since the felt came off. My own face I cannot find in water any longer; water shows the kept. The widows pay me coppers for an hour at a husband's glass, and I take it, and the hour costs me an hour of my own thread. Forty-one hands, your reader says. It was read at sixty at the loose winter.

[It was. I have looked in the book of Hask Water, which we hold. Sixty. Nine winters do not take nineteen hands from a thread.]

**Q.** You knew the Rule. A glass is covered at a death so the soul does not stop at itself.

**A.** I knew it. I covered three hundred glasses as a coverer, as you have. Did you ever lift a felt after, and look?

**Q.** It is forbidden.

**A.** It is.

**Q.** Tell the circle what is at the back of the deepest glass.

**A.** Aud's glass, at the heart of the coat, is ground so deep its back is thinner than a fingernail, and the light goes through it to somewhere. The kept go small in it, as people go small down a lane. Behind them is a space. It is round, and very wide, and empty, and I hold it is the Hollow: the eye of the loom, with no loom round it. Everything you send goes into it, and finds nothing there to be woven on, and comes back up the water.

**Q.** You have looked into it.

**A.** Every night since Hask Water.

**Q.** What do you see there?

**A.** Nothing. I want that set down right. I see nothing, and the nothing is clear. It is clearer than the faces. Clearer than your page, if you held it to the lamp.

[I did not hold it up.]

**Q.** Does it see you?

**A.** It is an eye with nothing behind it. But the faces have begun to turn. When I first sewed them in, each looked out of its glass at its own face, as the dead do. Now each looks a little aside, toward the heart of the coat, toward Aud's glass. The ones at the hem must look the farthest. I think that is why there are two in some of them. I think they are making room.

**Q.** Then you have filled the coat with the dead of others.

**A.** I took thirty-one.

**Q.** Will you take the felt, and be received again into the circle?

**A.** If you burn the glass, where will they go?

**Q.** Through the eye.

**A.** Then you send them to the place I have looked at, and they come back to your doors. I would keep them at their faces a little longer. The widows would. They come back either way.

[Here the keeper ended the questions and gave the coat to the fire at grey and the reader to the felt. What follows is my own.

The coat lay on the bench under felt through the night of this record. In the morning the felt over the hem was wet through, as glass sweats in a warm room, and the lodge was cold. I did not lift it. I counted the felt's stitches instead, and had them right.

At grey the coverers carried the coat out. The keeper says it burned. I stood in the lodge door and saw the fire, and saw the glass go white in it, and heard nothing from it.

I record that in the doorway, between the post and the felt that hangs there, there is a gap the width of a hand, and I have looked at it this morning for a long while, longer than the burning, and it is very clear.

I record that when they took the reader to the felt she turned to me and asked which way. I believed she meant the door. I have pointed to it.]
