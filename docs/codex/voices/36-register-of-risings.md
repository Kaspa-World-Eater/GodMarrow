# Register of Risings in the Hundred of Lessow, Kept for the Laying Down

*Taken from the hundred-chest under the moot-stone at Lessow, where the leaves were kept rolled in a cord with the hundred's dues; the last of them are in a hand that is not the reeve's, and the chest had been opened from inside the cord.*

Register of the risen of the hundred of Lessow, by place and season, kept by Tobbas, reeve, charged with the laying down, from the eleven hundred and first year of the Order's count. Risen counted at the lamp-hour, standing. Laid down counted at first light, lying.

**The Ford.** Wet season, eleven risen; dry, two; wet, thirteen; dry, one. They stand in the shallows with their faces downstream. The people of the Ford fear the river at high water, and fear it most for their children.

**Sedge End.** Every season, between nine and fourteen. They stand round the common well in a ring, an arm's length off, as if waiting to draw. The people of Sedge End fear the well. They say a woman went down it in the old days and something sent her bucket up full.

**Kiln Lane.** Sowing season, four; dark quarter, twenty-two. They rise in their doorways and face up the lane toward the road. The people of Kiln Lane fear the monks of the Gilded Peak, who come down in fours with their bowls in the dark quarter and ask for names.

**Old Pightle.** None, any season, as far back as the old reeve's tallies run. The people there fear nothing in particular. They are poor and tired, and I have entered them as such.

## What the accounts show

I kept the register four years before I set it beside the rolls of complaint, and when I did I saw at once what I had been keeping. The risen of a place rise in the season its people are most afraid, and in the number of their fear. They stand facing the thing feared. They do not care where they died; the old weaver who died at the Ford and was buried at Sedge End rose at the well.

The Order's anointers say bone stands by marrow. The Shrine Keepers say the dead rise on wrong breath and walk till it is breathed out of them. The Count-breakers of Fennick unmake tallies by night, holding that numbers keep the dead walking. I have kept more numbers than they have ever burned, and my dead lie down when the hundred is told the right thing. All three are wrong, and only the Count-breakers have the excuse of being simple.

## The trials

**The first, at Sedge End.** I had myself let down the well on the bucket-rope at noon, in sight of the whole end, and drew a bucket at the bottom and drank it at the top. I did the same the next noon and the noon after. Water: cold going down, warm when I came up, which I said nothing of. What I saw at the bottom I have no word for in this register, and the register needs none. Risen at Sedge End the following season: two. The season after: none. A fear proven harmless lays its dead down within a season.

**The second, at Kiln Lane.** The bowl-monks cannot be proven harmless, since they are not, so I stopped the talk of them. A fine of three coppers on any person heard to say the monks were coming, or to name a person who had given a name into the bowl. Risen the next dark quarter: nine, from twenty-two. I note that a fear forbidden is slower than a fear disproved, and leaves a remainder. Of the nine, one stood in no doorway. It stood in the lane, facing up it, and no one of the lane could say whose it was or who had buried it. It did not lie down at first light with the rest. I have entered it as the remainder.

**The third, at Old Pightle.** Since nothing rose there, I wanted to know whether a fear could be started. I had my wife's cousin say at the Pightle pump that the old lime-pit behind the tithe-barn had something in it that breathed. I said nothing myself. Risen at Old Pightle that dry season: six, standing at the pit's lip, facing in. Next season: eleven. I had the pit dug out in front of them to the chalk, and there was nothing in it, and I showed them the chalk. Next season: nineteen.

They had stopped fearing the pit by then. They feared me, because a rumour had gone round that the reeve had dug at Old Pightle and found it, and covered it again. I did not start that one. The nineteen stood with their faces toward the moot-stone. I set it down as the third finding: a fear started cannot be called back by the one who started it, since the calling back is itself something to fear.

## What works

Proven harmless: lays down within a season, entire.
Forbidden: lays down slower, leaves a remainder, the remainder unaccounted.
Started: rises, grows, and turns.

I have laid down, by these means, one hundred and forty of the risen of this hundred in six years, which is more than the knights of the Kostyak ever closed for us with their hooks, and I have not had to pay them for it, nor watch them count the living.

## The wet season of the eleven hundred and eighth year

The river bank above the Ford went in the third week of rain, a breach forty paces wide, and the hundred had seventy hands fit to carry earth and nothing like enough earth for them to carry. The water was over the low fields in a night and would be in the Ford by the second.

The risen stand where the feared thing is. I put it about at the Ford, through the women who keep the children, that the river had asked for the children of the hundred by name, and that the breach was where it would come in for them. By the lamp-hour every mother at the Ford had her children in the loft. By the second lamp-hour the dead of the Ford had risen and walked up the bank, and the dead of Sedge End after them, since the Sedge End women had heard it too, and they went down into the breach and stood in it, face to the water, shoulder to shoulder, the ones behind leaning on the ones in front. Risen at the breach that night: two hundred and twelve. More than I had ever entered. More than are buried in the hundred, by the old reeve's book. The water stood.

It is standing now. They are standing in it.

I cannot lay them down. Were the fear proven false, the bank would go. So it is to be kept. A fear wears thin in a season unless it is given cause, and I have found that out by keeping accounts, as I find everything. The children of the Ford do not go out. The mothers do not sleep. Each season, cause is given.
