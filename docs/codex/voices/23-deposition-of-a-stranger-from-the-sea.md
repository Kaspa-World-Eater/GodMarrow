# The Deposition of the Wet Man at Lessing Ford

*A fair copy from the reeve's roll at Lessing Ford, the leaf stained at one corner in a tide-mark that runs uphill; the reeve's widow let me copy it and asked that I take the original too, and I did not.*

Taken before me, Oswy Tamm, reeve of Lessing Ford under the Wardens, in the Order's year eleven hundred and twelve, the ninth day after the lantern-count, in the lock-room under the toll-gate. Witness to the mark: Adda the carter, who found him.

The deponent was found on the Barrow road two leagues above the ford, lying face down on the verge with his arms out. Beside him lay the ribs of a boat, eleven of them, joined at a keel, grey and split. There is no water on that road and has been none in any count I keep. His clothes were wet through and are wet still, the third day, though he has sat by the fire. The salt dries white on the cloth and the cloth stays wet under it.

He speaks a slow speech with some of our words in it, and others I have written as near as I could. Where I could not follow him I have said so.

**1. Of where he comes from.** Deponent says he is of a people on a shore he names Ouhm, or Ohum; he corrected me both times and I have let it stand. He says the shore lies at the edge of a great water that has no other side. He says the ground there is cold. He was surprised that ours is warm and put his palms flat to the floor of the lock-room many times, and asked what was under it. I told him the ground, as everywhere.

He says no one of his people has heard of the Hide. He did not know the word. He did not know the Wardens, the Order, the Count, or any of the faiths I named to him.

**2. Of the sea, and what is in it.** Deponent says the great water is called the sea, and that a god drowned in it before there were people. He will not give the god's name. He says the god's name went down with it, and the sea kept it, and to say it on land would be to take it out of the water, which is theft. He made a gap with his hands when he spoke of the god, the width of a cup, and left it empty.

He says his people do not pray to the god, because the god is drowned and cannot hear. They pray to what the sea remembers of it. He says the sea remembers the god the way a bowl of water remembers a stone dropped into it: it goes on moving after. His people go out in boats to the deep places and say into the water some small thing the god once was, as their elders gave it them: that it had a hand; that it was tall at evening; that it was once cold. They say it once, and do not say it again. The sea keeps it.

**3. Of how the sea answers.** Deponent says the sea answers in the count of the waves. After a prayer the waves on the shore come in a number, and the elders sit and count them, and the number is the answer. Seven is a yes. A number that will not finish is the god turning over.

I asked what the sea answers with when it is angry. He said the sea is never angry. He said it only remembers more than it should.

*Reeve's note.* I asked whether his drowned god was the Bearing Mother, gone down into water, since the Order teaches she fell. He did not know her and asked how a mother falls. I asked whether the counting of waves was a count like the Order's. He said the waves are counted once and the number thrown back. I asked whether the god he will not name was the Hush. He asked me what the Hush was, and I could not tell him, and wrote this instead.

**4. Of the making of the world, as his people say it.** Deponent says that before the sea, the world was dry and level, and a god walked over it looking for a thing it had put down. It walked so long that it forgot what the thing was. Every step it forgot more, and what it forgot ran out of it and lay in the low places, and that was the first water. At the end it had forgotten everything but its own walking, and it came to the lowest place, where all it had forgotten lay deepest, and it walked in, still looking. The water closed over it. The sea is everything the god forgot, and the god is at the bottom of it, and the sea is the only thing that remembers it now.

His people, he says, are the last thing the god was thinking when the water closed. The sea put them on the shore because it had no room for them. That is why they will not learn to swim.

*Reeve's note.* The Order says the world was set down. The Crone's circles say a thread. Adda says the Maiden's people say a wound, and her sister, who keeps a shrine, says a breath. This man's making has none of them in it. He says nothing fell, nothing was spun, nothing opened, nothing breathed. He says something walked, and forgot, and drowned. I do not know which faith to send him to. I do not think he has a soul that any of ours would take.

**5. Of how he came inland.** Deponent says he went out in a boat of four with his uncle and two others, to say a thing into the deep water that the elders had given his uncle: that the god, before it walked, had once been carried.

He says the waves came in a count that would not finish. He says the sea stood up under the boat and went inland with it, over country he did not know and a dark plain of ash, and the water was under them the whole way, high and smooth, though he could look down through it and see dry ground. His uncle and the two others went over the side, singly, each at a different place, and did not go under. He says they stood on the water a moment, looking down, and walked off into the dark. He says the sea was looking for something, and was going where it remembered a thing being, and that it should not have gone there, because there is no sea here and never was.

He says the water left him on our road. He says it went down into the ground, all of it, at once, the way water goes into sand, and the ground was warm.

**6. Of what he asks.** Deponent asks to be taken to water. I told him the nearest standing water is the Fen, and that I will not send a living man to the Fen. He asked whether the dead lie down here. I told him no. He said in his country they do, and they are put in the sea and it keeps them, and they are not counted, and they do not come back up. He asked me why ours come back. I wrote nothing.

He asks that if he dies on the Hide he be not counted.

*Reeve's note.* I have kept him in the lock-room. The cup of water I set by him at noon was full at dusk, and he had drunk from it. Adda counted the drips from his sleeve onto the flags in the hour after supper and they came to seven, and stopped, and she will not come back in.

*In another hand:* Lock-room found barred at first light. Floor wet to the door and the wet running up the outer wall. The boat-ribs on the Barrow road are twelve.
