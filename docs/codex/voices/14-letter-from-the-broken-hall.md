# The Letter Under the Seat

*Folded small and pushed into a crack in the ninth step of a great seat, in a broken hall so far from the Hide that no road I know of goes there; the salvager who brought it back brought nothing else, and has not gone back.*

Wenna,

I begin this at the first frost, with a coal from the kitchen fire on the back of a tally-skin, because Tam says a carter goes south in the spring and might carry it. If he does not, I will have written it anyway.

We are still here. Eleven of us this winter, nine since the stair came down. We live in the old kitchens under the east wall, where the roof holds, and each day we go out into the rest of it for what can be carried: nails, hinge-iron, lead from the gutters, glass from the high windows when a pane falls whole. Iron goes to the ford for meal. Glass we keep, for lanterns. There is more of this place than any of us has walked. Old Garro says it goes down as far as it goes up, and nobody living has seen the top.

You will want to know about the hall. Everyone who comes asks about the hall.

It is the great room in the heart of the walls, so long that the far end is blue on a clear day. The roof is mostly gone. Down the middle runs a floor of black stone, cracked through, and at the end of it, up nine steps, there is the seat, and on the seat there is the Sitter.

I have tried to think how to tell you its size. Stand our mother's whole cottage on the bottom step and the Sitter's knee would be over the thatch. It is grey and brown like the walls, and like the walls it is broken. Its bones come through its hide in rows along the side, white and dry, and between them the hide is drawn so tight it shines. One wing lies folded on its back, long as a mast, with skin hanging off it in sheets. Where the other wing was there is a stump, stopped with something dark like pitch. On its head is a ring of black iron as wide as a cart-wheel, with points standing up from it, and the iron has grown into the brow so that you cannot see where the ring ends and the head begins.

It is a statue that breathes. I can say it no better. Every so often, Garro counts it at a hundred and some, the sides go in, and after a long while they come out, and the air of the whole hall moves toward the seat and then away. You feel it on your face. That is all it does. We carry glass past it every day.

Tam says it looks like the dragon in Mother's winter story, and we laughed at him. That one had two wings and breathed fire on the king's barns. This one has one, and breathes dust.

There is one rule here, and only one. You are not to be afraid in the hall. You may be cold in it, or tired, or grieving, or you may curse the Sitter to its face, and people do. You may not fear it. Garro had the rule from his mother, who had it from hers. I asked him why, and he said because fear makes it stronger. I asked stronger how, and he said he did not know, and that was why the rule had to be kept so carefully. When the little ones first come into the hall we tell them it is a stone, and they believe us, and that is the best way.

*

Deep winter. I have not written for a while because of Ilse's boy.

He was on the west gallery after lead and the gallery let go. He fell the height of four men onto the black floor, below the seat. Ilse got to him first. She knelt there with him and then she stood up and screamed at the Sitter. She had no words in it. She screamed at its face for as long as she had breath.

It moved its head. The width of my hand, perhaps, no more, and slowly, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice. It turned toward her. The iron on its brow grated on the bone. All of us heard it.

Garro took her out by the arm and told her the rule over and over, quietly, until she could say it back. We laid the boy in the undercroft. I should tell you about the undercroft. Under the hall there are rooms full of bones, very old, heaped to the vaults. We take nothing from there; it is the one place we do not salvage. Many of the bones have long grooves worn in them, two and two, side by side. Garro says the old people were careless with their knives.

The Sitter's head did not go back. It is still turned the width of a hand toward the place where Ilse stood.

*

The thaw. A sheet of hide came off its flank in the first warm week and lay on the steps like a sail, and I went up closer than I ever have, to see if it could be cut and used. I could see the ribs plain. They are scored, all along, with long grooves, two and two.

*

Spring, and no carter. Instead a traveller came, from the north, a man in a grey coat with a lantern that had something moving in its glass. He said it was a soul and that his people kept their dark back that way. A wicked thing to carry. Garro thought so too, and would not sit near it.

He had come for the hall. He said he had looked for it most of his life. We took him in, and he walked up the black floor toward the seat, and halfway up he went white and wet as a fevered man and sat down on the stone. His hands shook so that the lantern rang. He was afraid. All of us saw it, and none of us knew what to do, because none of us had ever seen anyone afraid in the hall.

Garro went to lead him out. He would not go. He climbed the nine steps on his knees and put his face close to the base of the seat, where there are letters cut that none of us can read, and moved his finger along them, and said a name. Only once. I set it down as near as I can: Morrh-Ythraal. He said it the way you would say the name of someone dead in front of their mother.

The Sitter opened an eye. I did not know it had an eye. It was under the iron, yellow as tallow, and it opened, and it found him. Garro counted the breath that evening at fifty. The hall was warm all night, warm as a kitchen, and the snow in the corners went to water.

In the morning the traveller was gone. His lantern was on the bottom step, dark. His boots were on the seventh step, side by side, laced. Garro says he went north barefoot and we are to say no more about it.

*

Summer. The breath is at forty. The Sitter's head has come round the rest of the way and faces down the hall now, toward the door to the kitchens. Ilse cannot keep the rule anymore; she sits by the fire and shakes, and the hall is warmest on the side nearest her. Tam went down to the undercroft for the first time since winter to see to the boy. He says the boy is where we laid him. He says many of the old bones are lying in rows now, all along the stair, all with their heads toward the hall, and that he did not lay them so, and nobody did.

Garro says we must practise. So I am writing this in the hall, on the bottom step, where I can feel the breath. I am thinking of you, and of Mother's kitchen, and the plum tree by the well.
