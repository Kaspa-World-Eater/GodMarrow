# The Reckoning of the Cord

*Found folded small under the paper figure of a wayside shrine on the fen road, with a knotted cord wound round it many times; unwound, the cord was longer than the shrine was tall. I took the leaves and left the cord.*

Set down at the shrine of the Leaning Stone, in the forty-first Small Cutting since the water gave the years back.

The elders teach it this way, and I taught it this way at the fen shrines, before my mouth was wanted for other work. The great one breathed in, once, long. Every small god was inside that breath, the god of every stone and ford and bell, warm, all of them one sound. Then it began to breathe out. The world is the out-breath. Every stone, every ford, every bell is a small god riding it on the way out, and each gives its name to the wind as it goes, and settles like dust. When the out-breath is finished, one of us who has already walked down into the water comes back up and breathes the world in, once, as the last rite. The world is folded like a paper figure with its arms open, and set on a step. Nothing comes to breathe on it after.

I was taught that the one who comes up out of the water is the best of us. I was taught nothing about how she comes to be down there.

At my folding I was given a cord of undyed flax a forearm long, and the name I was born with was written on a paper figure and put to the lamp, and I watched the figure open its arms in the flame. For every wrong breath I take into myself by the rite, I tie one knot. The rite turns the breath back on whoever made it. The elders say all of it goes back. I tie the knot anyway, because the rite says tie it, and because I have stopped believing that all of it goes.

I have tied fifty-three knots. Tonight I counted the cord three times by the lamp and had seventy-one each time. The cord goes round my wrist nine times now. I am setting them down in order from the wrist outward, which is from the oldest, so that I may know which are mine.

**The first.** A boundary stone at the Hesk field, moved two lengths in the night. A moved stone breathes sour, the way a cut tongue tastes. I took it in at the new place and turned it back at the old. The man who moved it coughed until the thaw. Mine. I tie with the tail left out, and the tail is out.

**The second.** The well at Bram's Cross, where a woman had said a name down the shaft and the water came up tasting of her. I took it in from the lip of the well. I turned it back on her, and she has not spoken since. Mine.

**The third.** I do not know it. It is tied hard, harder than my fingers tie, and its tail is turned in under itself, the way the folders of the Well-Shrine tied when I was a novice, the way my teacher tied. My teacher walked down into the fen nineteen Cuttings ago. I put the knot to my lips, as we do to hear what a knot holds. It held fen water and the note of a bell under water. I have no bell in me.

**The fourth to the eleventh.** Mine: a lamp-road where the lanterns burned green, a charnel-shed whose dead would not lie down, a ford that took a carter and kept his breath in the reeds, and others like these. Each turned back. Each with its tail out.

**The twelfth, the thirteenth, the fourteenth.** Orrin's Ford, the whole town gone wrong in one night. Every soul in it breathing together, in, out, the same breath, slow, and their guardian stone turned to face the wall. I took in what I could from the square. Three knots. I could not find who made it, and a breath cannot be turned back on no one, so I hold those three still. That is what the jaw is. It opens less wide each Cutting, and when I draw a breath in by the rite I hear it in the hinge under my ear like a door that wants to shut.

**The fifteenth.** I do not know it. It is wet. I have dried it at the lamp and on the warm ground and it does not dry. I will not put it to my mouth again.

**The sixteenth to the thirtieth.** Mine, every one, and every one turned back. I set them down as one entry because I have less room than I had. That is plainly so. A purifier knows her room the way a jar knows its water. The fan opens to a certain place in front of the chest and stops there, and mine stops nearer than it did.

**The thirty-first.** I do not know it. It is tied in a strand that is grey and finer than flax and runs into the flax without a join. It is hair. My head has been shaved since my folding.

**The thirty-second to the forty-fourth.** Here are seven of mine and six I do not know, in among them, so close that I tied my own knots up against them without seeing. I can tell them only by the tails. All six have their tails turned in.

Two winters since, the bone at my temples began to ache the way the gums ache before a tooth. I can feel them now under the skin with two fingers, at either side, hard, the size of a thumbnail. I keep my hood up at the shrines. The keeper of the day-book has seen them and wrote nothing.

I will say this once, so that whoever reads this knows what I am and what I am not. The Horned sit along the drowned places with their hands in their laps and wait for the water. They say that the turning back is pride and the keeping is the true office, that a purifier's end is her work and she should walk to it gladly. I say they are tired, and have made a creed of their tiredness. A purifier who carries a wrong breath down into the water has purified nothing. She has moved it, as the man at the Hesk field moved his stone, and the water has already shown us what it does with what it is given. It gave the years back. I will not say their names. I do not know their names. I fear I would know them if I heard them.

**The forty-fifth to the sixty-fourth.** Mine, eleven. Not mine, nine. I stopped putting the strange ones to my mouth after the fifty-second, because when I did, the breath in it was mine. My own breath, warm, from before. And it said a name to me, the way a breath says a name on its way out, and I knew the name at once, though I was told at my folding that I would not keep it, and I had thought I had not kept it. I will not write it. It was burned.

**The sixty-fifth to the seventieth.** Mine. The last of them at Coldwater Steps, three nights gone, a stair where the dead had been laid wrong.

**The seventy-first.** I did not tie it. It sits past the place where I tied a red thread on the second night, so that I would know. The red thread is still there. The knot is outside it, at the free end, and its tail is turned in. I slept with the cord under my hand.

I have room for perhaps eight more. The elders would say nine. Let it be eight.

This is what I mean to do. I will take this page to the keeper of the day-book and ask her to count my cord against it, every knot, by the tails.
