# The Dream-Book of Marit Coe, Lantern-Tender of Low Tessing

*The book lay open on the bed in a turf house at Low Tessing, whose posts a neighbour had been lighting for a month; the bed was made tight, and the margins of the last leaves are bare.*

Begun the third night after the frost, at Low Tessing on the Barrow road, because the keeper at the ford-shrine told me to write them down as they come and before I wash my face. I am Marit Coe. I tend the nine posts from the ford to the Barrow gate and the lantern at the dead-shed. I can write because my father kept the reeve's tally. I have never kept a book of anything before.

**The first night.** A room under a roof that slants down to the floor on one side. One window, small, with glass in it. A jug on the sill with the lip broken off. A bed made so tight the cover is cold. I lay in the bed. Someone was on the stair below, coming up slowly, one step and a rest. I woke before they came.

*In the margin:* Our roof is turf laid flat on poles. I have never slept under a slant. I have never had glass.

**The second night.** The same room. I knew it at once. I looked at my hands on the cover and there was a ring on the third finger of the left, iron, worn thin on the inside. The step on the stair, and the rest. A lantern on the sill beside the jug, and the soul in it leaning toward the bed.

*In the margin:* I have no ring and never had. I looked at my hand by the shed lantern this morning until the carter asked was I hurt.

**The third night.** Someone below said a name up the stair, Ailveth, and I answered to it. I said, I am here. My mouth knew it the way it knows my own.

*In the margin:* My name is Marit Coe. My mother was Hesk Coe and her mother had no name anyone kept. I write mine here so that it is somewhere outside my head. I have never heard of anyone called Ailveth. Nobody in Low Tessing has a name with that much in it.

**The fifth night.** The fourth night I did not dream, or did and lost it. This night the room again, and the dream went on past where it stopped before. The steps reached the top. A hand on the latch, and the latch lifting, and I was glad. I was glad in the dream. I woke and lay in my own dark and was afraid of how glad.

*In the margin:* I slept through the lamp-hour. I have not done that in eleven years. Tobb from the next roof lit my posts, all nine, and the dead-shed, and did it badly. The room tastes of tallow and cold iron.

**The seventh night.** The door opened and the one who came in carried a length of grey felt over one arm. There is a glass on the wall of the room. I had not seen it before, or the dream had not finished putting it there. The one with the felt hung it over the glass and smoothed it flat with both hands. Then sat by the bed and took the hand with the ring.

*In the margin:* That is the custom of the glass-readers, the Veiled Crone's people, at a death: cover every glass in the room so the soul does not stop at its own face. We do not do it in Low Tessing. We have no glass to cover. My grandmother said the Crone's circles sit up all night saying names over somebody else's dead and call it kindness, and that a soul has better things to do than be talked at. Whose dream this is, I cannot tell the book.

I went to the ford-shrine this morning. The keeper breathed in at my mouth, as they do, and held it, and breathed it out over the water, and the water did nothing. She coughed after. She said there is no wrong breath in me. She said whatever I carry has no breath in it at all, and she went into the shrine and did not come out while I stood there.

**The eighth night.** The whole of it, from the stair to the felt, quickly, the way you say a thing you have said before. Then further. I lay with my hand held and I could hear the one holding it breathe, and I could not hear myself. The lantern on the sill leaned and leaned.

*In the margin:* I was at the trough at noon and the trough was the jug with the broken lip, and I stood with my hands in the water and did not know which. Tobb says I stood there till the water went still. I have given him the long hook and the wick-shears to keep. I am not to be trusted with the posts.

**The tenth night.** I died in the room. I set it down plainly because it was plain. The lantern on the sill went out. The one with the felt kept hold of the hand until it was cold and then laid it on the cover and went down the stair, one step and a rest, the same as coming up. Then nothing came to end it. The room stayed. I stayed in it. It was dark and warm, and below the floor, a long way below, people were singing the three notes my grandmother kept, and holding the rest after the third as long as a breath can be held, and longer.

*In the margin:* That was somebody's death and somebody finished it once. I do not understand why it has to be finished again, in me, in a turf house at Low Tessing. Grandmother said a dream that loses its sleeper does not know it is lost. It goes looking for a bed. I thought she meant it to frighten children off the roads at night.

I have lost the day. I came to myself at dusk sitting on the floor of the house facing the west wall with my hands in my lap. There is no window in the west wall.

**The twelfth night.** The room. The room. Under the floor they hold the rest after the third note and they are waiting for whoever is above them to give the fourth, and I am above them.

*In the margin:* Marit Coe. Nine posts. Ford to the Barrow gate. The dead-shed.

**The fourteenth night.** I dreamed of a low house with a turf roof laid flat on poles and no glass, and a woman in it who tends nine lanterns on a road and has a long hook for it, and sleeps on a pallet by the hearth and keeps a book. She is called Marit. She writes in the book each morning before she washes. In the dream I watched her write and I could read it over her shoulder, and what she wrote was the room under the slant, and the jug, and the felt over the glass. She was afraid. I was sorry for her. It was a small dream and close, and I liked it better than the other.

*In the margin:* Marit

**The fifteenth night.** The one with the felt came up the stair. Ailveth answered. The glass was covered. Under the floor they held the rest. The lantern on the sill went out, and the dream did not end, because the dream has somewhere to go on now. It has a pallet and a hearth and a hand to hold the pen. It is going on in the turf house. It goes to the trough and it writes in the book.

**The sixteenth night.** Made the bed tight. Sat by the west wall. Somebody sits up, she always said, when the lanterns go out one at a time. Tobb lit the nine.
