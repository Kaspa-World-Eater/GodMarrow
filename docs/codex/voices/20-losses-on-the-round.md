# Losses on the Low Round, Reckoned for the Keeper of the Rounds

*Found folded small in the oil-box at the twelfth post of the low road, under the wick-shears; the keeper it was written for had not come down that season to take it, and the post above it had a new lantern in it, unlit.*

To the keeper of the rounds, from the tender of the low round, Cullet Ford to the twelfth post.

When I took the round from old Hesk there were thirty-one lanterns on it, and he gave me thirty-one names with them, all but four. Every one of them burns a soul that came to the end and would not go down into the warm with the rest, and asked to be hung on the road instead. I wipe the glass of each one every morning. A soul burning fogs its glass from inside, a grey bloom like breath on a cold blade, and the bloom is the work. This season I have lost five. Twenty-six are lit at this writing. I set the losses down in order.

**The fourth post, at the culvert.** Held Brannoc the ditcher, eleven winters. Found at the morning wipe with no bloom on the glass. The glass was clean inside, which it never is. The wick stood white, as I trim it, unburnt, and the latch was shut and the pin through it. A lantern smells of the one in it. I put my face to the door and it smelled of tin.

**The ninth post, the one that leans.** Held a woman whose name Hesk did not know. Found the same way, glass clean, wick white. On the road side of the glass, at the height of a face, a ring of wet the size of a mouth set against it. I had the cloth up and it was dry before the cloth reached it. The bloom inside had gone toward that ring as smoke goes toward a crack, and there was no crack. Whatever drank her drank through the glass.

**The tenth and the eleventh**, both on the stretch past the Ashwake stones, both in one night. Neither name known. Both clean. Between them on the road, a carter dead on his own board, cold by morning, his eyes open. His mouth was dry inside to the throat. A dead man's mouth is wet, because the last breath goes out of it and leaves the wet behind. His last breath did not go out of it. Something had it on the way. I laid him in the ditch-yard and put salt in his mouth, late.

**The seventh post, at the ford.** Held Ysolt, who kept this round before Hesk. Her name was known and said each morning. I come to that below.

**What is seen at the edge of the light.** I walk the round by night when the losses come close together, which they now do. A lantern throws a ring, and the ring has an edge where the light goes thin and brown and stops. Things stand at that edge. I count them by the wicks, because when one stands at the edge the wick in the glass leans toward it, and a wick in a draught leans away. I have stood in the ring at the ninth with three wicks on the road leaning the same way, out, like grass in water. They do not come into the light. They wait for it to want them. Once at the tenth I saw the edge of the light move inward a hand's width all round, as the iris of an eye goes small, and stay there, and the glass bloomed thick, as though the one inside were breathing hard.

Hesk had the name for them from the tender before him. He would only say it into his sleeve, and so I write it once: the Yh'Vhoor. On the road they are the Mouths at the Wick. Hesk said they are no god and pray to none, and nothing prays to them. He said they take a soul at the dying, in the moment the breath is on the lip, before the warm can have it or any god can. He said they want the lantern souls most of anything on the Hide, because a soul in a lantern is dying all the while, slowly, every night it burns, and so it is always at the lip. It is the only reason I have heard that fits the glass.

**What has been tried.**

Salt. I laid a line of salt across the road at the edge of the light at the ninth, before she went. In the morning the salt lay dry along the whole line but one place a hand wide, where it was dark and wet through, the way salt goes when it is breathed on for a long time. The lantern held that night. The second night the wet place was two hands wide and she was gone. It held one night. I count that.

A lantern beside a lantern. Hesk forbade moving a soul off its post, because each one chose its post, and I have kept to that. But I hung a plain oil lantern with no one in it beside the eighth, on the old nail. Three nights the wicks at the eighth leaned toward the plain light. On the fourth morning the plain lantern's flame was out and its oil was full, and the eighth was bloomed and whole. I have hung plain lanterns at four posts since. It has worked, a little, at the eighth and the fifth. At the tenth and eleventh I had none to hang.

Saying the name. Every morning at the wipe I say the name of the one in the glass, the name Hesk gave, close to the glass so it blooms. Of the five lost, three had no name I knew, and the fourth, Brannoc, I had from Hesk and was never sure of, since the stone in the ditch-yard says Brannagh. I took from this that a soul with its name said over it is held, and I believed it until the seventh. Ysolt had her name said every morning for longer than I have tended. She went with her name on the glass. The only thing I can set against that is that I said it, and I think a name must be said by someone the soul remembers. She did not know me.

**Against the others.** In the leaf-fall a four of bowl-monks came down the round from the Ford and asked at each post for the name of the one inside, for their bowl. They say a name given into the bowl is kept safer than in any mouth. I gave them none. They walked the stretch from the seventh to the eleventh. I do not say they took anything. I say they asked, and four of the five are on that stretch. The Order's knights ride the low road in the dark with their standing dead and do not trim a wick. The Crone's unveiled readers came offering coin for the souls in glass, to carry them, and I put them off the round with the shears. Nobody keeps the road lit but the tender. The Hush is out past the edge of every ring and the lanterns are all that is said against it.

**This morning.** Hesk chose to stay. He burns at the twelfth, which is why I write here. I said his name at the wipe. The bloom on the glass was thick on my side and thin on the road side, thin as if it had been wiped from outside, and I had not yet wiped it. The wick leaned toward the road. I said his name again, and the one before him, and Ysolt's, and mine, in case. I have salt for one line and oil for one plain light, and I will sit up at the twelfth tonight inside the ring.
