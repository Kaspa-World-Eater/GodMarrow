# Roll of the Gang on the Cross Road, Hollin Cross to Tarrow Mere, for the Ash Season

*Nailed inside the lid of the tool-chest in the gang's hut at the twenty-ninth stone, where the next overseer would find it before the picks; the last leaf is in a second hand, and the chest was full of ash to the hasp.*

Roll of the gang keeping the road from the first stone at Hollin Cross to the forty-first at the Mere bank, for the ash season, kept by Wedda Rusk, overseer, who had it from Aud Rusk her mother, who had it from Tam Corle. Wage one copper a day and a heel of bread, the Cross paying the first twenty stones and the Mere the rest. The road was kept before the Cross was a town. It is to be kept.

**The gang, as taken on.**
Hobb Twine, eldest, ash-sweep.
Dunstan Ore, pick and lever.
Maud Tiller, cord and measure.
Ennet Lowe, ash-sweep.
Cael Harrow and Dunn Harrow, brothers, stone-setters.
Peg Hallow, water and bread.
Ketty Slade, hammer.
Garrit Lowe, Ennet's brother, ash-sweep, fourteen winters.
Wedda Rusk, overseer, all work.

**First day.** All present. Swept stones one to nine. Ash back on the first by noon, lying uphill of the broom-marks, against the wind. Swept again. Paid ten.

**Fourth day.** Milestone at the twenty-second has grown in the night by a hand above the cut line, the old cut line my mother made, and the one below hers. Ketty dressed it down with the hammer. It sheds grey dust, warm, and the dust does not blow. Set it back at the line. Dunstan found on the far face a notch that was not there at the season's end, square, clean, the chips lying fresh at the foot. There is no notch-iron in this gang. Paid ten.

**Fifth day.** Maud took the cord to the ring at Seven Teeth, as every tenth day and every fifth when the ash is bad. Nearest fang to the verge: forty-one paces. Last season's roll says forty-four at the close. The pit breathed while she measured. She says it breathes out longer than it breathes in. Paid ten.

**Ninth day.** The stretch between the thirtieth and thirty-first stones lay in the morning a long throw east of where we left it, down in the hollow by the dry beck, with the ditches still on either side of it and the night's ash on it and no mark of any digging. Where it lay yesterday is heath. We carried the thirtieth and thirty-first stones down to it and set them, because the road is where the road is. Mother's roll has the same twice, and Corle's once, and his says only *moved it*. Paid ten, and bread twice.

**Eleventh day.** A procession of the Bleeding Maiden came down from the Cross to the Mere, hooded, nineteen, and at each stone the one at the front opened a hand and laid a palm on the top. Ash sticks where blood has been. We swept and scrubbed from the first to the forty-first behind them, two days. They did not carry a broom between them. Paid ten, both days.

**Fifteenth day.** Maud at Seven Teeth: thirty-eight paces.

**Sixteenth day.** Ennet Lowe went in.

I set this down plainly because it will be asked. At the lamp-hour she was sweeping the verge by the twenty-sixth, which is the stone nearest the ring, and she put down her broom square across the road, as we are taught, and walked to the ring and between the two fangs on the road side and down into the ash. Garrit ran after and Dunstan held him. The ash took her to the knee, to the waist, and over, without a sound, and breathed out once after, longer than it breathes in. I struck her name. Her broom I gave to Garrit. Her wage for the day I paid, and her bread to Garrit for their mother. Paid ten, and one struck.

**Seventeenth to twenty-fourth days.** Swept and set. The twenty-second grown again; dressed it. A new notch on its far face, beside the last. The first stone under ash every noon. Paid nine a day.

**Twentieth day.** Maud at Seven Teeth: thirty-three paces. The fang nearest the road has a new face on it, pale, the old moss lying cut at its foot.

**Twenty-second day.** Four monks of the Peak came down the road with their bowls, asking each of us for a name. Dunstan gave his. They thanked him and went on to the Mere. Dunstan works as he did. At the roll at dusk I called him and he did not answer, and when I called again he looked round at the others to see who was meant. I have entered him since as *the tall one, pick and lever*, and he answers to that. The bowl-monks walk the road. They have never once swept it.

**Twenty-fifth day.** Word from Osk Fenner, overseer of the gang on the Mere to Dun Hollow, by a woman walking the road. Ennet Lowe came up out of the ring at Dun Hollow at noon of the ninth day after she went in, two valleys off, ash to the crown, and walked out between the fangs on the road side and asked for a broom. Osk kept her two days and sent her back along the road.

**Twenty-seventh day.** Ennet Lowe returned at noon. I have added her again, below the struck line, because a name is not struck twice in one season. She is whole. She knew her brother and the stones. She swept from the twenty-sixth toward the first, uphill, the way the ash goes, and when Hobb turned her she went the right way without being told twice. At pay I marked her left hand with the tar, as I mark every hand that is paid. At the next pay the old mark was on her right hand, where I do not mark. Garrit says her hands were always so. Paid ten.

**Twenty-ninth day.** Knights of the Pale Order rode down the road with their standing dead behind them in rows, toward the Mere, and the dead broke the verge from the eighteenth stone to the twenty-fourth. The knights asked how many we were. I told them ten. The one with the most names cut in his plate counted us and said eleven, and wrote it, and rode on. We were ten. Mended the verge three days. Paid ten.

**Thirtieth day.** Maud at Seven Teeth: twenty-seven paces. The ring is beside the twenty-sixth now, off the road side. Hobb sat down on the verge there at the lamp-hour, to rest, he said, and was dead by full dark with his face to the ring and his mouth full of ash, though he was upwind of it. We laid him in the verge-yard at the twenty-ninth, by Tam Corle, with his broom. A Shrine Keeper came by the next morning and set a little stone shrine at the edge of the ring for him, folded a paper figure into it, and walked on. By dusk the shrine stood inside the ring. Paid nine, and Hobb's wage to the Cross, who have no one to take it.

**Thirty-sixth day.** The Mere has not paid since the twentieth day. The Cross pays for twenty stones. We swept forty-one.

**Fortieth day.** Maud at Seven Teeth: twenty paces. The twenty-second is grown by two hands since I last dressed it, and its notches go round now to the near face. Ennet sweeps well. I asked Mother once why the road is kept. She said the season the Weir gang were not paid and did not go out, there was no Weir at the thaw. The road was there. It ran into a ring.

**Forty-first day.**

*In another hand.* Wedda Rusk struck by me this day, Maud Tiller, cord and measure, who will keep the roll. She went out at the lamp-hour to the twenty-sixth to dress the stone. Her broom is lying square across the road. The ring is at the verge. All others present and paid. Ennet Lowe says Wedda will be up at Dun Hollow by the ninth day and that we should keep her name by.
