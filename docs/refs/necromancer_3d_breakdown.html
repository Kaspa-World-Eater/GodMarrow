Necromancer Sprite Breakdown

How the Gravebound Necromancer went from a typed character map to a 3D voxel model that spins through 48 frames, every one rendered from the same model. The viewer on the right runs the real final renderer. Scrub through the turn and switch passes off to see what each one adds.

Everything is generated in the browser by plain JavaScript drawing into a 120 × 136 pixel canvas. No images, sprite sheets or libraries are loaded.

Part 1

How the brief changed, version by version

Each line is one publish to the same artifact link. The request that triggered it is in italics.

  1. v1
    "Draw me an animation in Dark Souls pixel art style of a necromancer."

    A full scene on a 192 × 108 canvas: ruined cathedral, dithered moon and sky, graves, skeletons that rise and crumble on an 8-second cycle, a rune circle, two fog layers, a bonfire, and a boss health bar you could click to strike. The necromancer was a 26-row character map typed by hand, one letter per pixel.

  2. v2
    "No background, just a simple idle animation."

    A file write failed before this publish, so v2 re-sent the old scene by mistake. v3 replaced it.

  3. v3

    The same character map alone on a 48 × 56 canvas at 12 frames per second: one-pixel bob, blinking eyes, swaying hem pixels, a six-pixel flame and rising soul specks.

  4. v4
    "Make it look better, like this" (a detailed Dark Souls knight GIF).

    The character map could not scale up, so the sprite became a list of filled polygons on a 96 × 108 canvas with automatic edge shading. The first screenshot read as a purple blob with rabbit ears; a second pass before publishing added a gold crown, dark contour lines between parts and cleaner gradients.

  5. v5
    "Same level of quality as the gif."

    A 120 × 136 canvas and a real lighting model: every part gets a distance field, a height, a surface normal and a lit palette index (section 2). Added plate armour, glowing cracks, green point lights from the fire and orb.

  6. v6
    "Refine it again."

    Hand-style fold lines in the cloth, a slimmer stance with visible legs and knee guards, hanging fur strands, fewer stripes on the greaves.

  7. v7
    "More detailed, like the souls gif."

    A detail pass: gold trim on the coat, rivets, scratches, a strap with vials, a swinging chain, a chained grimoire, a crimson cowl and scarf, a ribbon on the staff. You picked this one

  8. v8
    "More pixels, more realistic."

    Three times the resolution (360 × 408), each palette expanded to three times as many tones, surface textures and cast shadows. It looked softer and less like pixel art.

  9. v9
    "Don't draw it as pixels, make it any way you want."

    A vector illustration in SVG with gradients, turbulence filters for torn cloth, 800 generated fur strands and animated soul fire.

  10. v10
    "The older pixel version looked best. Animate it walking away, then turn around."

    Back to v7. A mirrored back view with an 8-frame walk cycle, a side view and the front view. The first plan shrank him with distance; your notes "no shrinking" and "walk in place" removed that.

  11. v11
    "Just a walk animation then a 180, in place."

    The loop was trimmed to walk, stop, turn through the side view, face you.

  12. v12
    "A smoother turn, 24 frames."

    The flat front and back drawings were narrowed by |cos θ| to fake in-between angles, with the staff moved along a circle (section 3).

  13. v13
    "No squish or cheating, draw every frame. Drop the walking. Just a spin. Make every angle consistent with the front and back."

    A new renderer: the necromancer is now a 3D model of 61 shapes turned into voxels, and each of the 48 frames is a real view of it (section 4). This is the version in the viewer above.

Part 2 · versions 4 to 12

The 2D sprite renderer

Before the 3D model, the sprite was drawn one part at a time into a 120 × 136 buffer. The same shading rules carried over into the 3D version, so they are worth seeing in order.

Buffers instead of a canvas API

Drawing goes into typed arrays, not canvas calls, so later passes can read what earlier ones wrote. col holds a packed RGB colour per pixel, fill says whether a pixel belongs to the figure (1), the outline (2) or nothing (0), and NX, NY, NZ store each pixel's surface normal for the lighting pass. At the end everything is copied into one ImageData and put on the canvas, which CSS scales up with image-rendering: pixelated.

const col  = new Int32Array(W * H), fill = new Uint8Array(W * H);
const NX = new Float32Array(W * H), NY = new Float32Array(W * H), NZ = new Float32Array(W * H);

Every part is a mask

A part such as a shoulder plate is a list of points. poly() marks every pixel whose centre falls inside the polygon (an even-odd crossing test), and ell() does the same for ellipses. Points are in sprite pixels, so [65, 44] means column 65, row 44.

const pr = poly([[65, 44], [75, 41], [81, 45], [81, 51], [68, 51]], 0, b);   // b = breathing offset
paint(pr, P.iron, { spec: true, round: 4, lift: 0.1 });

Shading one part: mask to lit pixels

This is the step that made the sprite look forged instead of flat. The strip below runs the same maths on that shoulder plate, live.

Distance. A two-pass chamfer transform gives each pixel its distance to the nearest edge (1 for straight steps, 1.41 for diagonals). Height. The distance is bent into a rounded profile, h = R·√(1 − (1 − d/R)²), so the part bulges like a pillow with a rim of radius R. Bump functions add folds, fur or plate lines on top. Normal. Central differences of the height give a slope, which becomes a unit normal (−∂h/∂x, −∂h/∂y, 1). Light. The normal is dotted with one fixed light from the top left, L = normalize(−0.55, −0.70, 0.65), and the result picks a colour from the part's palette. Metal reserves its brightest tone for pixels facing the light almost directly, which gives the sharp glints.

const diff = Math.max(0, nx * L[0] + ny * L[1] + nz * L[2]);
let v = amb + (1 - amb) * diff - vgrad * y / h + lift;
idx = spec ? (diff > specT ? n - 1 : Math.min(n - 2, Math.floor(v * (n - 1))))
           : Math.min(n - 1, Math.floor(v * n));

Palettes

Each material is a short ramp from shadow to highlight. Every pixel colour in the sprite, except the green glow, comes from one of these ten lists.

Contours and the outline

Where a part is painted over one already in the buffer, its edge pixels on the right and bottom drop to the darkest tone and its top and left edges drop to the second darkest. That draws the thin dark lines between pieces of armour and cloth. After all parts are drawn, every empty pixel that touches the figure becomes a near-black outline pixel. Switch Outline off in the viewer to see the silhouette without it.

Hand-placed detail on top of the maths

FunctionWhat it does
folds()Adds a sine wave across the cloth's height map, widening toward the hem, so the light catches ridges.
strokes()Draws long fold lines by hand: a lit ridge pixel beside a dark crease pixel, tapering at both ends.
fur()Height noise in vertical clumps for the mantle. The first version produced a knitted crosshatch and was redone.
lames()Grooves every few rows so plate armour reads as overlapping plates.
trim()A gilded edge where a mask ends on one side, used on the coat opening and shoulder plates.
hemStitch()Gold stitching that follows a ragged hem three pixels up from its edge.
grit()Moves a few per cent of pixels one tone darker or lighter, as scratches and wear.
rivet()One highlight pixel over one shadow pixel.
chain()A sagging curve of alternating light and dark link pixels.

Soul light, glow and the floor

Emissive pixels (eyes, cracks, the orb, the fire) are collected in a separate list and stamped on last, so nothing shades them. Point lights at the staff fire and the orb then tint nearby pixels green. Each pixel's stored normal is compared with the direction to the light, falloff is squared, and the result picks one of three mix strengths, with a 4 × 4 Bayer pattern dithering the faintest band. The floor shadow is an ellipse filled in a checkerboard, like the reference GIF.

Animation clock

The sprite advances on a fixed 10 frames per second step, independent of screen refresh, which keeps the motion chunky like hand-made sprites. Breathing moves the upper body down one pixel for 12 frames and back up for 12. Hems are rebuilt every frame from alternating points whose depth comes from wave(f, i) = round(sin(0.55f + 1.9i)), so tatters flick without drifting.

Part 3 · versions 10 to 12

Walking and the first turn, and why it was replaced

A back view by mirroring

Arms, shoulder plates and the staff were refactored into functions that take a mirror flag. With it set, every x becomes 115 − x, which flips a part around the body's centre line (57.5). That moved the staff to his left hand as seen from behind, as it should be. The back of the hood, the cape, the back of the mantle and the scarf end were drawn new.

The 24-frame projected turn

Version 12 faked the in-between angles. For a turn angle θ it drew the back sprite narrowed by KX = cos θ up to 55°, the side sprite from 55° to 125°, and the front sprite narrowed by −cos θ after that. The staff was moved separately along a circle, x = 57.5 − 28 cos θ − 12.5 sin θ, so it passed in front of the body instead of jumping. Angles were eased with smoothstep across the 24 frames.

You rejected this as squishing, and you were right. Narrowing a flat drawing is not a rotation: the face, crown and coat stayed facing the camera while getting thinner, and the side band was a held pose. Consistent angles needed an actual 3D model.

Part 4 · version 13

The 3D spin: one model, 48 real views

The final renderer keeps the pixel look (same canvas, palettes, light, contours, outline and glow) but takes its shapes from a 3D model instead of 2D masks.

Object space

x runs across him, y runs down using the same numbers as the 2D sprite, and z points toward the viewer when he faces front. His body axis is the line x = 57.5, z = 0. Many details are placed by their angle around that axis, a = atan2(x − 57.5, z): 0 is straight ahead, +π/2 is his left side, ±π is his back.

The model is 61 signed-distance shapes

Each shape is a function that returns how far a point is from its surface, negative inside. Four kinds cover everything:

ShapeUsed for
sdEll ellipsoidHood, chest plate, three shoulder lames, skulls, boots, knee guards, fist, mantle, cowl, the soul orb
sdCone capsule with changing radiusLegs, greaves, arms, gauntlets, claws, staff, gold rings, cage prongs, crown spikes, scarf ends
sdBox rounded boxGrimoire, skull jaw, staff ribbon
ring elliptical tube around the axisTabard, coat, cape, belt, crown band, with ragged hems from tat()

The coat is a shell 2.2 voxels thick with a wedge cut out of the front (|a| < 0.36), which reveals the grey tabard inside. The cape is a 1.6-voxel shell kept only where |a| > 1.75, so it covers his back and not his front. The face opening is a carve shape that empties the front of the hood. Shapes later in the list override earlier ones, so the fist wraps the staff and the cape covers the coat.

function sdCoat(p) {
  const a = ang(p[0], p[2]), rx = coatRx(p[1]), rz = coatRz(p[1]);
  const outer = ring(p, rx, rz), inner = ring(p, rx - 2.2, rz - 2.2);
  const cut = p[1] > 48 ? (OPEN - Math.abs(a)) * Math.hypot(p[0] - C, p[2]) : -9;
  return Math.max(outer, -inner, 46 - p[1], p[1] - coatHem(a), cut);
}

Voxelise once

At load, a grid of 76 × 130 × 44 cells is filled. Each shape only tests the cells inside its own bounding box and claims a cell when its distance at the cell centre is zero or less. This takes about a quarter of a second.

Keep only the surface, with normals and materials

A cell is kept if any cell one or two steps away along an axis is empty. The two-voxel shell stops gaps opening when the model is rotated. Each kept voxel stores:

  • a normal, from the gradient of its own shape's distance function, sampled half a voxel either side on each axis, plus fold or fur bumps;
  • a palette and tone offset, decided by its position: gold trim where the coat opening ends, crimson where the sash and scarf fall, a gold skull sigil on the back of the cape, rivets, sockets and teeth on skulls, glowing cracks on armour.

Thin shells caused the one real bug in this version. Voxels on the inner half of the cape had normals pointing inward, so they were skipped when facing away and left a net of holes across his back. The fix gives the coat and cape shells normals from their outer surface only.

Draw one frame

For frame f, the angle is φ = 2π · (f mod 48) / 48, 7.5° per frame. Then:

  1. Every surface voxel is rotated about the body axis and projected straight onto the canvas: x′ = 60 + (x − 57.5)·cos φ + z·sin φ, depth z′ = −(x − 57.5)·sin φ + z·cos φ.
  2. A depth buffer keeps the nearest voxel per pixel. Voxels facing the camera go first; inward-facing ones only fill pixels nothing else covered, which is how the lining of the coat shows.
  3. Each pixel's normal is rotated by the same angle and shaded with the palette rules from part 2, so the light stays fixed while he turns.
  4. Contours appear where a neighbouring pixel belongs to a different shape at least 2.2 voxels further away, then the outline pass runs.
  5. Green point lights use each pixel's real depth, so the fire lights the side of the hood facing it at every angle. The fire and soul specks are drawn last at the staff tip's projected position.

Switch the viewer to Normals or Depth to see the two buffers these steps work from.

Timing

48 frames per revolution at 12 frames per second, 4 seconds per spin. Each frame takes a few milliseconds, so the spin renders live instead of from a stored sheet.

Part 5

Problems found in screenshots, and the fixes

Purple blob with rabbit ears (v4)

Too wide a silhouette and two upright horns. Narrowed the robe and replaced the horns with a crown.

Noisy gradients (v4)

Full Bayer dithering over every surface looked like static. Dithering was limited to a narrow band between tones.

Knitted fur (v6)

A diagonal pattern in the fur function produced a crosshatch. Replaced with vertical clumps.

Fuzzy red worms (v9)

The torn-edge filter displaced narrow strips too far. Narrow cloth got a weaker filter.

Hidden legs on the walk (v10)

The back cape covered the stepping feet. The cape was shortened by nine pixels from behind.

Staff jumping across the body (v12)

Switching drawings moved the staff 18 pixels in one frame. It was moved along a circular path instead.

Wavy arcs on the cape (v13)

Folds that depended on height drew diagonal moiré. Folds now run straight down.

Net of holes on the cape (v13)

Inward normals on the inner half of the shell. Shells now take normals from their outer surface.
